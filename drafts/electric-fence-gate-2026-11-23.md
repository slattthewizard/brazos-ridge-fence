# Electric Fence Gate: How to Carry Power Across a Gateway

A gate is where an electric fence breaks. You have to open the line to drive a truck or a horse through, and then somehow keep the current moving on both sides. The answer is to treat those as two separate jobs. The gate itself is a removable, insulated barrier that is dead when it is unhooked. The power gets across the gateway through a buried, double-insulated cable that never touches the gate at all.

That one split solves most of the trouble people have. A fence that depends on its gate to pass current goes dead every time the gate is open. ## Two jobs at one gateway

Job one is closing the gateway against livestock. That is the gate: a few strands of wire, a tape, or a spring gate, hooked on one end with an insulated handle.

Job two is moving current from one post to the other. That crossing does not run through the gate. It runs under it or over it.

Gallagher's installation guide makes the same point in a caution box. It says not to rely on electric gates to get power across gateways, because when the gates are open the fence loses power, and the conductor in an electric gate is not designed to carry high currents, so voltage drops even when the gate is closed. The same guide says electric gates should be wired so they are dead when unhooked ([Gallagher, Electric Fencing 101](https://am.gallagher.com/-/media/Bynder/Animal-Management/Document/All-GGL-Manuals/Electric-Fencing-101-Manual-NA-original.pdf)). Both rules fall out of the split above.

## The gate itself: handle, spring gate or tape

The gate is the cheap part. There are three common builds.

### Insulated gate handles with wire or polywire

A gate handle is a plastic grip on a hook or loop. It insulates your hand from the wire, and it gives you something to pull against when you stretch the line closed. You run a strand from the hooked post to a fixed anchor on the far post, and the handle sits at the hook end.

A few rules keep this working:

- Hook the handle over an insulator or a ring on an insulator, not around bare metal. If the far end of the gate is wired to a live strand, unhooking the handle must leave the loose gate wire dead.
- Put the handle at the end you reach for, on the side of the gateway where you stand when you open it.
- Connect the gate wire to the hot line with a proper clamp or connector, not a twisted loop. Loose connections lose voltage.

### Spring gates and bungy gates

A spring gate is a stretchy coil of conductor with a handle at the end. It gives when livestock lean on it and snaps back. It suits a gateway you open daily and a wide gap where plain wire sags and drags. Gallagher's guide also suggests spring or bungy gates where the gate has to be stretched across a road to divert animals into a paddock.

### Tape gates

Tape is the most visible gate. Gallagher calls tape gates the most visible gate solution, and visibility matters with horses, which have to see the line to respect it. Our piece on [electric fence for horses](/blog/electric-fence-for-horses/) covers tape and wire choices, and the same logic applies at the gate. If your horses are on small acreage, the [horse fence](/horse-fence-waco/) page lays out what we build around those gateways.

## Carrying power under the gateway

This is the part worth doing carefully. It is hidden, and it is the hardest part of the fence to troubleshoot once it fails.

### Use cable made for the job

Virginia Tech's extension publication on electric fencing says to use only insulated lead-out cable manufactured specifically for going through buildings or under gates, and it warns that residential electric wire is not adequate to carry high voltage and will burn up, causing electrical shorts ([Virginia Cooperative Extension, How Does an Electric Fence Work?](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/content/pubs_ext_vt_edu/en/SPES/spes-688/spes-688.html)).

The University of Kentucky says the same thing with numbers on it. Residential-grade or copper wire is designed to carry 120 volts, not 10,000 volts. They recommend a quality double-insulated cable designed for electric fencing, the same diameter as the offset wire or larger ([University of Kentucky, ID-269](https://publications.mgcafe.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/ID269.pdf)).

Two details matter here:

- **Do not join copper to galvanized.** Kentucky notes that dissimilar metals corrode where they touch and lose conductivity. Gallagher's guide says never to use copper cable where it joins galvanized wire.
- **Keep the hop short.** Gallagher says undergate cable should not be used for long lead-outs or long distances underground, because its thin 16 gauge conductor adds resistance and cuts the voltage available to the fence. Use it for the gateway, then switch to heavier wire.

### Put it in conduit

Double-insulated cable survives in the ground better than bare wire, but not forever. Virginia Tech says buried insulated cable can develop cracks or abrasion in its coating, and recommends sleeving buried wire in conduit where it goes under gates. Kentucky is more direct: always put underground wires in protective tubing, because unprotected cable breaks and the shorts are hard to find and repair.

The gateway is the worst spot in the fence for this. It is where tires, hooves and gate drag all land.

So the build is:

1. Dig a trench across the gateway, post to post.
2. Lay PVC pipe or conduit in it. Kentucky suggests larger conduit, 3/4 inch or 1 inch, because it makes the cable easier to push through.
3. Pull the double-insulated cable through the pipe.
4. Seal the ends. Kentucky suggests an unglued end cap with a hole just big enough for the wire. Gallagher says to seal the ends or turn them down so moisture stays out.
5. Leave enough cable at each end to reach the fence wire without strain.
6. Backfill with soil free of rocks and debris, as Gallagher advises, and make the end connections with join clamps or through a cut-out switch.

### How deep

The two sources differ a little. Kentucky says wires under gates should be buried about 6 to 12 inches. Gallagher says at least 12 inches. Plan on 12 or more, because a gateway gets graded and driven on harder than the rest of the ground.

On blackland clay around Waco, trenching is easy when the soil is damp and slow going when it has baked in August. West of the Brazos and into Bosque County, thin soil over limestone and caliche can stop a trench short. If you hit rock, you can still lay the pipe as deep as the rock allows and cover it, which beats bare cable on the surface. Our guide to [setting posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/) covers what that rock does to the rest of the gateway.

### Overhead crossings

The alternative is to go over, with cable strung between a pole on each side. It needs tall posts and clearance above your tallest equipment, so where trucks and tractors run, underground is the usual answer.

## Cut-out switches at the gateway

A cut-out switch is a small switch on the fence line that lets you turn off a section of fence without shutting down the charger. Gallagher recommends putting them at gateways or junctions, where a single line or several lines can be turned off. Their guide says to connect the undergate cable to one switch terminal and the tail of the second line wire to the other.

A switch earns its place at a gateway for three reasons:

- You can kill the fence on one side while you work on it, with the rest still hot.
- When you are chasing a fault, you can open the switch and see whether the problem is in the segment you just cut off. Our [electric fence not working](/blog/electric-fence-not-working/) guide covers narrowing down a short.
- You can isolate a paddock you are not using.

The trap is a switch left open. It kills the fence beyond it while the fence up to that point reads fine, and Gallagher's troubleshooting list names it as a cause of no voltage beyond a point. If part of your fence reads zero, walk back to the last switch.

## Keeping the gate dead

Back to the first rule: an electric gate should be dead when it is unhooked.

If the gate wire is connected to the hot line at its fixed end and hooked at the other, unhooking the handle leaves a strand with live voltage lying in the dirt. The fix is to wire the gate so the live connection is made only by the hooked end, or to isolate it with a switch. Either way, the loose end you pick up has to be dead.

Keep gate wire clear of steel gate panels and posts, or you can electrify the whole structure, and keep the loose end off the ground, where a hot wire in grass or dirt leaks current and drains the fence.

## What people get wrong at gates

**Using the gate to carry current.** The fence works when the gate is shut and dies when it opens. The fix is the buried cable.

**Direct burial without conduit.** It works for a while, then a crack or abrasion appears in the coating and the fence leaks to ground through the gateway. A leak to the soil can look like a grounding problem at first, so check the crossing before you add rods. Our [electric fence grounding](/blog/electric-fence-grounding/) guide covers testing the ground side.

**Open pipe ends and loose clamps.** Water collects in an open pipe and sits against the cable, and a twisted wire at the connection loses voltage. Kentucky says to make all connections with clamps or crimps, not wraps.

## Building a gateway that lasts

Gateways take more abuse than any other part of a fence. If the gate also swings, see how to [hang a farm gate](/blog/how-to-hang-a-farm-gate/) first, because a gate that sags drags its wire through the dirt and shorts the line. If the electric line is portable, the [temporary electric fence](/blog/temporary-electric-fence-grazing/) guide covers using an insulated gate handle where you drive through, and the underground crossing is usually skipped on portable fence.

For a permanent fence, build the gateway in this order:

1. Set the gate posts and brace them to carry the pull of the fence.
2. Trench across the opening and lay the pipe.
3. Pull the cable and connect it to both fence lines with clamps, either directly or through a cut-out switch.
4. Hang the gate wire, tape or spring gate, with the handle on the hooked end only.
5. Test with a fence tester on both sides of the gateway, with the gate open and closed. Both sides should read about the same.

If the far side reads much lower than the near side, the crossing is leaking or a connection is loose. Check the clamps first.

If you are building a full ranch entrance, plan the electric crossing from the start, since trenching across a finished road is harder than trenching before it is surfaced. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you run electric fence across a gate?

Run insulated cable under the gate, not through it. Use double-insulated cable made for electric fencing, put it inside PVC pipe or conduit, bury it across the gateway, and clamp each end to the fence wires or to a cut-out switch. The gate itself is a separate, removable section that is dead when unhooked.

### Can I use regular wire to carry power under a gate?

No. Virginia Tech warns that residential wire will burn up and cause shorts at fence voltage. Kentucky notes household wire is built for 120 volts, not 10,000. Use cable made for electric fencing, and keep it in conduit.

### How deep should underground electric fence cable be buried?

Kentucky Cooperative Extension says about 6 to 12 inches under a gate. Gallagher's installation guide says at least 12 inches. Plan on 12 or more where vehicles cross, keep the cable inside pipe, and seal the pipe ends against water.

### What is an electric fence gate handle?

It is an insulated grip on a hook that you pull to stretch a gate wire, tape or spring across an opening. The plastic keeps the live fence off your hand. Hook it so that unhooking leaves the gate dead.

### Do I need a cut-out switch at a gate?

You do not need one to make the fence work, but it helps. A switch at the gateway lets you shut off one section for repairs or to find a fault while the rest of the fence stays live. Just remember that a switch left open kills everything beyond it.
