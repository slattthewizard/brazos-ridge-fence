# Fence for Pigs: What Holds Domestic Pigs on Central Texas Pasture

The fence that holds pigs best is a tight woven wire or hog panel perimeter with one electrified wire low on the inside, set about where a pig's snout hits. A pig doesn't jump much and doesn't climb. It pushes, and it roots. So the question isn't how high the fence is. It's what happens at the bottom 12 inches.

This post is about domestic pigs: a few sows and a boar, a batch of weaners for the freezer, a 4-H project, or a pet pig on a few acres. Feral hogs are a different problem with different numbers, and we cover those in [how to build a hog-proof fence](/blog/hog-proof-fence/). ## Why pigs break fences differently than cattle

Cattle lean on a fence and horses test it with their chests. Pigs go under it. The University of Georgia's circular on farm fences says hogs try to root their way under a fence, so the bottom of the fence has to be secure ([UGA Extension, Fences for the Farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/)). The University of Tennessee's fence guide says the same thing from the other direction: swine need strong fences built close to the ground to stop them from escaping by rooting underneath, and barbed wire along the ground helps ([UT Extension PB1541, Planning and Building Fences on the Farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf)). That same guide says swine fences shouldn't need to be higher than 54 inches.

Put those together and you get the design rule. Make the bottom tight, make it hard to lift, and give the pig a reason to stay a few inches back from it. Height is the easy part.

Three habits explain most escapes:

- **Rooting.** A pig digs with its snout along the line of least resistance, and that line is usually a fence. Soft ground along a creek or a wet corner goes first.
- **Lifting.** A pig pushes its snout under a loose bottom wire and levers up. Net wire with slack at the ground gives way to this.
- **Pushing through a gap.** Piglets fit through openings a grown pig wouldn't try. Mesh that holds a sow will let weaners walk out.

## The hot wire is what makes the fence work

A pig that has been shocked on the nose stays off the fence. A pig that has never touched the wire will test it, and a wire the pig can't feel is decoration. That is why most pig fences, from backyard pens to pasture rotations, put an electric wire where the snout meets the fence.

### How low, and how many wires

For pigs on a woven or panel perimeter, one hot wire about 6 to 8 inches off the ground, set 6 to 12 inches inside the fence, handles most adult pigs. If you're running only electric (a temporary paddock, say), three strands is the usual practice: roughly 6, 12 and 24 inches for grown pigs, with the lowest wires closer for small pigs. Those are common working numbers, not a code. Pigs vary by breed and size, so set the lowest wire where the snout will be, and drop it for weaners.

Smooth wire, polywire or polytape all work for a pig. Barbed wire isn't the better choice here. The Tennessee guide mentions barbed wire along the ground as a rooting deterrent, but a hot wire does the same job, and a pig that has been shocked doesn't hang on a barb or tear itself up on it.

### Voltage and charger

The Tennessee guide puts the minimum shock needed to control short-haired breeds of cattle, pigs and horses at 700 volts, and around 2,000 volts for long-haired cattle, sheep and goats (UT PB1541, linked above). That's a floor at the farthest point of the fence, not at the charger. In Central Texas, a hot wire down at 6 inches gets shorted by weeds, rain-wet grass and, in July, cracked, dry ground that won't carry a return.

So size the charger above the minimum, keep the bottom wire clear of vegetation, and don't skimp on grounding. Our posts on [electric fence charger sizing](/blog/electric-fence-charger-sizing/) and [electric fence grounding in dry Texas ground](/blog/electric-fence-grounding/) cover the joules and the ground rods. Dry blackland clay in August is a poor ground, and a pig fence is one of the first places you'll notice it.

## Train the pigs to the wire before you trust it

This is the step people skip, and it's why a good fence "fails" on day one. Pigs that have never met a hot wire can run through it the first time they're startled, and then they're loose and have learned that the wire doesn't stop them.

The standard practice is to train them in a small, solid pen before they get pasture:

1. Build a small pen with the hot wire on the inside of a solid fence (hog panels work well for this).
2. Turn the pigs in with the charger on and let them find the wire with their noses.
3. Leave them in for a couple of days, until they stay back from the wire, then move them to the pasture.

The solid pen matters. A pig that gets a shock and has nowhere solid behind it can bolt through a polywire strand. With a panel behind the hot wire, it backs up instead.

Check the fence with a meter, not a guess, before the pigs go out.

## Fence types that hold pigs

| Fence | Where it fits | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Hog panels (16 ft welded rod) | Pens, farrowing areas, training pens, short runs | Height is 34 inches, so a big sow can climb over a loose one; anchor the bottom |
| Woven or net wire plus a hot wire | Pasture perimeter | Slack at the ground; tight bottom spacing matters |
| Electric only (3 strands) | Temporary rotational paddocks inside a perimeter | Untrained pigs, shorted wires, weeds |
| Pipe or heavy rail | Boar pens, working areas | Cost per foot; gaps at the bottom |
| Barbed wire only | Not a good fit for pigs | Pigs go under, and piglets go through |

### Hog panels for pens

A hog panel is a 16-foot sheet of welded galvanized rod, 34 inches tall, with the lowest openings tight so a pig can't root under or squeeze through. For a farrowing pen, a training pen, or a small boar lot, it's hard to beat on cost and speed. We wrote up the specs and posting in [hog panels vs cattle panels](/blog/hog-panels-vs-cattle-panels/), so we won't repeat them. Two points matter here. Anchor the bottom edge, with posts every 8 feet or less and the panel wired tight to them, because a pig will find the one loose corner. And don't use plain cattle panels for pigs, because the bottom openings are too wide for piglets.

### Net wire for a pasture perimeter

For a pig pasture of a few acres, the long-lived answer is woven wire with a hot wire inside. The UGA circular notes woven wire is made with horizontal wires spaced closer at the bottom (about 1.5 inches for small animals) and wider at the top, and most heights run from 26 to 48 inches. Pick a mesh with tight spacing at the base, stretch it so the bottom doesn't lift, and set the hot wire 6 to 12 inches inside. Our [net wire fence service](/net-wire-fence-waco/) builds this kind of fence, and the [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page has current ranges: net wire runs $3.50 to $8 per foot installed, so you can price a perimeter from your own footage.

Posts need to be closer than for cattle. For a tight pig perimeter, 8 to 10 feet between line posts is reasonable, and corners and ends should be braced like any woven wire fence.

### Pipe and heavy rail

Pipe fence can hold a boar, and it makes sense at a working pen where the animals get handled. It's the most expensive option: welded pipe runs $12 to $28 per foot on our table. For pasture pigs, you'd rarely spend that on perimeter. Use it where the animal pushes hardest.

## Soil, mud and the bottom of the fence

Central Texas ground does two things that make pig fence harder than it looks.

**Blackland clay** holds water, and pigs turn wet clay into a wallow in a week. A wallow along a fence line undercuts the bottom wire and the posts. Plan the pen and water so the low, muddy spots aren't against the fence, and keep the waterer or feeder at least a few feet from the line.

**Thin soil over limestone**, west of the Brazos and in Bosque County, is the opposite problem. You can't always bury the bottom of the fence, so the hot wire and a tight bottom carry more of the load, and posts may need to be drilled.

Where the ground is soft, a short skirt of net wire laid flat on the pig side and pegged down gives a rooting pig something to hit other than the post line.

## Keeping feral boars away from your sows

If you're near creek bottoms in McLennan, Bosque or Falls County, feral hogs may come to your fence. Feral boars can be drawn to a pen of domestic sows, and escaped domestic pigs can end up running with feral hogs. A pig fence that holds your pigs in doesn't necessarily keep a feral boar out. For that, you need a tight bottom, a hot wire on the outside, and a gate that closes. We cover the damage and the fixes in [feral hog fence damage](/blog/feral-hog-fence-damage/). It's a different build from your pig fence, and often the two sit side by side.

## Texas stock laws and pigs on the road

Much of Texas is open range by default, but counties can adopt stock laws, and the Texas Agriculture Code allows those laws to cover hogs along with cattle, horses, sheep and goats. The Texas A&M AgriLife fence law guide also notes that the code says a person responsible for a hog may not knowingly permit it to roam at large, unattended, on a highway right-of-way ([AgriLife Extension EAG-029, A Landowner's Guide to Fence Law in Texas](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Texas-Fencing-Law-Information.pdf)). Whether a stock law applies to pigs where you live depends on the county. Our post on [Texas stock laws by county](/blog/texas-stock-law-by-county/) is the place to start, and your county clerk can confirm.

A pig loose on a farm-to-market road is your problem, so put a good gate and a latch pigs can't lift on the list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How high does a fence need to be for pigs?

Less than you'd think. Domestic pigs don't jump much, and a 34 inch hog panel holds most of them in a pen, and Tennessee Extension says swine fences need be no higher than 54 inches. The bottom matters more than the top, because a pig that can root under or lift the wire will leave long before it climbs.

### Is electric fence enough to hold pigs?

For trained pigs in a rotational paddock, yes. Most pig keepers still put the electric wire inside a solid perimeter, because an untrained pig can run through a hot wire the first time it's startled. Train the pigs in a small panel pen first, test the voltage at the far end of the fence, and keep weeds off the bottom wire.

### How low should the electric wire be for pigs?

Put the lowest wire at snout height, about 6 to 8 inches off the ground for adult pigs, and lower for weaners. Set it a few inches inside the fence so the pig meets it before it reaches the net wire or panel. These are common working heights rather than a fixed rule, so adjust to the size of your pigs.

### Can I use cattle panels for pigs?

Not by themselves. The bottom openings on a cattle panel are wide enough for piglets to walk through. A hog panel is built with tight spacing at the bottom for this reason. A combo panel works too. Our [hog panels vs cattle panels](/blog/hog-panels-vs-cattle-panels/) post lays out the difference.

### Will my pig fence keep feral hogs out?

Not reliably. A fence built to hold domestic pigs in isn't built to resist a feral boar pushing from the outside. If hogs are in your area, build the outside to the standard in our [hog proof fence](/blog/hog-proof-fence/) guide, with a tight bottom and a hot wire facing out, and keep domestic sows away from the fence line.
