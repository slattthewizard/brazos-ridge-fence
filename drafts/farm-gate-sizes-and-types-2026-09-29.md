# Farm Gate Sizes and Types: What Width and Build Your Ranch Gates Need

Farm gates are sold in 2-foot steps, usually from 4 feet to 16 feet for a single leaf, with 18 and 20 foot openings covered by longer gates or a pair of leaves. A 4 or 5 foot gate is for people and a led horse. A 12 foot gate passes a pickup and most small tractors. For hay equipment, a gooseneck, or anything pulled behind a tractor at an angle, plan on 16 feet, and go to 20 on a main road entrance.

Width is only half the choice. The other half is what the gate is made of: a light tube gate, a heavier bull or corral gate, a wire-filled gate, or a welded pipe gate built on site. Each one belongs somewhere on a ranch, and each one is the wrong call somewhere else.

## Standard farm gate sizes and what each one fits

Most steel farm gates come in even lengths: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 and 16 feet, with some makers going to 18 or 20. The standard tube gate is about 50 inches tall, close to the height of a typical barbed wire or net wire cattle fence.

| Gate width | What it passes | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| 4 ft | People, a horse on a lead | Walk-through by a barn, house yard, pen alleys |
| 6 ft | ATV, UTV, a person with a wheelbarrow | Side gates, garden, small pens |
| 8 ft | A side-by-side with room to spare, a small mower | Paddocks, trap pens, lanes |
| 10 ft | Pickup with care, compact tractor | Interior cross fences with light traffic |
| 12 ft | Pickup, most utility tractors, a bumper-pull trailer driven straight | Pasture gates on a small place |
| 14 ft | Stock trailers, tractors with a front loader, most hay equipment | Working pasture gates |
| 16 ft | Goosenecks, round balers on wide tires, shredders turned at an angle | Main pasture gates, hay meadows |
| 18 to 20 ft (often as a double) | Hay trucks, 18-wheelers, wide equipment pulled off a county road | Road entrances, headquarters |

### Measure the opening, not the gate

A "16 foot" gate is not 16 feet long. Manufacturers build the gate shorter than its listed size so the hinges and latch have room. [Tarter's tube gate spec page](https://tarterusa.com/product/american-gate/) states that its 1-3/4 inch tube gates are 3 inches shorter than the listed length for exactly that reason. Other makers use their own allowance, so check the spec for the gate you buy.

So set your posts with the clear distance between them equal to the gate's nominal size. If you set 8 inch pipe posts 16 feet apart on center, the clear opening is only about 15 feet 3 inches, and a 16 foot gate will not go in. Measure inside face to inside face.

### Widths the extension services recommend

Extension guidance lines up with what ranchers learn the hard way. [Penn State Extension's fence planning guide for horses](https://extension.psu.edu/fence-planning-for-horses) says people-only gates should be no less than 4 feet wide with 5 preferred, and vehicle gates should be a minimum of 12 feet and up to 16. The [University of Georgia's Fences for Horses bulletin](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) goes a step further for pasture gates, saying they should be wide enough for tractors, mowers and fertilizer trucks, a minimum of 14 to 16 feet.

Penn State also recommends setting a gate 40 to 60 feet back from a road so a vehicle can pull fully off the pavement before the gate is opened. On a county road with a truck and trailer, that setback matters more than the gate itself.

## How wide should a farm gate be for your equipment?

Start with the widest thing that will ever go through, then add room for the way it comes at the gate.

Round balers are a good test. The [Vermeer 605N spec sheet](https://www.vermeer.com/na/balers/605n) lists the machine at 114 inches wide on standard tires and 129 inches on the optional flotation tires. That is 9 feet 6 inches and about 10 feet 9 inches. A 12 foot opening leaves about 7 inches a side on flotation tires if you come through dead straight, and nobody pulls a baler through a pasture gate dead straight. A 14 or 16 foot gate is the honest answer for a hay meadow.

Shredders are the other one. Measure your cutter's transport width with the wings folded, and remember a pull-type cutter tracks inside the tractor on a turn. A gooseneck backing through at an angle also eats far more width than its fenders suggest. If cattle leave a pasture by trailer, that pasture wants a 16.

Two rules of thumb hold up:

- On a gate you drive through straight, give yourself at least 2 feet of clearance on each side of the widest implement.
- On a gate you turn into, add another 2 to 4 feet, or move the gate so you can come at it straight.

## Types of farm gates and where each one belongs

### Utility tube gates

The farm-store tube gate: round or oval tubing, usually 5 or 6 horizontal bars, 50 inches tall, sold with hinges and a chain. Economy tube gates are commonly built from thin-wall tubing around 20 gauge. They are light, cheap, and easy for one person to hang.

They work well on interior cross fences, trap pens, and gates opened a few times a month. They do not hold up to a bull that leans on them, a horse that paws them, or a pickup bumper. A light tube gate on a pen where cattle crowd will be bowed within a season. The gaps between bars also let calves, goats and sheep put their heads through, and small stock walk under a gate that hangs high.

### Bull gates and corral gates

"Bull gate" is a heavier class of tube gate, typically 6 rails of about 2 inch tubing in 16 gauge, with more vertical stays than a utility gate. Some makers sell rough stock or corral gates in 14 gauge for pens where cattle are worked and crowded. These are the gates to hang in [corrals and working pens](/corrals-working-pens-waco/), alleys, and pasture gates where cattle bunch up at feeding time.

They weigh more, so they need a hinge post and brace that can carry them. A heavy gate on a light post just moves the failure to the post.

### Wire-filled gates

A tube frame with welded wire mesh (often 2 by 4 inch) filling the openings. They keep foals, calves, goats and dogs from putting their heads through the rails and stop small stock from walking through. They are common on [horse fence](/horse-fence-waco/) paddocks, around barns, and on [goat and sheep fence](/goat-sheep-fence-waco/). The mesh can bend if a horse paws it, and on an open cattle pasture it is more gate than you need.

### Welded pipe gates

Built from steel pipe, often 2-3/8 or 2-7/8 inch oilfield pipe to match a pipe fence, and hung on heavy weld-on or bolt-on hinges. These are the heaviest gates on a ranch and the longest-lived. They take a bump from a trailer, they do not dent when a bull rubs on them, and they match the rest of a [pipe fence](/pipe-fence-waco/) so the whole line looks like one job. For the difference between pipe sizes, see [what size pipe for a pipe fence](/blog/what-size-pipe-for-pipe-fence/).

A pipe gate needs a pipe post sized for it and set deep in concrete. On blackland clay that shrinks and swells, a heavy gate on a shallow post will lean by the second summer. On limestone west of the Brazos, the post hole usually has to be drilled.

### Wire gaps

The cheapest gate on a place: barbed wire strands on a few stays, pulled shut with a lever or a wire loop over the post. On a back fence you open twice a year, a well-built wire gap is fine. It is a poor choice where you drive through often or keep horses.

### Tube gauge in plain numbers

Gauge is wall thickness, and a lower number means a thicker wall. In round tubing, 14 gauge is about 0.083 inch, 16 gauge about 0.065 inch, and 20 gauge about 0.035 inch. That puts a 14 gauge wall at more than twice the thickness of a 20 gauge wall, and more than a quarter thicker than a 16. Diameter matters too: a 2 inch tube is stiffer than a 1-5/8 inch tube of the same gauge. For pens and crowding pens, 14 gauge earns its weight. For a pasture gate cattle rarely lean on, 16 is usually plenty.

## Single gate or double gate?

Past 16 feet, most openings are better as two leaves than one long gate. A 20 foot opening hung as two 10 foot leaves puts half the weight on each hinge post, sags less, and lets you open one side for a pickup without swinging 20 feet of steel.

Double gates need a center stop or a drop rod on one leaf so the pair has something to latch against, and the ground at the center has to stay level or the drop rod will not seat. They also need two good hinge posts instead of one hinge post and one latch post.

A single long leaf still makes sense where one side of the opening sits against a building or bank. If you hang a single 16 or 18 footer, its hinge post should be the heaviest post and brace on the line. ## Matching the gate to the fence and the stock

A few pairings that work in Central Texas:

- **Cattle on barbed wire or net wire:** 12 to 16 foot tube or bull gates, bull gates where cattle crowd.
- **Horses:** wire-filled or smooth pipe gates at fence height, with no diagonal braces a leg can slip into. Penn State suggests placing horse gates toward the middle of a fence line so a horse is not trapped in a corner.
- **Goats, sheep and hogs:** hang the gate with the bottom as close to the ground as the grade allows. Feral hogs will root under a big gap.
- **High fence:** gates as tall as the fence. A 50 inch gate in an 8 foot game fence is a hole.
- **Road entrances:** heavy pipe gates, often doubled, or a cattle guard with a gate beside it for moving stock.

## Getting the openings right before you buy

The most common gate mistakes are not about the gate. They are an opening set 12 feet wide where a baler needs 16, a post set 2 feet deep in clay, or a gate placed in a corner where a trailer cannot turn in. Walk each opening with the widest thing that will go through it, measure the clear space between posts, and think about which way you will approach it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common farm gate size?

On working pastures, 12 to 16 feet covers most gates, with 16 the safest pick for anywhere a trailer or hay equipment goes. Walk gates are usually 4 feet. Remember that the listed size is the opening the gate fits, not the gate's actual length, so a 16 foot gate is built a few inches short to leave room for hinges and a latch.

### How wide should a farm gate be for a tractor?

For a utility tractor alone, 12 feet works if you drive through straight. With a front loader, a baler or a shredder, go 14 to 16 feet. A common round baler runs about 9 and a half feet wide on standard tires and close to 11 on flotation tires, which leaves little room in a 12 foot opening. If you have to turn into the gate, add a few more feet.

### What is the difference between a tube gate and a bull gate?

A utility tube gate uses thin-wall tubing, often around 20 gauge, and is fine for light use on interior fences. A bull gate uses heavier tubing, typically about 2 inch in 16 gauge with more stays, and stands up to cattle crowding and leaning on it. Bull gates weigh more, so they need a stouter hinge post and brace.

### Is 14 gauge or 16 gauge better for a ranch gate?

14 gauge has a thicker wall, about 0.083 inch against 0.065 inch for 16 gauge, so it resists bending better. For pens, alleys and gates where cattle crowd, 14 gauge is worth it. For a pasture gate that cattle rarely lean on, 16 gauge is usually plenty. Either one only works if the post holding it is heavy enough and set deep.

### Should I use one long gate or a double gate?

Up to 16 feet, a single gate is simpler and cheaper to hang. Past that, two leaves meeting in the middle sag less, put less load on each post, and let you open one side for a pickup. A double gate needs a center stop or drop rod and level ground in the middle, so plan the grade before the posts go in.
