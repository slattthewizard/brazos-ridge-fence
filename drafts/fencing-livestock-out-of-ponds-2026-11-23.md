# Fence Around a Pond: How to Keep Livestock Out of a Stock Pond and Still Water Them

If you want to keep cattle, horses or goats from wrecking a stock pond, you have two workable options. Fence the whole pond and water the herd from a trough, or fence most of it and leave one hardened access point. Either way, the fence goes around the dam and the banks first, because that is where hooves do the damage. Full exclusion protects the most. A single access point costs less and is easier to live with in a dry summer.

Most Central Texas stock ponds were dug to water livestock, so fencing one off feels backwards at first. It isn't. A pond that cattle wade in all day turns muddy, loses its edges and slowly fills. ## Why fence a stock pond at all

You don't have to. Plenty of ponds around McLennan, Bosque and Falls counties have had cattle on them for decades. But three problems show up over and over when stock have the whole shoreline.

### Banks wear down and the pond gets shallow at the edges

Texas A&M AgriLife Extension says pond edges wear down from rain, wind and livestock, which widens the pond and creates shallow areas where aquatic plants take hold ([AgriLife publication EWF-110, Aquatic Vegetation Prevention](https://extension.rwfm.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2021/04/EWF-110-AV-Prevention.pdf)). It also notes that most ponds in Texas are used as livestock watering "tanks," and that those ponds keep collecting nutrients from livestock waste. Cattle trailing in and out also cut paths down the bank that turn into gullies after the next hard rain.

### Water quality drops

A pond that cattle wade in is a pond that stays cloudy. Clay particles stay suspended once they're stirred up, and manure goes in with the mud. If you run a few head and the pond is only for them, you may not care. If you also fish it, water a horse barn from it, or the pond drains toward a neighbor, you probably do.

### The dam takes the abuse

The dam is the one part of a pond you can't afford to lose. Hooves on a dam face leave bare, packed ground. Bare ground on a slope erodes. A fence across the dam, or a fence that keeps stock off the dam entirely, is cheap protection for the most expensive piece of earthwork on the place.

## Two ways to do it: full exclusion or one access point

### Full exclusion with a trough

The fence closes the whole pond off, and water goes to the cattle in a trough somewhere else. This is what most extension and conservation material points to as the cleanest option. The AgriLife best management practice sheet on watering facilities (NRCS practice code 614) describes an off-stream water supply, such as a trough, as a way to cut bacteria and sediment going into water, reduce bank erosion from trampling, and keep animals out of muddy footing ([AgriLife SP-412, Reducing Bacteria with Best Management Practices for Livestock: Watering Facility](https://forages.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/26/legacy-files/PDF/Reducing%20Bacteria%20with%20Best%20Management%20Practices%20for%20Livestock_Watering%20Facility_SP-412.pdf)). That sheet reports E. coli reductions of 85 percent from an off-stream water supply, and notes that during drought, when surface water dries up, an alternative source can keep cattle producers in business.

That last point matters here. A pond can go dry. A trough fed from a well, a line or a tank doesn't care how the summer went.

### One hardened access point

You fence most of the pond and leave a short stretch open where stock can walk in to drink. AgriLife says limiting cattle to a single watering point or a few, and placing large cobblestones in those access points, may decrease wading time and erosion and reduce suspended clay and nutrient concentrations ([EWF-110](https://extension.rwfm.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2021/04/EWF-110-AV-Prevention.pdf)). It says "may," and you should read it that way. It's a big improvement over open shoreline. It isn't the same as keeping them out.

In practice, an access point works like this:

- A gentle ramp, not a steep cut bank, so cattle can walk in and walk out.
- Rock or other firm material on the ramp so it doesn't turn into a mud pit.
- A fence on both sides of the ramp that runs out into the water far enough that stock can't walk around the end.
- A width sized to the herd, narrow enough that cattle line up rather than spread out.

### Which should you pick?

| | Full exclusion + trough | One access point |
|---|---|---|
| Water quality | Best | Better than open shoreline |
| Dam and bank protection | Best | Good if the fence covers the dam |
| Depends on | A water source for the trough | Pond holding water |
| Upkeep | Trough, float, line | Ramp, fence ends, mud |
| Fits | Dry-prone land, larger herds, ponds you fish | Small herds, tight budgets |

If the pond is your only water in the pasture, full exclusion needs a plan for where the trough water comes from. Don't fence a pond off until the cattle have somewhere else to drink.

## Getting water to a trough below the dam

If the pond sits above the trough, gravity can do the work. A common setup runs a pipe from the pond through or around the dam to a trough on the downhill side. The trough fills on its own, a float valve stops it, and the cattle never touch the pond.

Two cautions. First, a pipe through a dam is earthwork, not fence work. A bad penetration can start a leak that undermines the dam, so have an experienced pond builder or your local NRCS office look at it before anyone cuts into the embankment. Second, plan for the pond level dropping. The intake has to sit low enough to keep pulling water when the pond is down a few feet in August.

If gravity won't work, the other routes are a well with an electric or solar pump, or a line from a water supply. The AgriLife sheet gives Texas NRCS cost figures for these, with watering troughs running from $450 to about $7,600 depending on size and material, electric water pumps $1,900 to $4,000, and solar pumps $5,700 to $12,000 depending on well depth. Those are general figures from a sheet dated 2011, not our prices, so treat them as a rough scale and get current quotes.

Put the trough on firm ground, with a rock or concrete apron around it, and away from the pond edge. A trough placed beside the water simply moves the mud ten feet.

## Building the fence itself

### Where the fence goes

Set it back from the waterline. Pond levels rise and fall, and a fence built right at the shoreline ends up in the water after a wet spring. Leave a strip of grass between the fence and the edge. That strip also helps catch runoff before it reaches the water.

Fence the dam. At minimum, keep stock off the dam face and the spillway, because a spillway is the first place an eroding path turns into a washout. If the pond sits in a draw or the spillway channel carries creek flow, read our guide to [water gap fences](/blog/water-gap-fence/), because the same flood-load problems apply. An exclusion fence in the path of runoff catches debris like any other.

### Which fence

For cattle, a 4 or 5 strand [barbed wire fence](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/) is the standard answer. Our published range is $2.50 to $5.50 per foot. If goats, sheep or calves are in the same pasture, [net wire](/net-wire-fence-waco/) at $3.50 to $8 per foot holds smaller stock. Horses are a different animal and need a smoother, safer fence than barbed wire.

Here's the arithmetic, framed as a hypothetical. Say you have a 1-acre pond. An acre is 43,560 square feet, which makes a circle about 740 feet around the water. Fence the full circle with room to spare and call it 900 feet.

- Barbed wire: 900 ft at $2.50 to $5.50 per foot is about $2,250 to $4,950.
- Net wire: 900 ft at $3.50 to $8 per foot is about $3,150 to $7,200.

If you only fence the dam and one side, say 400 feet, barbed wire comes to about $1,000 to $2,200. Rock, terrain and access change that, and the free estimate is where you find out. Our full table is on the [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page.

For the access ramp, some ranchers use a short run of welded pipe, since cattle lean and push there. Our pipe range is $12 to $28 per foot, so a 40-foot pipe section runs about $480 to $1,120.

### Post and corner notes for pond ground

- Pond ground is often soft or wet. Posts set near the water in heavy clay can heave and lean as the soil shrinks and swells. See [setting posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/).
- Brace every corner and every end of the access ramp. Cattle crowd at the opening and push on the end posts harder than anywhere else on the fence.
- Keep the bottom wire tight to the ground on the access ramp so calves and hogs don't slip under. Feral hogs love ponds, and a cattle fence won't stop them. If hogs are the real pond problem, our post on [hog proof fence](/blog/hog-proof-fence/) covers what does.
- Electric can work as a pond exclusion, particularly as a single offset wire on the pond side of an existing fence. Keep it well back from the water and check the charger after every flood.

## Cost-share and where to start

Conservation programs have helped pay for practices like off-stream water and the fence that goes with them. The AgriLife sheet lists NRCS EQIP cost-share of up to 75 percent for the watering facility practice, and points producers to their County Extension Agent, the local Soil and Water Conservation District or the NRCS office for help. That sheet is old, and program rules, payment rates and sign-up windows change. Nothing here guarantees you'll qualify or get paid. Fence is its own NRCS practice (Code 382), and the usual rule is to talk to the NRCS service center before you build, and ask whether work done before approval is eligible.

## Mistakes we'd steer you away from

- **Fencing right to the waterline.** The first wet spring puts the posts in the water.
- **Leaving the dam out.** The pond may look protected while the embankment is still taking the traffic.
- **No water plan.** Closing the pond before the trough works leaves cattle at your gate.
- **Skipping the ramp.** An access point without firm footing just relocates the mud.
- **Forgetting drought.** A low pond pulls cattle toward whatever water is left. Build the trough for the worst August, not an average one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I fence my stock pond?

It depends on how hard the pond is used. If a few head share a big pond, you may get by without it. If the banks are breaking down, the water stays muddy, or cattle are loafing on the dam, a fence usually pays for itself. Even a partial fence that protects the dam and leaves one access ramp is a solid middle ground.

### Can cattle drink from a pond with a fence around it?

Yes, through either of two setups. A hardened access point lets them walk in at one spot. Or you pipe water out to a trough so they never touch the pond. AgriLife extension material says limiting access to one or a few watering points may reduce wading time and erosion, though a trough keeps animals out of the pond entirely.

### How much does it cost to fence a pond?

At our published rates, barbed wire is $2.50 to $5.50 per foot and net wire is $3.50 to $8 per foot. A 900-foot fence around a 1-acre pond comes to roughly $2,250 to $4,950 in barbed wire. Fencing only the dam and one side costs less. Troughs and water lines are separate. The [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page has the full ranges.

### How far back from the water should the fence sit?

Far enough that normal level changes don't put the posts in the water. There is no single number, because it depends on the slope and how much your pond rises. Walk the shoreline after a wet spell and note the high-water mark. Set the fence well above that line, and keep it clear of the spillway.

### Is there cost-share for pond fencing and troughs?

Sometimes. AgriLife Extension lists NRCS EQIP cost-share of up to 75 percent for off-stream watering facilities, and points producers to their County Extension Agent, Soil and Water Conservation District or NRCS office. The sheet is dated and rules change, so nothing is guaranteed. Ask the NRCS service center before you build.
