# Hydraulic Post Driver or Auger? Driven vs Augered Fence Posts in Central Texas

For wood line posts in ground that is not solid rock, a hydraulic post driver sets a stronger post, faster, than an auger and a tamping bar. Extension guidance backs that up: University of Tennessee Extension puts a driven post at 1.7 times as strong as a tamped one. The auger still earns its place. It is the right tool for big corner and gate posts, for pipe set in concrete, and for the limestone west of the Brazos where a driver just bounces.

Most good ranch fences in McLennan, Bosque and Falls counties use both machines on the same job. The driver handles the long runs of line posts. The auger, or a rock auger, handles the corners, the gates, and whatever rock the line runs across.

## Why a driven post holds better

When you auger a hole, you take soil out, stand a post in the gap, and put loose soil back. The post is only as tight as your backfill. Tamp it well, in thin lifts, and it holds. Rush it, and you have a post sitting in a column of fluffed-up dirt that will never be as dense as the ground around it.

A driven post does the opposite. Nothing comes out. The post shoves its own volume of soil sideways and down, packing the ground around it tighter than it was before. There is no backfill, no seam between old soil and new, and the post has full friction against firm, packed earth the day it goes in.

That is why the numbers favor driving. The University of Tennessee's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) states plainly that a driven post is 1.7 times as strong as a tamped post. University of Georgia Extension's [Fences for the Farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/) says the same thing in different words: driving is faster than digging and tamping, and it gives the post a stronger foundation.

There is a Central Texas reason too. In blackland clay, the backfill in an augered hole is a softer plug surrounded by hard, tight clay. When the clay dries and cracks in August, water from the next storm finds that plug first. A driven post has no plug. The clay closes right against the wood.

## The equipment, piece by piece

### Hydraulic post driver

A heavy hammer inside a guide frame, run off a tractor's hydraulics or mounted on a skid steer. The operator sets the post under the hammer, plumbs it, and the hammer drops on the top again and again until the post reaches its mark. In decent soil a line post goes in fast, which is why a driven fence moves along quicker than an augered one. Tractor and skid steer mounts both work. The skid steer is handier on rough ground and tight corners, the tractor is steadier on long straight runs.

It is the fastest way to set wood and pipe line posts. It is not the tool for a rock ledge.

### Post pounder

The hand tool: a heavy steel tube with handles, closed at one end, that you lift and slam down over a post. It is made for T-posts and small posts. Nobody should be driving 5 or 6 inch wood posts with one. Gas and air powered pounders exist for T-posts in hard ground, and they save a lot of shoulders on a long run.

### Post hole auger

A spiral bit spun by a tractor PTO, a skid steer hydraulic motor, or a small engine on a one or two-man rental unit. It pulls soil up and out and leaves a round hole. A skid steer auger has far more torque than a handheld unit and will dig full-depth corner holes in dry clay. A one-man rental auger is fine for a dozen posts in soft soil and miserable for a hundred in anything else.

### Rock auger and core drill

A rock auger is a heavy-duty bit with carbide teeth, usually on a skid steer or mini excavator with real hydraulic flow behind it. It grinds through fractured limestone and caliche that stops a dirt bit cold. A core drill cuts a round plug out of solid rock instead of grinding it all to dust. Both are slow compared to driving, and both are how you get a real hole in real rock. We covered the rock methods in detail in [setting fence posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/), so we will not repeat that here.

| Method | Best for | Holding power | Weak spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic post driver | Wood and pipe line posts in clay or loam | Highest for its size, no backfill | Stops or splits posts on rock ledges |
| Hand post pounder | T-posts, small posts | Good for T-posts | Useless for large wood posts |
| Auger, set and tamped | Corners, gate posts, big diameters | Only as good as the tamping | Loose backfill lets water in |
| Auger, set in concrete | Pipe corners, gate posts | Strong if deep and shaped right | Shallow concrete heaves in clay |
| Rock auger or core drill | Limestone, caliche, solid shelf | Excellent once grouted or packed | Slow, and the most expensive holes on the line |

## Soil decides more than the machine

The honest answer to "driven or augered" is usually "what is under your grass." Around Waco you can cross three kinds of ground in a single pasture.

### Blackland clay east and south of Waco

This is driver country. The Houston Black clay across much of the Blackland Prairie is very deep, and the USDA's [official series description](https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/H/HOUSTON_BLACK.html) lists high shrink-swell with cracks up to 4 inches wide at a foot down in dry spells. Water pours into it when it is cracked and barely moves when it is wet.

That behavior is hard on augered posts. Every crack that opens beside a backfilled hole is a channel straight to the bottom of the post. A driven post, with clay packed tight against it from top to bottom, gives the water much less to work with. It still moves some in a bad drought, but less.

Two cautions. First, in late summer, dry blackland can be hard enough that a driver works harder than you expect, and a tired or dry post can split. Second, in a wet winter, clay can ball up on auger flights and smear the sides of the hole slick. If your whole place is blackland, [fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers depth and footing choices in more detail.

### Limestone and caliche west of the Brazos

Head toward Crawford, Valley Mills, Clifton and Meridian and the soil thins out over limestone. Here the driver's weakness shows. A post hits a ledge at 12 or 14 inches and stops. Keep hammering and the top mushrooms, the post splits, or a steel post bends. A post that stopped short is a post that will lean.

In broken, layered rock, a driver sometimes punches through. In a solid shelf it will not. That is where the rock auger or drill comes out. On a lot of places west of the river, the practical answer is to drive most of the line and drill the stretches where the rock comes up, which is why nobody can give you an honest number on rocky ground without walking it.

### Creek and river bottoms

Deeper soil along the Brazos, the Bosque and the bigger creeks usually drives easily. The catch is the water. Posts near a crossing take flood debris on the wire, so they need depth more than speed, and [water gap fences](/blog/water-gap-fence/) get their own design.

## Corners, braces and gate posts

Line posts only have to stand up and hold the wire in place. Corners, ends and gate posts carry the whole pull of the fence, and that changes the math.

UGA Extension notes that corner posts can be driven, but that it is sometimes necessary and always advisable to drill a pilot hole about 3 to 4 inches smaller than the post first. They also recommend driving end posts at a slight lean away from the direction of pull so they stand straight once the wire is tight. The Tennessee bulletin recommends corner and gate posts at least 8 inches in diameter and brace posts 5 inches or more, and notes that posts larger than 4 inches may need a dull point or a pilot hole to drive.

Put those together and the rule on most Central Texas jobs is simple:

- Line posts get driven.
- Brace posts get driven if the ground allows, or piloted and driven.
- Big corner and gate posts get augered, either set and tamped hard or piloted and driven, and they go deep.
- Pipe corners and gate posts that will hang a heavy gate usually get augered and set in concrete, with the footing shaped so clay cannot grab and lift it.

No matter how a corner post goes in, it fails without a proper brace. [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) walks through why.

## The pilot hole: driving and augering together

The best trick in this whole debate is using both tools on one post. Auger an undersized hole, then drive the post into it. The pilot takes the hardest work off the driver, keeps a big post from splitting, and helps it go in plumb. Because the hole is smaller than the post, the post still packs its own soil tight on the way down. You keep most of the driven post's grip without fighting the ground for every inch.

Pilot holes are also how you get through a thin rock layer or a caliche crust. Punch through with a rock auger, then drive the post the rest of the way into the soil underneath.

## When augering and tamping is the right call

Driving is not always the answer. Auger when:

- **The post is too big to drive clean.** Large corner and gate posts, especially dry cedar, split under a hammer.
- **You hit rock.** Solid limestone needs a rock auger or drill, full stop.
- **The post is getting concrete.** Pipe gate posts and some corners are set in concrete by design.
- **You are doing it yourself with rental gear.** Augers are easy to rent. A hydraulic post driver can be harder to find and takes practice to run safely.

If you do auger and tamp, do it the way the Tennessee bulletin describes: center the post, put the soil back in small amounts, tamp each lift hard, and check plumb as you go. Most weak augered posts come from dumping the whole pile in at once. A tamping bar and patience beat a bag of concrete poured around a short post, a common cause of [leaning fence posts](/blog/leaning-fence-posts/) in clay.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are driven fence posts stronger than dug and tamped posts?
Yes, in most soils. University of Tennessee Extension puts a driven post at 1.7 times as strong as a tamped one. Driving packs the surrounding soil tighter and leaves no loose backfill around the post. A carefully tamped post can still hold well, but it depends on how well the backfill was tamped, and in blackland clay that backfill gives water an easy path down the post.

### Can you drive a wood corner post?
You can, but big corner posts often split or wander when driven straight into hard ground. UGA Extension advises drilling a pilot hole about 3 to 4 inches smaller than the post first, and driving end posts with a slight lean away from the pull. Large gate posts and pipe corners are usually augered and set, then braced with an H-brace either way.

### How deep should a driven wood line post go?
UGA Extension says wooden line posts should be set at least 2 feet deep, and preferably deeper. In shrink-swell clay, more depth helps the post ride out wet and dry cycles. Corners, ends and gate posts need to go deeper than line posts because they carry the pull of the whole fence.

### Will a hydraulic post driver work in rocky ground?
Sometimes. In fractured limestone or caliche a heavy driver can punch through. On a solid ledge the post stops short, and hammering on it only splits wood or bends steel. The fix is a rock auger or drill, either to make the full hole or to open a pilot hole through the rock so the post can be driven the rest of the way.

### Should fence posts be pointed before driving?
Small line posts usually drive fine as they come, small end down, which UGA Extension recommends. The Tennessee bulletin notes that posts larger than 4 inches may need to be sharpened to a dull point or driven into an auger-drilled pilot hole. A pilot hole is often the better choice for large posts because it also cuts down on splitting.
