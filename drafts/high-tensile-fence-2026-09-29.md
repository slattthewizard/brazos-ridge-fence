# High Tensile Fence: What It Is, Where It Works, and What It Demands

A high tensile fence is built from hard, springy steel wire, usually 12.5 gauge, that is stretched tight between heavy brace assemblies and held there by in-line strainers. It barely stretches, it springs back when an animal leans on it, and it lets you set line posts much farther apart than on a conventional fence. The tradeoff is simple: every pound of that tension ends up on your corners, so the braces have to be built heavier and set deeper than most people are used to.

On Central Texas land, high tensile wire is a good fit for long, straight runs on open ground, especially as electric cross fence for cattle. It is a poor fit for brushy creek bottoms, tight horse paddocks with no visibility aids, and any fence line where the corners sit in moving blackland clay and nobody plans to check them.

## What makes wire "high tensile"

Fence wire comes in two basic steels. Low-carbon (soft or malleable) wire stretches when you tension it, stays stretched, and sags over the years. High tensile wire is a harder steel that resists stretching and returns to length after a load comes off.

The University of Tennessee Extension publication [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) puts numbers on it. High tensile fences are built mostly with 12.5 or 14 gauge Class 3 galvanized wire rated at 170,000 to 200,000 psi or more, with a breaking strength of roughly 1,800 pounds per wire. It says the fence can take more than 1,100 pounds of livestock pressure without losing its elasticity.

The [NRCS Texas Fence (382) specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf) sets similar floors. For smooth high tensile wire it calls for 12.5 gauge with Class III galvanizing, at least 170,000 psi for fences of three or more wires and at least 130,000 psi for one- or two-wire fences.

Two practical things follow from that steel:

- **It holds tension for years.** A tight high tensile strand does not creep and sag the way soft wire does, so you are not re-stretching every spring.
- **It does not forgive a bad corner or a careless cut.** The wire will pull a weak brace out of the ground, and a strand cut under load can whip back hard. Gloves and eye protection are not optional when you work on it.

## Three ways high tensile wire gets used

"High tensile fence" means three different things at the feed store.

### Smooth high tensile electric fence

The classic setup: smooth 12.5 gauge strands on insulators, some hot and some grounded, stretched between braced ends and tightened with ratchet strainers. Animals learn to respect the charge, so the fence needs fewer wires. UT's PB1541 says three to five wires are enough for cattle when the fence is electrified, against eight to ten wires for a non-electric high tensile cattle fence.

Line posts can open way up. UT gives as much as 150 feet apart for an electrified fence on very flat land, with battens between, and NRCS also caps it at 150 feet with two or more stays. In practice terrain, wind and animal pressure pull that number down.

NRCS says the top wire should always be hot, and with three or more wires they alternate hot and ground, top and bottom hot. The spec adds that where soil dries out too much to give a good shock, a mix of positive and negative wires should be used. That is a real concern in a Texas August. If you already have a fence that stopped biting, our [electric fence troubleshooting guide](/blog/electric-fence-not-working/) walks through the usual causes.

### Non-electric smooth high tensile

Smooth wire with no charger, usually chosen for horses or to keep barbs away from people and wildlife. NRCS Texas notes that non-energized high tensile needs more strands than barbed wire for the same control, and UT puts it at eight to ten wires for cattle. For most cattle work, that math sends people back to barbed or net wire, or to a charger.

### High tensile barbed wire

High tensile barbed wire is usually 15.5 gauge: two thin, hard strands twisted together with barbs, and much lighter to handle than soft 12.5 gauge. It builds a conventional five-strand cattle fence and falls in the same price band as any [barbed wire fence](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/): $2.50 to $5.50 per foot. Terrain, clearing, rock and corner count decide where in that band you land more than the wire does.

High tensile barbed is a physical barrier. It is not meant to carry a charge.

| Setup | Typical wire | Strands for cattle | Carries a charge | Best spot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smooth HT electric | 12.5 ga smooth, Class 3 | 3 to 5 (UT PB1541) | Yes | Interior cross fence, rotational grazing, open ground |
| Smooth HT, non-electric | 12.5 ga smooth, Class 3 | 8 to 10 (UT PB1541) | No | Horse turnout with visibility aids, where barbs are unwanted |
| HT barbed | 15.5 ga barbed | Usually 5 | No | Perimeter and cross fence on long, clean runs |

## Why high tensile needs stronger braces

UT PB1541 calls corner and end assemblies the backbone of the fence and notes that some producers have ripped them out of the ground while tightening wires, because the posts were set too shallow. Multiply 200 pounds of working tension by five or eight strands and you can see why. Rules worth building to:

- **One brace per direction.** UT says a corner post needs a brace assembly for each fence leading into it.
- **Double span on longer pulls.** Over 200 feet of fence, UT recommends a double span assembly, more than twice as strong as a single span.
- **Braced line assemblies in long runs.** UT puts a braced line assembly every 650 feet between distant corners.
- **Depth.** NRCS Texas requires brace posts buried at least 3 feet unless driven, and driven 2 3/8 inch pipe goes 5 feet.
- **Brace height.** NRCS puts the horizontal brace at no less than 2/3 of the top wire height.

Our guide to [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) explains why corners fail.

### In-line strainers

What separates high tensile from soft wire fence is the in-line strainer, a small ratchet in each wire that takes up slack without re-stretching the run. NRCS Texas puts strainers near the center of the line so tension pulls in equally from both ends, no more than 4,000 feet apart on straight stretches or 1,200 feet on uneven or curving ground. UT says runs under 600 feet can have the strainer near one end.

UT's method: tension one wire to 200 pounds with an indicator spring, then match the rest by feel. Over-tightening damages the wire and can lift posts out of low spots.

### Splices

For high tensile, NRCS lists crimp sleeves (two 3/8 inch or one 3/4 inch, ends overlapped at least 2 inches, rated at 80 percent or more of the wire's strength) or a figure-eight knot, not the wrapped splices used on soft wire.

## Where high tensile fits in Central Texas

The steel is the same everywhere. The ground in McLennan, Bosque and Falls counties is not.

### Where it works

**Open blackland and improved pasture.** Long straight lines across flat or gently rolling ground are what high tensile was made for. Say you have 200 acres of coastal east of Waco to split into four pastures for rotational grazing. Smooth high tensile electric cross fence makes sense there: long runs, clean ground.

**Cross fence behind a good perimeter.** NRCS Texas marks its one- and two-wire electric cattle designs as cross fence for grazing systems, not recommended for exterior fences. Its five-wire design is rated for boundary use, but that is a lot of wire, braces and charger to keep hot on a road line. Keep the road frontage physical (barbed, net wire or pipe) and put the electric high tensile inside.

**Offset wires on an old fence.** UT notes an electrified wire on offset brackets can keep a worn barbed or woven wire fence going for many more years. NRCS spaces the brackets 50 to 60 feet apart at about 2/3 the height of the animal.

### Where it does not

**Moving clay under the corners.** Blackland clay shrinks in drought and swells when it rains. Any wire fence goes slack when a corner moves. High tensile wire has almost no stretch to take up the difference, so every inch a corner gives comes straight off the tension across the whole pull. If your corners sit in that clay, they need to be set deep and checked, which our guide to [setting fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers.

**Brushy creek bottoms and cedar.** A limb across a hot wire grounds it, and flood debris loads it. Cedar and mesquite growing into the line will short an electric fence all summer. High tensile does not suit a line you cannot keep clear.

**Rock west of the Brazos.** Thin soil over limestone around Valley Mills, Clifton and Meridian means brace posts often have to be drilled and set in rock to reach depth. That shifts cost into the corners and eats the savings from spreading out line posts.

**Hog pressure at the bottom wire.** Feral hogs root under and lift low wires, and long spans between posts give them room to do it. Where hogs work a line, look at a hot bottom wire, tighter spacing, or a different fence.

**Cold snaps and ice.** NRCS reminds builders that wire tightens in cold weather and loosens in heat. A fence cranked hard in August is tighter still in a January freeze, so tension to spec and use the ratchets.

## High tensile horse fence

The case for high tensile around horses is strength and low upkeep. The case against it is that bare smooth wire is hard to see. UT PB1541 warns that high tensile wire can entangle horses and suggests closer post spacing or ribbons to improve visibility. The University of Georgia's [Fences for Horses](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) says visibility is not a strength of smooth steel electric fence, that vinyl-coated wire adds significantly to it, and it describes vinyl rails 4 to 6 inches wide with high tensile wires encased. Penn State's [Fence Planning for Horses](https://extension.psu.edu/fence-planning-for-horses) points out a hazard people miss: diagonal brace wires on tension fences leave triangular spaces where a horse can trap a foot or head.

For small paddocks around Waco, coated high tensile or pipe usually beats bare smooth wire. Our [horse fence](/horse-fence-waco/) page gives general Central Texas market ranges: no-climb 2x4 mesh at $6 to $10 per foot, no-climb with a pipe top rail at $12 to $18 per foot, and full welded pipe at $12 to $28 per foot. Bare high tensile is best kept to large pastures where horses have room and time to learn the fence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What gauge is high tensile fence wire?
Most smooth high tensile fence wire is 12.5 gauge with Class 3 galvanizing. The NRCS Texas fence specification calls for 12.5 gauge high tensile with Class III coating and a minimum strength of 170,000 psi for fences of three or more wires. High tensile barbed wire is usually lighter, at 15.5 gauge, because the harder steel lets a thinner wire carry similar loads.

### How tight should high tensile wire be?
University of Tennessee Extension gives 200 pounds per wire as adequate tension for 12.5 gauge high tensile. Set one wire with a tension indicator spring, then match the others by feel. Over-tightening damages the wire and can pull posts out of low spots, and wire tightens further in cold weather, so avoid cranking it as hard as it will go.

### How many strands of high tensile wire do cattle need?
If the fence is electrified, UT PB1541 says three to five wires are enough for cattle. A non-electric high tensile cattle fence needs far more, eight to ten wires in the same publication. That gap is why most smooth high tensile fence in cattle country is electric and used as interior cross fence behind a physical perimeter.

### Is high tensile wire safe for horses?
It can be, with care. Bare smooth wire is hard for horses to see and can entangle them, so extension guides recommend coated wire, polymer rails with high tensile inside, flagging, or closer post spacing. Watch the diagonal brace wires too, since they leave spaces where a hoof or head can get caught. Our [horse fence](/horse-fence-waco/) page covers safer options.

### Can high tensile wire be used on the perimeter?
High tensile barbed wire can, and it builds a normal five-strand perimeter. Smooth high tensile electric is better kept to interior cross fence. NRCS Texas does not recommend its one- and two-wire electric designs for exterior fences, and a charger failure or a cedar limb on the line means a road-frontage fence stops holding stock until someone finds the problem.
