# Fire Damaged Fence: What to Save, What to Replace, and in What Order

A grass fire does not ruin a fence the way it looks like it did. After the smoke clears, the wire is gray and streaked, the wood posts are black stubs, and it feels like the whole line has to come out. Usually it doesn't. Texas A&M research on grass fires found that the wire itself kept its strength. The posts, especially wood ones, are what you actually lose.

So the job after a fire is sorting. Wood posts and stays that burned get replaced. Wire gets tested before you decide. Steel posts, braces and gates get checked for bends and loose ground. This article walks through that sort, what the repair work costs, and where USDA disaster help may fit. ## Start with stock and safety, not the fence

Before you touch wire, deal with the animals. Burned ground and a down fence mean cattle, horses or goats can be anywhere. Get them into a pen or a trap with a good fence first. Our post on [storm damaged fence repair](/blog/storm-damaged-fence-repair/) covers the first-day order, and most of it applies after a fire too.

A few fire-specific cautions:

- Smoldering posts can reignite. A post burned at the base can hold a coal inside. Wet them down, and check again the next day and after wind picks up.
- Stay out of the burn until the fire agency says it's clear. Stumps, stump holes and burned-out root cavities can hide hot spots and collapse under a boot or tire.
- Wear gloves and eye protection when you handle wire. Cut or broken strands under tension snap back.
- If you run an electric offset, shut the charger off and inspect before you turn it on.

## What a grass fire does to fence wire

This is the part people get wrong in both directions. Some folks assume all the wire is trash. Others assume it's fine because it's still hanging there.

Researchers at Texas A&M tested barbed wire exposed to prairie grass fires, including wire that had burned as many as six times over 13 years. They found no loss in breaking strength or ductility, and the zinc coating still resisted corrosion. The gray or brown staining you see afterward looked like rust, but the researchers attributed it to iron and zinc alloy layers, and said it does not mean the coating failed. You can read the summary at [AgriLife Today, "Study shows wildfire does not damage barbed wire"](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2017/03/16/study-shows-wildfire-does-not-damage-barbed-wire/).

That finding has limits, and it's worth saying so plainly:

- It was a grass fire study. Grass fire passes quickly. A fire that burns for a long time in one spot, such as a cedar brake, a brush pile, a stack of round bales or a dead tree standing against the fence, puts a lot more heat into whatever wire is near it. That finding doesn't speak to those cases, so treat wire that sat against a hot spot as suspect.
- Age still matters. Old wire that was already weak will be the first to break when you stretch it. A fence that was near the end of its life before the fire is still near the end of its life after it. Our post on [rusted barbed wire repair or replace](/blog/rusted-barbed-wire-repair-or-replace/) shows how to tell a worn strand from a sound one.
- The coating is not the only thing that can go. A strand that has lost its galvanizing in spots will rust faster even if it still holds load today.

### A simple wire test you can do at the fence

Pick three or four spots along the burned run, including the hottest-looking one, and do these two checks:

1. Cut out a short piece and bend it back on itself, then straighten it, a few times. Sound wire bends and straightens. Brittle wire cracks or snaps in one or two bends.
2. Wrap a strand around a post or a short piece of pipe. It should bend smoothly and keep its shape without flaking or splitting.

Wire that passes both on a section that was in the hot part of the burn is usually fine to reuse as a whole. Wire that fails one is a signal to test more spots before you trust the rest. If it comes up short everywhere, replace the strand and move on.

Expect splices where wire let go next to burned posts. A splice and retension of a run typically lands at $400 to $900 on our repair work.

## Judging the posts

Posts are where the fire really takes its money. What you do depends on what the post is made of.

### Wood posts

Burned wood posts and stays have to be replaced. That's the Texas A&M recommendation. The part of the post that matters is the section from a few inches above the soil down to a foot or so below it, where the post carries the load. Fire eats that section first because dry grass burns right at ground level.

Here's how to sort them:

- **Charred surface only, solid underneath.** Probe with a screwdriver or the point of a pocket knife. If it goes in less than about a quarter inch and the wood underneath is hard, the post may keep working. Treat it as a post on probation and check it again after the first wet season.
- **Burned at ground line, stub left, post leaning or loose.** Replace it. A post that has lost its cross section at the ground line will snap when wire is stretched or cattle lean on it.
- **Burned all the way through or hollow.** Replace it, and replace the posts on both sides of it if they're marginal.

Our post comparing [cedar vs treated pine posts](/blog/cedar-vs-treated-pine-posts/) covers how each holds up in the ground, which matters when you pick replacements. Either one can be charred on the outside and still sound in the middle, so probe before you condemn a post.

Corner posts and braces get the closest look. Wood H-braces carry every pound of tension on the line. If one burned, the brace is gone even if the post looks fine. Rebuild the corner before you restring anything; see [H brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) for how they work. A rebuilt corner or H-brace runs $600 to $1,400 on our price list.

### Steel posts

Steel T-posts and pipe are much more forgiving. Check for these:

- Posts that bent or leaned where a burning tree or heavy stack fell on them.
- Loose posts. Fire burns off the grass roots, and in blackland clay the ground can crack and shift once it dries out further. A post that was solid before may rock now.
- Burned-off paint and coating on pipe and gates. That is cosmetic at first, but bare steel rusts, so plan to repaint.
- Clips and tie wires. Plastic or lightly coated clips can melt or burn off, and then the wire sits loose against the post.

T-posts that are straight and tight can be reused. T-posts that are bent get pulled and replaced; don't straighten them in place. Pipe fence that is straight and tight generally needs cleaning and paint, not rebuilding.

### Gates

Wood gate frames and entrance posts burn like line posts do. If the gate is intact but its post is not, reset the post first, then rehang.

## Salvage or replace: how to make the call

A rough sort that holds for most Central Texas lines:

| Situation | Usual call |
|---|---|
| Wire sound on bend test, staining only, posts mostly steel | Keep the wire, replace the damaged posts, splice breaks |
| Wire sound, but half the wood posts burned | Keep the wire, reset posts, retension everything |
| Wire brittle in the hot section, sound elsewhere | Replace the bad section only |
| Old wire that was already rusty and loose | Replace the run rather than pay to reset posts under it |
| Corners and braces burned | Rebuild the corners first, then deal with the line |

## Order of work after the fire

1. Get stock secured on ground with a sound fence, and keep them off the burn until the line is rebuilt.
2. Photograph everything before you move anything: burned posts, broken wire, downed trees. This helps with any claim or program application.
3. Call your insurance agent and your county FSA office, as covered below, before you start hauling out wire and posts you may need to show.
4. Rebuild corners and braces first.
5. Replace burned line posts.
6. Splice or replace wire, then retension the run.
7. Check gates and latches, and test the electric offset before turning it back on.

Set posts before you stretch wire. Wire pulled against unset posts goes slack by spring.

## Insurance and USDA

Insurance depends entirely on the policy. Fence on a farm and ranch policy is often limited or excluded, and a homeowners policy may treat a fence as an "other structure" with its own limit. Our post on [whether insurance covers fence damage](/blog/does-insurance-cover-fence-damage/) breaks down the difference and how to document a claim. Read your policy before you assume.

The other place to look is your county office of the USDA Farm Service Agency. After some disasters, FSA opens signups for help restoring farmland, and fence is sometimes part of that. Programs, cost-share rates and deadlines change, so get them from the county office directly. Whether your land and your fire qualify is up to FSA, not us or anyone else. Call and ask:

- Is any disaster assistance open for my county and this fire?
- Do I need to apply or get approval before I start rebuilding?
- What damage records do you want to see?

Photograph the damage first, and don't haul everything off until you've had that answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does fire ruin barbed wire?

Not usually. Texas A&M tested wire exposed to grass fires, including wire burned several times, and found no loss in breaking strength or ductility. The staining is mostly iron and zinc alloy, not coating failure. That study was of grass fires. A burning brush pile or tree against the fence is a different case, so test wire from the hottest spots.

### Do burned wood fence posts have to be replaced?

If the post burned at the ground line and lost wood, yes. Texas A&M's advice is that burned wooden posts and stays have to be replaced. A post that is only surface charred and still hard when you probe it may keep working for a while. Corner posts and braces deserve the closest check because they carry the load.

### Can I reuse steel T-posts after a fire?

Straight, tight T-posts can be reused. Pull and replace any that are bent. Check that the ground around each one is still firm, because fire burns off the grass roots and the soil can shift. Also check clips and tie wires, since plastic and light coatings may have burned off.

### When is grass fire season in Texas?

Texas A&M Forest Service describes the dormant season as winter and spring, when grass cured by freezing burns and passing cold fronts bring stronger winds. Its [January 2026 outlook](https://tfsweb.tamu.edu/dry-conditions-and-increased-vegetation-set-stage-for-higher-wildfire-risk-in-texas/) said areas west of Interstate 35 had heavy grass loads.
