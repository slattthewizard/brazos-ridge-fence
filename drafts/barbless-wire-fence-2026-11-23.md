# Barbless Wire Fence: Where It Works, Where Cattle Will Beat It

Barbless wire is fence wire with the barbs left off. Most of what's sold under that name is two strands of smooth galvanized wire twisted together, so it looks like barbed wire without the points. It's safer around horses and deer than barbed wire, it's easier on your hands, and it holds a line well. What it won't do is stop a cow that has decided to lean on it, unless you add a hot wire or a lot more strands.

That one trade-off decides almost every question about it. On a horse pasture, a deer crossing or a gentle interior fence, barbless is a sensible choice. On a road fence with a bull behind it, it usually isn't. ## What barbless wire actually is

The term gets used for three different things, and mixing them up leads to bad fence.

- **Twisted barbless wire.** Two smooth strands twisted together, usually sold in rolls to be strung like barbed wire.
- **Single-strand smooth wire.** One plain wire, either soft low-carbon or high tensile. This is what most people mean when they say "smooth wire fence."
- **Smooth high tensile wire.** A single hard-steel strand, normally 12.5 gauge, strung under heavy tension on strong braces. That's a different system with its own rules, covered on our [high tensile fence](/blog/high-tensile-fence/) page.

This article is about the first one and how it compares with the other two. Twisted barbless wire is built like a barbed wire fence and stapled to posts the same way. You still need good corners, and you still need to keep the wire tight. If you aren't sure how the gauge numbers work, the [barbed wire gauge](/blog/barbed-wire-gauge/) guide explains them, and the same numbers apply to the smooth strands here.

## Where barbless wire beats barbed

### Horses

Horses are the reason most people search for this. Extension guidance is blunt about barbed wire and horses. The University of Tennessee's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) says barbed wire should be avoided because there are many chances for a horse to tear its hide on the barbs. Texas A&M's veterinary school makes a narrower point in its [Horse Enclosures](https://vetmed.tamu.edu/news/pet-talk/horse-enclosures) article: a three-strand barbed wire fence is a poor choice where horses will be kept on both sides of it, though it could be quite safe on very large pastures with no horses across the fences.

Barbless wire removes the barbs, but not every risk. Horses can still get a leg over a wire or hang a shoe in a loose strand. Smooth wire is also harder to see than a board or a coated rail. The University of Georgia's [Fences for Horses](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) says plainly that visibility is not a characteristic of electric fences built with smooth steel wire, and it recommends adding a vinyl-coated or high-visibility strand. The Tennessee bulletin says the same about high tensile fence: closer posts or ribbons hung on the wire help a horse see it.

So for horses, barbless wire is a step up from barbed and a step down from board, pipe or no-climb mesh. It fits large turnout where horses have room to learn the fence, and it fits cross fence. It's a poor fit for a small paddock with a young horse or a herd that runs. Our [best fence for horses](/blog/best-fence-for-horses/) guide ranks the options, and the [horse fence](/horse-fence-waco/) page covers what we build when barbless isn't enough.

### The bottom strand and the top strand

Deer don't get hung on barbs if there aren't any. A smooth bottom strand lets a fawn slide under, and a smooth top strand won't tear a deer that clips it going over. That's why a lot of ranchers run a standard barbed fence but swap those two wires. We cover the heights and spacing in [wildlife friendly fence](/blog/wildlife-friendly-fence/). You don't have to build the whole fence barbless to get most of the benefit.

### Hot-wire lines

Barbed wire and electricity don't mix. Smooth wire does. If you plan to run current, smooth wire (twisted or single) is the right conductor, and the barbed strands stay dead. The [can you electrify barbed wire](/blog/can-you-electrify-barbed-wire/) article explains why, and why an offset smooth wire is the safe fix for an old fence.

### Handling

This one is plain. Barbless wire doesn't cut your gloves or your arms. If you're building or repairing alone, it's easier to roll out, splice and tie off. Those are real benefits on a long run.

## Where barbless wire fails

### Cattle that push

Barbs are a deterrent. A cow rubbing on a smooth wire feels nothing, so she leans harder. A barbed wire fence holds cattle by discomfort as much as strength, and barbless gives up the discomfort. Anything that relies on strength alone has to be built heavier.

The Tennessee bulletin shows how much weight that carries. For a non-electric high tensile fence for cattle it recommends eight to ten wires. For an electrified one, three to five wires are enough. The animals respect the shock instead of the barbs. Without either, you need a lot more wire than a four or five strand barbed fence.

### Bulls, pressure points and roads

A bull working a cow across the fence, a hungry cow looking at greener grass, or calves testing the bottom wire will all tear through a smooth fence faster than a barbed one. Road frontage is the worst place to find out. If livestock get out, you carry the problem. Our [barbed wire fence](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/) page describes the 5-strand build we use when the fence has to hold cattle without help.

### Feral hogs and goats

A few strands of any wire, barbed or smooth, won't stop them. Hogs root under and goats go through gaps, so those animals need net wire or panel.

## Strand counts and spacing

There's no single published spec for twisted barbless wire, so these numbers come from extension guidance on smooth wire and from standard practice. Treat them as starting points, then adjust after a walk of the line.

| Use | Strands | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Horse perimeter fence | 4 to 5 smooth strands (UGA) | Top wire at 60 inches (5 feet) or higher, bottom 6 to 8 inches off the ground, add a visible strand |
| Horse dividing fence | 4 to 5 | UGA allows 54 inches (4.5 feet) for fences that divide pastures |
| Cattle, electrified | 3 to 5 (UT PB1541) | Alternate hot and ground wires |
| Cattle, not electrified | 8 to 10 (UT PB1541) | Impractical for most ranches. A barbed fence is cheaper |
| Wildlife crossing section | Smooth top and bottom on an otherwise barbed fence | See the wildlife friendly guide |

The horse numbers come from the University of Georgia's bulletin, which sets a minimum height of 5 feet for perimeter pasture fences, 4.5 feet for dividing fences and a bottom wire 6 to 8 inches off the ground. It recommends four to five strands for a high tensile perimeter fence. UGA also says to space line posts about 10 to 12 feet apart for most fences, and 25 feet for high tensile wire fences. Twisted barbless wire is not high tensile wire, so don't stretch your posts out to 25 feet. Stay closer to the 10 to 12 feet for most fences. Closer posts also help a horse see the line, which the Tennessee bulletin notes.

Tennessee's bulletin recommends stringing wire on the inside of the posts, or on the outside of curves, so animal pressure pushes the wire against the post instead of off it.

## Tension, staples and corners

Barbless wire only works when it's tight. A loose smooth strand is the one that catches a hoof.

- **Tension.** Tennessee's bulletin says adequate tension for 12.5 gauge high tensile wire is about 200 pounds, checked with a tension indicator spring. Twisted barbless wire isn't sold as high tensile, so don't assume it can take that load. Follow the maker's tension guidance, and put an inline strainer in each run so you can retension it as it settles.
- **Staples.** Leave a little play. A staple driven tight against the wire pins it where heat and cold can't move it, and the wire kinks or snaps at that spot.
- **Corners and ends.** Barbless gets no discount on braces. Every corner, end and gate needs a proper H-brace, and long runs need a brace every so often. Weak corners are why most fences sag, whatever the wire.
- **Class.** Galvanizing class decides how long smooth wire lasts before it rusts at the twist. Ask what class the roll is and buy the heavier coating where you can.

## Central Texas conditions

Blackland clay shrinks and swells, so posts lean and a smooth wire goes slack in a dry summer. Check tension after a drought, not just after a storm. West of the Brazos and in Bosque County, limestone near the surface means fewer deep-set posts and more reliance on braces. Cedar and mesquite on the fence line push on the wire whatever type it is. Clear the line before you build, and expect to walk the fence after any storm.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is barbless wire fence?

It's fence wire without barbs. Most barbless wire is two smooth galvanized strands twisted together and strung like barbed wire. The same name is also used for plain single-strand smooth wire, including smooth high tensile wire. The twisted type is easier to handle and safer around horses than barbed wire, but it still needs good braces and real tension.

### Is barbless wire safe for horses?

It's safer than barbed wire, which extension guidance says to avoid around horses. It isn't fully safe. Smooth wire is hard to see, and a horse can still get a leg or a shoe caught in a loose strand. Keep it tight, set the posts close, and add something visible such as coated wire, tape or a top rail. Ask for coated or no-climb options if the paddock is small.

### Will barbless wire hold cattle?

Only with help. Barbs deter cattle from leaning, and smooth wire doesn't. UT PB1541 recommends eight to ten wires for non-electric high tensile cattle fence, but only three to five if it's electrified. For most cattle, a hot wire or a standard barbed fence is cheaper and holds better than a smooth fence with no current.

### Is barbless wire the same as smooth high tensile wire?

No. Twisted barbless wire is two strands twisted together. Smooth high tensile wire is a single hard-steel strand, usually 12.5 gauge, strung at much higher tension. UT PB1541 gives about 200 pounds of tension for 12.5 gauge high tensile. The two are built and tensioned differently, so don't treat them as interchangeable.

### Can I use barbless wire on part of a fence?

Yes, and it's a common choice. Many fences run smooth wire on the top and bottom strands only, for deer and fawns, and keep barbed wire in the middle for cattle. It costs little extra and removes the strands deer snag. The usual guidance puts a smooth bottom wire about 16 inches up and keeps the top wire at or under about 42 inches where deer cross.
