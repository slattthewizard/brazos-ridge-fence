# Setting Fence Posts in Concrete: When It Helps a Ranch Fence and When It Hurts

Setting fence posts in concrete is the right call for steel pipe posts, corner and brace posts, and gate posts, because those posts carry the load of the whole fence. It is usually the wrong call for wood line posts and T-posts. Concrete around a wood line post tends to hold water against the wood, and in blackland clay a fat plug near the surface gives the swelling ground something to lift.

For depth, start with one third of the post's length in the ground and go deeper at corners and gates. On most ranch fence around Waco that means line posts at about 2 feet and corner, brace and gate posts at 3 to 4 feet.

## Where concrete earns its keep

Concrete turns a post into a bigger, heavier plug that the soil has to move as a unit. That matters most where a post fights a steady pull or a heavy swinging load.

### Steel pipe posts

Pipe posts are either set in concrete or driven deep. A 2 3/8 or 2 7/8 inch pipe has little surface area, so a pipe dropped in an augered hole and backfilled with dirt gets poor grip. Concrete widens the footprint and stops the pipe rocking in the hole. The [NRCS Texas fence specification](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/16619/382_TX_PS_Fence_2015) (Practice 382, October 2015) calls for a hole at least 12 inches across for any post backfilled with concrete, and for steel pipe to be permanently capped so rain cannot run down inside. Brace posts that are driven instead of set go deeper under the same spec: 5 feet for 2 3/8 inch pipe and 4 feet for 2 7/8 to 4 1/2 inch. Our [welded pipe fence](/pipe-fence-waco/) page covers how pipe lines are built.

### Corners, H-braces and pull points

A quarter mile of stretched barbed wire is anchored to two corners, and every strand pulls on them all day, every day. Concrete at the corner and brace posts adds mass and a wider bearing surface against the soil. It does not replace the brace. A concreted corner with no brace will still lean. For the brace build itself, see [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/).

### Gate posts

A 16 or 20 foot steel gate hanging off one post twists it every time it swings. Gate posts are where concrete and depth pay back the most. A heavy entrance gate wants pipe well above line-post size, set about 4 feet deep in concrete. The [ranch entrance and gate page](/ranch-entrance-gates-waco/) covers the rest of an entrance.

### Low spots and water gaps

The Texas NRCS spec says posts in low areas or gullies need weighting down so they do not lift out, and lists concrete as one approved way to do it. After a flash flood on a Bosque County creek, you see why.

## Where concrete does more harm than good

### Wood line posts

This is the one most people get backward. [UGA Extension's Fences for Horses](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) says concrete is generally not recommended around posts, because a wood post set in it has a shorter life than one properly tamped in dirt.

The way it plays out is simple. Wood swells and shrinks with moisture and concrete does not. After a few wet and dry cycles a small gap opens between post and collar, rain runs down it, and the concrete holds that water like a cup. The post rots at the ground line faster than its neighbor in tamped dirt. The rot side of this is covered in [fence post rot at the ground line](/blog/fence-post-rot/).

### Shallow collars in blackland clay

Around Lorena, West and most of the Falls County farm ground, the soil is expansive blackland clay. A short, wide plug near the surface is the wrong shape there: wet clay swells, grips it, and jacks it up a little each season. Our [clay soil post-setting guide](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers that ground in depth. For concrete, the rule is go deep, keep the plug narrow at the top, and consider stopping the pour below the surface.

## Post type, concrete and depth at a glance

| Post | Concrete? | Rule-of-thumb depth | If not concrete |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steel T-post, line | No | About 2 ft driven (18 in in rocky ground) | Driven, no backfill |
| Wood line post | Usually no | At least 2 ft, deeper in clay | Tamped soil or crushed base in thin lifts |
| Pipe line post | Usually | About 2 ft | Driven deeper |
| Corner or brace post | Yes for pipe; for wood in weak or wet ground | 3 ft minimum, 4 ft on long pulls | Tamped soil, hole 6 in wider than the post |
| Gate post | Yes | About 4 ft | Not recommended |

Line post depths follow the NRCS Texas spec, which also puts brace assembly posts at least 3 feet down unless driven. The corner and gate depths are our working rules for this area. Treat them as a floor.

## How deep to set fence posts

The common rule is one third of the post's length in the ground. The [Quikrete Fast-Setting Concrete Mix data sheet](https://www.quikrete.com/pdfs/data_sheet-fast%20setting%20concrete%20mix%201004-50.pdf) gives the same rule. For an 8 foot corner post that is about 32 inches, and we would go deeper.

Load, soil and height push it deeper. Long barbed wire pulls, heavy gates and 8 foot game fence put more force on a post, a taller fence is a longer lever, and blackland clay moves most in the top few feet, so a deeper post sits more of its length in steadier ground.

Rock pulls the other way. West of the Brazos, around Crawford, Valley Mills and Clifton, limestone or caliche can show up a foot down. You will not get full depth there without a rock auger, and some posts get pinned into the rock rather than buried deep in it. See [setting posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/) for what works when the auger bounces.

## Hole size, shape and the bell-bottom footing

### Diameter

Quikrete suggests a hole about three times the post's diameter, and the NRCS Texas spec sets a 12 inch minimum for any concrete-backfilled post. For a 2 7/8 inch pipe corner, a 12 inch auger bit is the usual choice. A big gate post needs a wider hole to get a real wall of concrete around it.

### Shape

Straight-sided or wider at the bottom, never wider at the top. A mushroom cap, where the concrete spreads over the lip of the hole at grade, is the worst shape you can pour. Swelling clay gets a wide lip to push up on, and the flat top catches rain right at the post.

### Bell bottom

A bell-bottom footing flares the bottom of the hole a few inches wider than the shaft. Once the concrete sets, that wider foot has to drag a cone of soil up with it before the post can lift. It suits gate posts, corners in clay, and posts in low ground that want to float. The 2021 revision of the [Texas NRCS fence specification](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/29884/382_TX_PS_Fence_2021) suggests belling out the bottom of concrete-filled holes for exactly this reason. You can bell a hole with a clamshell digger or a digging bar after the auger comes out. It is slow, but a gate post set right only gets set once.

### The top of the pour

There are two correct finishes, depending on your soil:

1. **Crown it.** Fill to the surface and trowel the top into a small dome that sheds water away from the post. The NRCS Texas spec asks for concrete-filled holes to be crowned so water cannot pond at the base. That matters for steel as much as wood, because the ground line is where pipe rusts first.
2. **Stop short and cap with soil.** The same spec allows that on blackland soils the concrete may stop up to 12 inches below the surface, with soil backfilled on top. That keeps the concrete out of the zone where clay swells hardest.

Either way, no flat or dished concrete at grade for water to sit in.

## Dry pour or mixed?

Quikrete's fast-setting mix is sold to be poured dry into the hole and soaked. Its data sheet says to fill to within 3 or 4 inches of the top, add about a gallon of water per 50 pound bag, and on holes deeper than 30 inches, fill and soak in stages so water reaches the bottom. The last few inches get soil or sod.

That works for a small post in a 30 inch hole. In a 4 foot corner hole it is easy to leave a dry pocket near the bottom. For corners and gate posts, mix the concrete wet in a wheelbarrow or mixer and rod it with a bar as you pour to settle out the air.

### How many bags a hole takes

Quikrete publishes a yield of about 0.375 cubic feet per 50 pound bag of fast-setting mix, or 0.45 per 60 pound bag. Say you are setting a 2 7/8 inch pipe corner in a 12 inch hole, 48 inches deep:

- Hole: 3.14 x 0.5 ft x 0.5 ft x 4 ft = about 3.14 cubic feet
- Pipe takes up about 0.18, so you need about 2.96 cubic feet
- 2.96 / 0.375 = about 8 bags at 50 pounds, or about 7 at 60 pounds

On blackland with the pour stopped 12 inches down, that drops to about 6 bags at 50 pounds. A corner with a brace running each way is three posts, so a four-corner pasture means buying concrete by the pickup load.

## How long to let fence post concrete cure before you stretch wire

The bag says one thing and the wire says another. The Quikrete data sheet says the mix sets in 20 to 40 minutes and to wait 4 hours before the post takes any strain. That is fine for a mailbox or a yard fence panel. The same sheet lists typical compressive strength of about 1,000 psi at 24 hours, 2,500 psi at 7 days and 4,000 psi at 28 days.

At 24 hours the concrete has about a quarter of its 28-day strength. The safer order is to set corners and braces first, build the line posts, and stretch wire several days later, longer in cold weather when concrete gains strength more slowly. On a normal job the order of work gives you that time for free. Hang a heavy gate on a day-old post and you may be resetting it.

## Concrete or gravel for wood posts

For wood line posts, tamped native soil or crushed limestone base in thin lifts is almost always the better choice. The NRCS Texas spec describes dirt backfill as a hole at least 6 inches wider than the post, tamped in 4 to 6 inch lifts up to ground level. UGA's bulletin adds that packing the bottom 6 inches well matters most. Tamping in lifts is the step people skip.

A few inches of gravel at the bottom helps water drain away from the end grain. Crushed base with fines packs tighter than washed round gravel, which can roll and let a post shift.

## Fixing posts that were set in concrete wrong

Concrete poured wrong tends to fail three ways: wood posts snapped at the top of a collar that held water, corners in clay heaved by a shallow plug, and gate posts rocking in a cracked collar because the post was too light or too shallow for the gate.

Once a concreted post fails, the plug usually has to be dug, broken or jacked out before the post is set again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep should fence posts be set in concrete?

Start at one third of the post's total length and go deeper for anything carrying load. On a Central Texas ranch fence that usually means corner and brace posts at 3 to 4 feet and gate posts at about 4 feet. Line posts, which usually need no concrete, go about 2 feet. Rock may limit depth, and clay rewards going deeper.

### Should wood fence posts be set in concrete or gravel?

For wood line posts, tamped native soil or crushed limestone base in thin lifts is usually better. Concrete pulls away from a wood post over time, and the gap holds water at the ground line where rot starts. UGA Extension advises against concrete around wood posts. Wood corners in wet or weak ground are the exception, with the top crowned or finished below grade.

### How long should concrete cure before stretching fence wire?

Fast-setting mix is hard in under an hour, and the maker says a post can take strain after 4 hours. That covers light loads. Concrete reaches only about a quarter of its full strength in the first day, so set corners and braces first and wait several days before putting full wire tension on them. In cold weather, give it longer.

### Can you pour dry concrete mix straight into a post hole?

Fast-setting mixes are made to be poured dry and soaked, and that works on small posts in shallow holes. On holes deeper than about 30 inches, the maker says to fill and soak in stages so water reaches the bottom. For big corner and gate holes, mixing the concrete wet and rodding it gives a more reliable footing.

### Are concrete fence posts a good choice for ranch fencing?

Precast concrete posts show up mostly in residential and garden fencing and are rare on working ranch fence in Texas. They are heavy to haul down a fence line, and tying wire to them takes more work than stapling to wood or clipping to steel. Steel pipe in a concrete footing gives you the strength and weight at corners and gates without those drawbacks.
