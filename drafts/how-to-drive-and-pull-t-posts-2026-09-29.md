# How to Drive and Pull T-Posts: Driver, Depth, Pullers and Safety

A t-post is deep enough when the anchor plate is fully under the dirt, and on most Central Texas cattle fence that means about 24 inches in the ground. Use a manual t post driver for small jobs and a powered driver when you are setting hundreds. Either way, get the post plumb in the first few blows, because a lean you ignore at six inches is a lean you live with for twenty years.

Pulling is the reverse problem. A t post puller or a farm jack lifts the post straight up without bending it, a tractor does it faster, and dry blackland clay fights you on every one. Below: depth, driving straight, hard ground around Waco, pulling, and the safety habits that save your hearing and your eyes.

## How deep a t-post needs to go

The short rule comes from the University of Georgia bulletin [Fences for Horses](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/): in most soils, a studded steel post only has to be driven until the anchor plate is beneath the surface. The anchor plate is the flat plate fixed near the bottom. It keeps the post from pulling up when a cow leans on the wire or the wire lifts over a dip.

For a firmer number, the October 2015 edition of the Texas NRCS fence specification ([Code 382](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf), hosted by AgriLife Extension in Leon County) says steel line posts should be at least 6 feet long and set 24 inches in the ground. In rocky soil it allows a 5 1/2 foot post set 18 inches. It wants at least 2 inches of post above the top wire, and a minimum weight of 1.25 pounds per foot, with 1.33 pound posts preferred.

That gives you simple math for picking a length:

| Post length | Set depth | Above ground | Highest top wire (2 in. clearance) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 1/2 ft (66 in.) | 18 in. (rocky ground) | 48 in. | 46 in. |
| 6 ft (72 in.) | 24 in. | 48 in. | 46 in. |
| 6 1/2 ft (78 in.) | 24 in. | 54 in. | 52 in. |
| 7 ft (84 in.) | 24 in. | 60 in. | 58 in. |
| 8 ft (96 in.) | 24 in. | 72 in. | 70 in. |

A five-strand barbed wire fence with the top wire around 52 inches wants a 6 1/2 foot post. Net wire with a barb on top usually wants 7 feet. When unsure, go a size longer.

Clay is the reason to err deep here. The black gumbo east of the Brazos swells when wet and cracks when it dries, and a shallow post works loose in those cracks. Our guide to [setting fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers the heave problem. For driving, the fix is simple: bury the plate, then give it a few more inches.

## Driving t-posts straight

Most crooked fence is made in the first three blows.

### Set the line first

Build the corners and braces before anything else. They carry the wire tension; t-posts only hold the wire at height (the reasons are in [t-post vs wood post](/blog/t-post-vs-wood-post/)). Then pull a string between the braces a few inches off the ground and mark your spacing along it. Twelve to 15 feet between line posts is common for barbed wire on cattle, and our [fence post spacing](/blog/fence-post-spacing/) guide covers other wire types and animals.

### Face the studs the right way

The studs are the knobs down one face of the post. Clips hook against them, so the studs go on the side where the wire hangs. Most builders hang the wire on the side the stock pushes from, so a leaning cow presses the wire into the post instead of popping the clips. Pick that side before you start and turn every post the same way.

### Plumb it early, then drive

Stand the post on its mark, set the driver over the top, and give it two or three light taps to seat the point. Check it for plumb along the line and across it. Correct any lean now by pushing the post while you tap. Once it is 6 or 8 inches in, it will not move without a fight.

Then drive it home with full strokes. Keep the driver on the post the whole time. Lifting it clear of the top between strokes is how a driver comes down on a knuckle or the side of a head. Drive every post to the same reference, such as a paint mark a set distance from the tip, so the wire heights stay even. On rolling ground, keep the height even above the ground, not above a level line.

## Manual driver vs powered driver

A manual driver is a heavy steel tube with handles and a closed top. It is cheap to own, goes anywhere you can walk, and it is the right tool for repairs, short runs, and anything under a few hundred posts.

Powered drivers come in three kinds:

- **Gas-powered handheld drivers.** An engine does the pounding, but you still carry the tool to each post.
- **Pneumatic drivers.** Run off an air compressor on a truck or trailer. Fast and steady in soft to medium ground.
- **Hydraulic drivers on a tractor or skid steer.** Mounted on the three-point or loader and run off the machine's hydraulics. This is how posts get set by the mile.

The tradeoff with power is feel. With a manual driver you know the instant the post hits rock, because the driver bounces and your shoulders tell you. A hydraulic driver keeps hammering and folds the post over a ledge before you notice. Watch the post, not the controls, and stop as soon as the rate of sink changes.

## Hard ground: dry clay and rock

### Blackland clay in August

In a wet spring, t-posts slide into blackland clay. In August the same ground rings like concrete. Drive clay after a rain if you can. If the job cannot wait, soaking each spot with a few gallons of water the evening before helps more than you would expect. Do not beat a post into dry clay until the top mushrooms. A flattened top is hard to cap.

### Limestone and caliche

West of the Brazos and up into Bosque County, the soil gets thin fast over limestone and caliche. You hit rock at 8 inches in one spot and drive a full 24 a few feet down the line. What works:

- **Slide along the line.** If a post stops short on rock, pull it and try a few inches either way. Spacing does not need to be exact to the inch.
- **Make a pilot hole.** A rock bar, or a drill with a rock bit, can open a path through a caliche layer.
- **Use the rocky-ground allowance.** The 2015 NRCS spec allows a 5 1/2 foot post set 18 inches in rocky soil. If the plate is buried and the post will not go further, that is a fair place to stop.
- **Stop before it bends.** A t-post kinked over a ledge never stands straight again, and the bend is a weak point for good.

For when a steel post is the wrong post altogether, see [setting fence posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/).

## How to pull t-posts

Pulling is where most people bend good posts. Lift straight up, along the post's own line, with steady force. Any sideways pull bends the post above ground and leaves the plate stuck below.

### Take the load off the wire first

If the post is in a live fence, release the tension before you pull. Remove the clips, and if the wire is tight, back off the stretcher or strainer. A post that comes free while tight wire is still clipped to it can whip the wire at you.

### Lever-type t-post puller

This kind of t post puller is a long handle with a jaw that grabs the studs just above the ground. The foot rests on the ground as a fulcrum, you push the handle, and the post rises a few inches. Regrip lower and repeat. For a handful of posts, nothing beats it.

### Farm jack and chain

A tall farm jack handles stubborn posts. Wrap a chain around the post just above the ground, a couple of turns so it bites into the studs, hook it to the jack nose, and jack straight up. Some jacks take a post-pulling attachment that clamps the post directly. Keep your head out of the handle's path. A handle that slips under load comes up hard.

### Tractor or skid steer

For a whole fence line, a chain on a loader bucket or three-point hitch pulls a post in seconds. Choke the chain low and lift straight up. Do not drag the post sideways with the tractor moving; that bends it and can snap the chain. Keep everyone clear of a loaded chain.

### When a post will not come

Rock it side to side a few times to break the soil's grip on the plate. In dry clay, pour water around the post and give it time to soak. If the fence does not need to be gone this week, pull it after a good rain. And if the plate is hung under a rock shelf or a root, a few minutes with a sharpshooter beats snapping the post off.

## Safety habits that matter

- **Hearing.** Steel on steel is loud, right next to your ears, for hours. Wear earplugs or muffs, especially with a gas or pneumatic driver.
- **Eyes.** Chips of paint, zinc and rust fly off the post top with every blow. Safety glasses, every post.
- **Hands.** Wear gloves, and never hold a post for someone else while they drive it. Start it with light taps yourself.
- **Overhead lines.** A long post with a driver on top puts steel well above your head. Look up before driving near road power lines or a service drop to a barn, and keep loaders clear of them.
- **Buried lines.** Texas Utilities Code [Chapter 251](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/UT/htm/UT.251.htm) requires anyone who excavates with powered equipment to 16 inches or deeper to notify a notification center (the 811 system) at least 48 hours ahead, not counting weekends and legal holidays. It exempts activities on private property in connection with agricultural operations, so on a working place the call may not be legally required. A pipeline crossing your pasture does not care about the exemption. Calling 811 before powered driving near a pipeline marker, easement or buried service is free.
- **Horses.** The Penn State guide [Fence Planning for Horses](https://extension.psu.edu/fence-planning-for-horses) warns that steel t-posts carry a serious impalement risk and says capped posts may be used cautiously only in very large pastures where contact is rare. Keep t-posts out of small horse pens altogether, and anywhere horses share a t-post line, cap every post.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep should a t-post be driven?
At minimum, deep enough to bury the anchor plate completely. The 2015 Texas NRCS fence specification calls for steel line posts at least 6 feet long set 24 inches in the ground, or a 5 1/2 foot post set 18 inches in rocky soil. In blackland clay, which swells and shrinks, a few extra inches keep the post from working loose in drought cracks.

### What is the easiest way to pull t-posts?
For a few posts, a lever-type t-post puller that grips the studs. For stubborn ones, a farm jack with a chain wrapped low on the post. For a whole fence line, a chain on a tractor loader lifts each one in seconds. Whatever you use, release the wire tension first, pull straight up, and soak dry clay around the post before you start.

### Can I drive a t-post with a sledgehammer?
You can, but it is slower and harder to keep straight. A sledge tends to glance off the narrow top, mushroom it, and knock the post out of plumb. A manual t-post driver slides over the post and keeps every blow centered. For more than a few posts, the driver is the better tool.

### What do I do when a t-post hits rock?
Stop before the post bends. Pull it and try a few inches along the line, or open a pilot hole with a rock bar or a drill and rock bit. The 2015 Texas NRCS specification allows a 5 1/2 foot post set 18 inches deep in rocky soil, so if the anchor plate is buried and the post will not go further, that is usually an acceptable place to stop.

### Can old t-posts be reused?
Yes, if they are straight, the anchor plate is still attached, and the steel is not rusted thin at the old ground line. Reused posts work well on interior cross fences. Bent posts rarely drive true again after straightening, so they are better sent to scrap than set on a perimeter line.
