# Does Insurance Cover Fence Damage? Homeowners vs Farm and Ranch Policies in Texas

Often, yes, but only when two things line up: the fence is property your policy actually covers, and the damage came from a cause the policy names. Wind, hail, fire and a tree falling across the wire are the usual covered causes. Flood, the weight of ice, rot, rust, hogs and plain old age usually are not. And on a ranch, the pasture fence out on the back of the place is the piece most likely to fall through the cracks of a policy.

A homeowners policy covers fence as an "other structure" with a limit tied to the house. A farm and ranch policy may cover your corrals and pens and still leave field and pasture fence uncovered unless it was listed with its own limit. The policy wording decides every one of these questions, so read this as a map of what to check, not a promise of what you will be paid.

## Fence damage coverage by cause

Here is how the common causes tend to play out. Your declarations page and policy form are what count.

| Cause of damage | Homeowners policy | Farm and ranch policy | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind (straight-line, tornado) | Usually covered if you carry wind coverage | Covered only if that fence is covered property | Separate wind and hail deductible |
| Hail | Usually covered with wind and hail coverage | Same as wind | Same deductible question |
| Tree or limb falling on the fence | Often covered as a falling object | Depends on the form, and the fence must be covered | Tree removal coverage is limited |
| Grass fire | Usually covered under other structures | Usually covered, if the fence is covered | Limit on fence or other structures |
| Flood, creek rise, washed-out water gap | Not covered; home policies exclude flood | Usually excluded as flood or surface water | Possible USDA help, see below |
| Weight of ice or snow | Most policies do not pay | Often excluded for fences | Ice storm damage is mostly on you |
| Car or truck through the fence | Look first to the driver's liability insurance | Forms commonly exclude it if the vehicle is yours or a household member's | Crash report and the driver's insurer |
| Rot, rust, sag, lean, wear | Generally not covered | Generally not covered | Maintenance, not a sudden loss |
| Hogs, deer, your own livestock | Rarely covered | Rarely covered | Animal damage exclusions |

## Homeowners insurance: fence counts as "other structures"

On a standard home policy, a fence sits under other structures coverage, the same bucket as a detached garage, a shop or a storage shed. The [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/home-insurance-structures-covered.html) says most policies set that coverage at 10% of the house's insured value, so $300,000 on the house gives you $30,000 for everything else standing on the place. TDI also says that if wind damaged your fence and you have wind coverage, the policy will probably pay, and that most Texas home policies away from the coast include wind and hail.

Three things shrink that check.

**Actual cash value.** TDI's [storm claim FAQ](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/consumer/storms/home-damaged-faq.html) says fence coverage is usually limited to actual cash value, meaning the company deducts depreciation. Fifteen-year-old barbed wire on cedar posts does not get paid like new wire on new posts.

**The deductible.** You pay it before the company pays anything, and many policies carry a different deductible for wind and hail than for other causes.

**Business use.** TDI notes that if a structure on your property is used to run a business, you should ask if you need a separate policy or extra coverage. A cattle operation is a business, so do not assume a homeowners policy treats pasture fence like a backyard privacy fence.

Here is how the math can land. Say you have 20 acres outside Lorena and a storm flattens a quarter mile of 5-strand barbed wire. At our published range of $2.50 to $5.50 per foot, 1,320 feet runs $3,300 to $7,260 to rebuild. Now take a worked example with made-up numbers: the rebuild comes to $6,000, the adjuster depreciates the old fence by a third, and your wind deductible is $3,000. That leaves $4,000 of actual cash value, minus $3,000, for a check near $1,000. The $6,000, the one-third and the $3,000 are invented for illustration, not a quote and not typical policy terms, but the shape is real: on older fence with a big deductible, the claim can be small.

## Farm and ranch policies: where pasture fence falls through

TDI's [home insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb025.html) describes farm and ranch insurance as coverage for homes outside city limits on land used for farming and raising livestock. It is the better fit for most working places in McLennan, Bosque and Falls counties. But a farm policy is several coverages stacked together, and fence can land in different ones.

A common arrangement in farm property forms goes like this. Corrals, pens, chutes, feed racks and other fences, such as the yard fence, fall under farm structures coverage. Field and pasture fences are specifically listed as property that coverage does not include. To insure the miles of perimeter and cross fence, the fence has to be described and given its own limit on the declarations, or added by endorsement.

Some forms add two more wrinkles worth asking about:

- **Insurance to value.** Some property forms carry a coinsurance or insurance-to-value clause. If the limit you carry is well below what your fence would cost to replace, a partial loss may be paid only in part. Ask whether yours has one, and set the fence limit off real footage, not a guess.
- **Peril limits.** Vehicle coverage commonly leaves out fence damage caused by a vehicle you or someone in your household owns or drives, and special-form farm policies commonly exclude fences from damage caused by freezing or by the weight or pressure of ice and water.

Livestock coverage is a separate line. It insures the animals, not the wire, and it will not rebuild a fence. The question of who pays when cattle get through a downed fence and onto a road is a liability question, which we cover in [cattle on the road and liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/).

Before the next storm season, sit down with your agent and get these answered in writing:

1. Is field and pasture fence covered property on my policy, and at what limit?
2. Are corrals, working pens, pipe fence and my entrance gate covered, and under which coverage?
3. Is fence paid at actual cash value or replacement cost?
4. Which causes of loss apply to fence: wind, hail, fire, falling trees, vehicles?
5. What is my wind and hail deductible, in dollars?
6. Is there any coverage to remove a fallen tree from a fence line?

## What insurance usually will not pay for

**Flood and washed-out water gaps.** TDI is plain that home policies do not cover floods. When a creek comes up and takes the [water gap](/blog/water-gap-fence/) and 200 feet of fence on either side, that is flood damage. Standard flood policies are written around buildings and their contents and generally do not cover fences either.

**Ice storms.** TDI says most policies do not pay for fence damage caused by ice or snow unless your house collapses on the fence.

**Age, rust and rot.** Insurance pays for sudden damage. A fence that sagged over ten summers, posts that heaved in blackland clay, or wire that rusted through is maintenance. If the storm simply finished off a fence that was already failing, expect an argument about how much was storm and how much was age. Our guide on [rusted barbed wire](/blog/rusted-barbed-wire-repair-or-replace/) covers how to judge where your wire stands.

**Animals.** Hogs rooting under the bottom wire and deer hitting a net wire fence every night are chronic damage, not a covered event. The fix is a better fence, not a claim. See [feral hog fence damage](/blog/feral-hog-fence-damage/).

### When insurance says no: USDA disaster help for fence

For working ag land, there is one more place to look. The USDA Farm Service Agency's [Emergency Conservation Program](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/resources/disaster-recovery/emergency-conservation-program-ecp) lists restoring permanent fences among its covered practices after natural disasters. It pays cost-share of up to 75% of the allowable cost, and up to 90% for limited resource producers. Applications are taken during signup periods announced after a natural disaster, the damage has to be serious enough to impair the land's farming use, funding depends on what Congress appropriates, and the application includes an assessment of the damage. That last part matters: call your county FSA office before you tear everything out, so there is still damage to assess.

## Documenting fence damage so the claim holds up

Our post on [storm damaged fence repair](/blog/storm-damaged-fence-repair/) covers the first day after a storm: finding stock, walking the line and getting a temporary patch up. For the claim itself, the adjuster needs a clear record, and TDI's advice is to take pictures of the damage before doing any work.

Build the record like this:

- **Wide photos** that show the whole damaged run and what caused it, such as the tree still lying on the wire.
- **Close photos** of snapped posts, broken strands, pulled staples, bent T-posts and any damaged corner or H-brace. Put a tape measure or a fence post in the frame for scale.
- **Measured footage.** Walk it with a measuring wheel or a GPS app and write down feet of damaged fence by type: barbed wire, net wire, pipe.
- **Counts.** Posts broken, posts leaning, strands cut, braces moved, gates bent.
- **Age and condition.** Actual cash value turns on this. Dig up the invoice or at least the year the fence was built, and note the wire gauge and post type if you know them.
- **Receipts** for anything you buy to hold stock in the meantime, such as panels, T-posts and wire.

TDI says it is generally fine to make temporary repairs, but not to make permanent repairs until you have talked to your insurance company. Keeping cattle in is a temporary repair. Rebuilding the whole quarter mile is not.

Texas law also sets deadlines on the company's side. Per the TDI guide, your insurer must acknowledge the claim within 15 days, accept or deny it within 15 business days of getting all the information it needs, and pay within five business days of agreeing to pay. It can take up to 45 days if it tells you why, and deadlines may be longer after major disasters.

## Getting a written repair estimate

The adjuster writes an estimate, and the company bases its payment on it. TDI notes that if the damage turns out worse than the adjuster first thought, you or your contractor can talk to the adjuster about raising the estimate. A written estimate from the person who will actually do the work gives that conversation real numbers.

A useful fence estimate separates the job into pieces the adjuster can check against the photos. Our published repair ranges look like this:

| Repair item | Brazos Ridge range |
|---|---|
| Splice and retension a run | $400 to $900 |
| Reset or replace posts | $45 to $120 per post |
| Rebuild a corner or H-brace | $600 to $1,400 |
| Storm or tree clearing plus repair | $900 to $2,500 |
| Rebuild a washed-out water gap | $700 to $1,800 |

So a storm that snaps 12 line posts runs 12 times $45 to 12 times $120, or $540 to $1,440, for the posts alone, before any wire or bracing. When the damage is long enough that a rebuild beats patching, the per-foot numbers on our [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page apply instead.

One warning. In Texas it is illegal for a contractor to waive your deductible, pay it for you, or pad an estimate to cover it, according to [TDI](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/can-a-contractor-waive-my-deductible.html). The company can ask for proof you paid it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does homeowners insurance cover fence damage from a storm?
Usually, if the policy includes wind and hail coverage. The Texas Department of Insurance says most home policies away from the coast do, and that fence claims are usually paid at actual cash value, minus your deductible. Ice and snow damage is usually not covered. Check your declarations page for the other structures limit and the wind deductible.

### Does a farm and ranch policy cover pasture fence?
Not automatically. Many farm property forms cover corrals, pens and chutes but list field and pasture fences as property not covered unless they are described and given their own limit on the declarations, or added by endorsement. Ask your agent whether your pasture and perimeter fence is covered property, at what limit, and for which causes of loss, and get the answer in writing.

### Will insurance pay replacement cost or actual cash value for a fence?
Most often actual cash value, which is the cost to replace minus depreciation for age and condition. That is why the age of the fence matters so much to the check. Ask your agent whether replacement cost on fence is available on your policy and what it would add to the premium. If your fence is old barbed wire on cedar posts, expect a meaningful depreciation deduction before the deductible comes off.

### Who pays when a car or truck runs through my ranch fence?
Start with the driver's auto liability insurance. Get the crash report information from the officer at the scene, photograph the damage and the vehicle marks, and patch the gap so cattle do not get out. Your own policy may help if the fence is covered property; the common vehicle exclusion for fences applies when the vehicle is yours or a household member's. See [cattle on the road and liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/) if stock got out.

### Does insurance cover fence damage from a flood?
Generally no. Texas home policies do not cover flood, farm policies usually exclude flood and surface water, and flood policies are built around buildings rather than fences. For working ag land, the USDA Farm Service Agency's Emergency Conservation Program can cost-share the restoration of permanent fences when a signup opens after a natural disaster, so call your county FSA office before rebuilding.
