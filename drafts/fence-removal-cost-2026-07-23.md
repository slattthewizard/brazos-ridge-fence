# Old Fence Removal Cost and What Tear-Out Actually Involves

That rusted fence line you've been ignoring for ten years? Pulling it out will probably run you $1 to $5 per foot, and on a ranch that adds up faster than most folks expect. A half mile of old barbed wire tear-out can cost $3,000 or more before a single new post goes in the ground.

Here's the thing most landowners around Waco don't realize. Fence removal cost isn't just about yanking wire off posts. It's cutting fence loose from twenty years of embedded brush, pulling posts set in concrete or clay, rolling up wire that wants to slice your forearms open, and hauling everything to a place that'll legally take it. Skip any of those steps and you've got a hidden hazard sitting in your pasture waiting to lame a horse or shred a tractor tire.

This guide breaks down what tear-out really costs in McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties, what the work actually involves, and where you can save money without creating a bigger problem.

## What Tear-Out Actually Involves, Step by Step

Folks picture fence removal as pulling a few staples and rolling up wire. On a fence that's been standing since the 80s, it's a different animal. Here's the real sequence.

**1. Walk the line and flag problems.** Before anything gets cut, you find the gates, the water gaps, the spots where the neighbor's fence ties in, and any wire that's disappeared into tree trunks. Trees grow around wire. Cutting it wrong can send tension snapping back at you.

**2. Clear the brush.** Most old fence lines in Central Texas are buried in mesquite, cedar, and greenbriar. You can't safely remove wire you can't see. On overgrown lines, brush clearing is often a third of the total bill.

**3. De-tension and cut the wire.** Old barbed wire holds tension even when it looks slack. Pros cut at the posts in sections, never in the middle of a long tight run.

**4. Roll or spool the wire.** Loose piles of barbed wire are how livestock get cut and tires get ruined. Wire gets rolled tight, tied off, and staged for haul-off.

**5. Pull the posts.** T-posts come out with a jack or a tractor loop. Wood posts set in clay or concrete are the fight. Rotten ones snap at ground level, and then you're digging out the stub or grinding it below grade.

**6. Sweep for debris.** Staples, clips, broken wire ends, and post stubs get picked up. A good crew runs a magnet bar over high-traffic areas. This step is what separates a clean tear-out from a future vet bill.

**7. Haul off and dispose.** Wire, posts, and concrete go to a metal recycler or an approved disposal site. More on that below, because it's where a lot of DIY jobs go sideways.

## What Drives Fence Removal Costs Up or Down

Two fences of the same length can cost wildly different amounts to remove. These are the factors that move the number.

**Brush and access.** A clean fence line a tractor can drive along is cheap. A line swallowed by ten years of mesquite, or one running through a creek bottom no machine can reach, can double or triple the price. If your line is overgrown, clearing it first changes everything, and we covered how in our guide to [clearing a fence line of brush](/blog/clearing-fence-line-brush/).

**Post material and how they're set.** T-posts pull in seconds. Wood posts in concrete take real time. Pipe posts set deep in concrete usually get torched off at ground level because pulling the footing costs more than it's worth.

**Post condition.** Ironically, badly rotted posts can cost more to remove than solid ones, because they break off instead of pulling clean. If your posts are crumbling at the ground line, that's rot doing its work, and our post on [fence post rot](/blog/fence-post-rot/) explains why it happens so fast in our soils.

**Wire condition.** Rusted, brittle wire snaps constantly during removal, which slows the rolling and multiplies the sharp ends on the ground. If you're on the fence about whether your old wire is worth saving, read our breakdown on [rusted barbed wire, repair or replace](/blog/rusted-barbed-wire-repair-or-replace/).

**Terrain and soil.** Rocky ground around Valley Mills and Crawford makes digging out stubs slow. Blackland clay near Lorena and West grips wood posts like concrete when it's dry.

**Haul-off distance and volume.** Steel is heavy and treated wood can't just go anywhere. Distance to the recycler or landfill is baked into every quote.

## Disposal, Haul-Off, and What You Can't Just Dump

This is the part of fence removal cost that surprises people most. Getting the fence out of the ground is half the job. Getting it off your property legally and safely is the other half.

**Steel wire and T-posts** go to a metal recycler. Waco has several scrap yards that take fencing steel, and on big pipe jobs the scrap value offsets some of your haul-off cost. Don't expect to get rich. Rusty barbed wire fetches shredder-grade prices, but it beats paying to landfill it.

**Treated wood posts** are the tricky one. Most treated posts from older fences contain preservatives like creosote or CCA, and they should never be burned. Burning treated wood releases toxic compounds, and it's prohibited under Texas outdoor burning rules. The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality spells out what you can and can't burn on your own land at [tceq.texas.gov](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/), and treated lumber is firmly on the can't list. Treated posts go to the landfill.

**Concrete footings** can go to a construction and demolition landfill, or honestly, a lot of ranchers keep them for filling washouts and erosion cuts. Free riprap.

**Old wire left in piles** is the worst outcome. Buried or piled wire injures livestock, wrecks hay equipment, and tangles shredders for decades. If a previous owner left wire piles on your place, budget to clean them up. It's cheaper than one colic surgery or a new baler pickup.

## Should You Tear Out a Fence Yourself?

Plenty of ranchers pull their own fence, and for the right fence it makes sense. A quarter mile of loose 3-strand on T-posts across open pasture? If you've got a tractor, a T-post jack, gloves, eye protection, and a free Saturday, have at it. You'll save $500 to $1,000.

But know where the line is. Call a professional when:

- The wire is under tension you can't read, especially high-tensile or wire grown into trees
- Posts are set in concrete or the line runs through rock
- The line is heavily overgrown and you'd be cutting wire blind inside brush
- There's a slope, creek crossing, or water gap involved
- It's a boundary fence shared with a neighbor, where cutting the wrong wire creates a legal mess and loose cattle
- You're removing pipe fence, because torch work and multi-ton steel handling aren't weekend jobs

Old wire under tension can whip back with enough force to take an eye. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension publishes solid guidance on fence construction and wire handling at [agrilifeextension.tamu.edu](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/), and their consistent message applies to tear-out too: respect the stored energy in stretched wire.

There's also a middle path. Some of our customers clear the brush and pull the T-posts themselves, then hire us for the tensioned wire, the concrete-set corners, and the haul-off. That hybrid approach usually trims 30 to 40 percent off the removal bill.

One more reason to get a pro's eyes on it before you start: sometimes the fence doesn't need to come out at all. Solid posts with tired wire can often be re-wired for far less than full replacement. And if you are replacing, knowing your rebuild number first matters, because tear-out pricing changes when it's bundled. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to remove 1,000 feet of barbed wire fence?

Plan on $1,000 to $2,750 for a standalone job, depending on post type, brush, and terrain. T-posts on clear ground land at the low end, wood posts in brushy or rocky country at the high end. If you're rebuilding the same line, bundled tear-out often drops to $500 to $1,500.

### Is old barbed wire worth anything as scrap?

A little, but not much. Rusty barbed wire sells as shredder-grade scrap, often just a few cents per pound, and a thousand feet of 5-strand only weighs a few hundred pounds. Pipe fence is the exception, since heavy steel pipe can return $100 or more per ton and meaningfully offset haul-off costs.

### Can I burn old treated fence posts on my property?

No. Treated posts contain preservatives like creosote or CCA that release toxic smoke and ash when burned, and Texas outdoor burning rules prohibit burning treated wood. Haul them to a landfill that accepts treated lumber. Untreated cedar posts can usually go in a legal burn pile, but check current TCEQ rules and county burn bans first.

### Does fence removal cost less if I'm installing a new fence?

Almost always, yes. The crew and equipment are already on site, so most contractors fold tear-out into the new build at $0.50 to $1.50 per foot instead of $1 to $5 standalone. Always ask for the quote both ways so you can see exactly what the removal portion costs you.
