# Who Pays for a Fence Between Neighbors in Texas?

Here's the answer most landowners don't want to hear: in Texas, nobody has to pay for that fence. Not you, and not the fella on the other side of the line.

The question of who pays for a fence between neighbors in Texas comes up on almost every boundary job we quote around Waco, and the honest answer surprises people. Texas has no statute that forces one landowner to chip in on a fence the other one builds. Some states have "fence viewer" laws that split costs down the middle by force of law. Texas isn't one of them.

That doesn't mean you're stuck paying the whole bill every time. Plenty of neighbors in McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties split fence costs fairly, and there are smart ways to make that happen. There are also a few situations where the law does put money on the other side of the table. We'll walk through all of it, with real numbers from real jobs.

## The Short Answer on Who Pays for a Fence Between Neighbors in Texas

Under Texas law, a boundary fence is treated like any other improvement. If you build it, you pay for it. Your neighbor has no legal duty to contribute, even if the fence sits right on the property line and even if his cattle benefit from it every single day.

The flip side is just as true. If your neighbor builds a fence on the line, he can't send you a bill for half and take you to court when you don't pay. There's no Texas statute that lets him do that.

The relevant law lives in [Chapter 143 of the Texas Agriculture Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/AG/htm/AG.143.htm), which covers fences and range restrictions. Read it front to back and you won't find a single line requiring cost sharing between neighbors. What you will find is a rule that matters a lot in practice: it's illegal to attach your fence to your neighbor's fence without their written permission. More on that below, because it's the closest thing Texas has to a "you use it, you pay for it" rule.

So the default answer is simple. The person who wants the fence pays for the fence. Everything beyond that comes down to agreements, and that's where most of the good and bad neighbor stories start.

## What Texas Law Actually Says About Boundary Fences

A few pieces of Texas law touch boundary fences, and it helps to know each one before you knock on your neighbor's door.

**Open range is still the default.** Texas is an open range state at the state level, which means livestock owners generally aren't required to fence animals in. Most counties, including McLennan and the counties around it, have passed local stock laws that flip that rule and require owners to keep livestock off roads and off other people's land. We break down how that works in our post on the [Texas open range law](/blog/texas-open-range-law/), because it changes who's liable when cattle get where they shouldn't be.

**No forced cost sharing.** As covered above, no Texas statute makes a neighbor pay for a boundary fence he didn't ask for. Courts have held this position for well over a century.

**No attaching without permission.** Agriculture Code Section 143.121 makes it unlawful to attach a fence to someone else's fence without written consent. If your neighbor ties his cross fence into your line fence without asking, you can require him to take it down. In practice, this is your leverage. A neighbor who wants to use your fence has a reason to help pay for it.

**Fences and property lines are separate questions.** A fence is not automatically the legal boundary. Old fences wander off the line all the time, and a fence built in the wrong spot for long enough can create adverse possession headaches. Before you spend money on a line fence, know where the line actually is. Our article on [building a fence on the property line in Texas](/blog/fence-on-property-line-texas/) covers surveys, setbacks, and why we sometimes build a foot inside the line on purpose.

**HOAs and deed restrictions are a different animal.** If your land sits in a subdivision with deed restrictions, those documents can require fences, dictate materials, and sometimes address cost sharing. Rural acreage around Waco usually isn't restricted, but small ranchette subdivisions near Lorena and China Spring often are. Read your deed before you build.

## When Your Neighbor Might Actually Owe You Money

There's no automatic cost split, but a few situations do put real money on your neighbor's side of the ledger.

**A written agreement exists.** If you and your neighbor sign an agreement to share fence costs, that contract is enforceable like any other. Some older deeds in Central Texas even carry fence maintenance covenants that run with the land. Check your title documents.

**Your neighbor damaged the fence.** If his bull went through your fence, his dozer operator clipped a corner post, or his hunting lease guests drove through a water gap, he's liable for the damage he caused. That's basic negligence law, not fence law. Document it with photos and dates before anything gets repaired.

**His livestock are using your fence in a stock law county.** In a county with a local stock law, your neighbor has a duty to keep his cattle contained. If the only thing containing them is your fence, he doesn't owe you rent, but he does carry the liability if they get out through a gap on his side. Our post on [what happens when cattle get out](/blog/cattle-getting-out-of-fence/) explains why that liability motivates a lot of neighbors to pay their half without a fight.

**He attached to your fence.** If he tied into your fence without written permission, you can demand removal. Most neighbors would rather write a check for a share of the fence than tear out their own cross fencing. It's not a court-ordered split, but it gets to the same place.

## How to Split Fence Costs Between Neighbors the Right Way

Most boundary fences in our area do end up cost shared, not because the law requires it but because it's fair and everybody knows it. Here's the process that keeps it friendly.

**Talk before you build.** Nobody likes finding out about a fence project from the sound of a skid steer. Walk the line together, talk about what the fence needs to hold, and find out what matters to each side. One of you runs goats and needs net wire. The other just wants the line marked. That conversation shapes the price.

**Get a survey if there's any doubt.** A boundary survey on rural acreage typically runs $500 to $1,500 around here. That's cheap insurance against building a $15,000 fence on the wrong dirt.

**Get the quote in writing, then split it in writing.** A one-page agreement that names the fence line, the total cost, each party's share, and who handles future maintenance will prevent 90 percent of fence feuds. Sign two copies. You don't need a lawyer for this, though it never hurts to have one look it over.

**Splits don't have to be 50/50.** Common arrangements we see: one party pays for materials and the other pays labor, one party pays extra to upgrade from barbed wire to net wire because they're the one running sheep, or one party covers the brush clearing because the overgrowth crept in from their side. Fair beats equal.

**Put maintenance in the deal.** The old Central Texas custom is the "right hand rule": stand at the middle of the shared line facing the fence, and you maintain the half to your right. It's not law, but it's a handy convention worth writing into your agreement.

Robert Kalich and his neighbor west of West did it about as well as it can be done. They shared 2,600 feet of line where an old fence had rotted out. Robert wanted net wire for his Boer goats, the neighbor only needed barbed wire for cows. Total for the net wire build came to $15,600. They split the barbed wire equivalent 50/50 at $5,200 each, and Robert paid the $5,200 net wire upgrade himself. Both signed a one-page maintenance agreement. Five years from now there won't be an argument, because it's all on paper.

## When One Neighbor Refuses to Pay

Sometimes the conversation goes nowhere. The neighbor doesn't run livestock, doesn't care about the line, or just doesn't want to spend the money. In Texas, you can't force him. So here's how to protect yourself when you're building alone.

**Build it entirely on your side.** Set the fence 6 to 12 inches inside your line. The fence is then 100 percent your property. Your neighbor can't attach to it without written permission, can't claim any ownership of it, and if he ever wants to tie in, you're holding all the cards for a buy-in conversation.

**Document everything.** Keep the survey, the invoice, and dated photos of the completed fence. If a cost dispute or a boundary question ever surfaces, paper wins.

**Don't touch his side.** Clearing brush or old wire on your neighbor's side of the line without permission can create trespass liability, even with good intentions. Clear your side and stop at the line.

**Skip the retaliation moves.** We've been called out to look at fences built out of spite, and they never end well. A fence built in anger gets built fast and cheap, and it fails fast too.

Marcy Templin in Lorena ran into the refusal problem last fall. Her 22 acres shared 1,900 feet with an absentee owner who wouldn't return calls, and her horses kept testing the collapsing old wire. She stopped waiting. We built 1,900 feet of net wire with a top rail, set a foot inside her surveyed line, for $12,350. It's her fence, on her land, under her control. When the absentee owner eventually sells, the new buyer will have to come to her if they want to use it.

One safety note that matters here. Tearing out an old shared fence is where DIY boundary projects go sideways. Rusted wire under tension can snap and whip, old corner posts hide rot, and pulling wire near a disputed line can turn a cold disagreement hot. If the old fence is in bad shape or the line is contested, stop and bring in a professional. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I legally force my neighbor to pay half for a fence in Texas?

No. Texas has no statute that requires a landowner to share the cost of a boundary fence, so courts won't order your neighbor to contribute to a fence he never agreed to. Your options are a voluntary written agreement or building the fence yourself on your own side of the line. If he later attaches to your fence, you can require written permission, which is your best leverage for a buy-in.

### Can my neighbor attach his fence to mine?

Not without your written permission. Texas Agriculture Code Section 143.121 makes it unlawful to attach a fence to someone else's fence without consent, and you can require unauthorized attachments to be removed. Many landowners trade that permission for a share of the fence cost or a signed maintenance agreement. Get any deal like that in writing.

### Who pays when my neighbor's cattle damage the boundary fence?

If his livestock or equipment caused the damage, he's responsible for it under ordinary negligence rules. Take dated photos before you repair anything and send him a written repair estimate. Most disputes settle once there's a clear number on paper, and if he won't pay, small claims court handles amounts up to $20,000 in Texas.

### Does the fence on the line belong to both of us?

Only if you both paid for it or agreed it's shared. A fence built and paid for by one owner belongs to that owner, even if it sits on the boundary. That's why we recommend building 6 to 12 inches inside your own line when you're paying alone, so ownership stays clean and there's no argument later.
