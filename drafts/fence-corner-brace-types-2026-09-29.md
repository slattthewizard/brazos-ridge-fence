# Fence Corner Brace Types: Which Brace Fits Your Fence Load

A fence corner brace is the assembly that holds all the wire tension at a corner, an end, or a gate so the rest of the fence can stay tight. (If you searched "corner brace" and got shelf brackets, this is the other kind.) Ranch fences use six brace families: the single H, the double H, the N or diagonal-rail brace, the floating brace, the welded pipe corner, and the dead-man. They are not interchangeable. Each suits a different amount of wire, length of pull, and kind of dirt.

The short version: a single H handles five or six strands of barbed wire on a normal pull in firm soil. Net wire, game fence, long pulls, and soft or wet ground call for a double H or welded pipe. Floating braces and dead-man anchors earn their place where you want to dig only one hole, which around here usually means rock, as long as there is firm soil for the foot or anchor to bear on.

## What a corner brace is actually holding

The British Columbia Ministry of Agriculture's factsheet on [how brace assemblies work](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/agriculture-and-seafood/farm-management/structures-and-mechanization/300-series/307220-1_brace_assemblies_for_wire_fences.pdf) puts the normal pull from tensioned fence wire at 1,000 to 1,500 pounds, rising to as much as 2,500 pounds when cold weather shrinks the wire. A cow leaning on the fence or a limb across it adds a shock load on top.

That factsheet lists three ways a brace fails: a post or rail breaks, the soil in front of the post shears and lets it move, or the end post pulls straight up out of the ground. The third one explains most of what follows. When the diagonal in a brace runs steep, part of the fence load turns into an upward pull on the corner post. Short braces with steep diagonals lift their own corner post. Longer braces with a flatter diagonal push the load sideways into the soil, where it belongs.

## Brace types at a glance

| Brace type | Holes to dig | Best fit | Weak spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single H (two-post, horizontal rail) | 2 | Up to about 6 strands, normal pull, firm soil | Span too short, rail set too low |
| Double H (three-post) | 3 | Net wire, game fence, long pulls, poor or wet soil | Posts out of line, wire tied to the wrong post |
| N-brace (two-post, diagonal rail) | 2 | Up to about 6 strands, pull from one direction | Rail joints slip, more uplift on the corner post |
| Floating brace (angle or "Kiwi" brace) | 1 | Rock, light to moderate pulls | Foot block settling or sliding |
| Welded pipe H or corner | 2 or 3 | Pipe fence, gates, pens, heavy pulls | Poor welds, shallow setting |
| Dead-man anchor | 1 plus anchor | One-hole corners and ends in firm soil | Soft or wet soil lets the anchor creep |

## Single H-brace: the default for barbed wire

The single H is two posts about eight feet apart, a horizontal rail pinned between them, and a diagonal brace wire twisted tight from low on the corner post to high on the brace post. BC calls this the standard brace for fences of six wires or less in suitable soils, and reports test loads of 3,000 to 6,000 pounds for two-post braces in favorable conditions. That covers a typical five-strand barbed wire fence on firm ground.

We already have a full walk-through on [building an H brace](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/), so the steps are not repeated here. The two things that matter when choosing it are span and rail height. BC's rule of thumb is a rail about twice the fence height, set at about three quarters of the fence height: on a 4-foot fence, an 8-foot span with the rail about 3 feet up. The Texas NRCS fence specification says the horizontal member goes no lower than two thirds of the top wire height.

## Double H-brace: when one panel is not enough

A double H is a three-post brace: corner post, middle post, and back post, with two rails and two brace wires. BC puts three-post braces at almost double the capacity of a two-post brace. Use one when:

- **You are hanging net wire.** Every horizontal line in a roll of field fence carries tension, so a 47-inch net wire is really a fence of many tensioned wires. BC's rule is three posts for anything over six line wires.
- **The pull is long.** The University of Georgia's [horse fencing bulletin](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) recommends a double span H brace whenever the fence span runs more than 200 feet.
- **The soil is weak.** BC names sandy soils, very wet plastic clays, and peats as ground where three-post braces are standard. Blackland clay after a wet spring fits the middle item uncomfortably well.
- **It is a high fence.** An 8-foot [game fence](/game-fence-installation-waco/) carries its load high on a tall post, so those corners are nearly always double H or welded pipe.

One detail people get wrong: where to tie the wire. BC's guidance says a three-post end brace ties off at the centre post, with the back panel "slack wired." Tie off at the far back post and any small misalignment between the three posts can buckle the whole assembly.

## N-brace: the diagonal-rail version

"N-brace" gets used loosely, but it most often means two posts joined by a diagonal rail instead of a horizontal one, so the side view looks like the letter N. BC's companion factsheet on [end, inline and change-of-direction braces](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/agriculture-and-seafood/farm-management/structures-and-mechanization/300-series/307220-2_brace_assemblies_for_wire_fences_-_end_inline.pdf) calls it a two-post, diagonal rail brace and rates it about the same load as a single H in similar soil, with three trade-offs:

- **It works in one direction only.** Load it from the wrong side and the brace wire goes slack, so it is no good as an in-line brace in the middle of a run.
- **It lifts the corner post harder.** BC notes the diagonal rail moves the end post less but puts a greater uplifting force on it, and recommends deeper end posts for all diagonal braces.
- **The joints have to be tight.** A sloppy notch lets the rail slip, and then the brace is just two posts leaning together.

N-braces make the most sense in welded pipe, where the diagonal is welded solid at both ends and joint slip stops being a worry.

## Floating brace: one hole, one diagonal

The floating brace also goes by angle brace, diagonal brace, or "Kiwi" brace. It uses one set corner post with a diagonal running from partway up that post down to a block on the ground instead of a second set post. A tension wire from low on the corner post to the foot of the diagonal holds it in place.

A Virginia Tech extension agent's [write-up on the floating brace](https://ext.vt.edu/content/dam/shenandoah_ext_vt_edu/files/ag/graze300/The%20Fenceline%20-%20July%206%202020.pdf) gives a workable spec: a 7 or 8 foot end post, 5 to 8 inches across, notched at two thirds of the top wire height; a brace at least 4 inches round and 6 to 8 feet long; a flat rock or treated 2x10 under the foot; and one strand of 12.5 gauge high-tensile wire around the base of the end post, tightened with an in-line strainer.

On strength, the sources run from about equal to an H (that agent, and BC's single span rating) to UGA's "not as strong as the H-brace." A fair read: a well-built floating brace does the job of a single H on a normal barbed wire pull, and it is not the brace for a long net wire stretch.

Where it shines is rock. West of the Brazos and through much of Bosque County, limestone or caliche sits a foot or two down, and every set post means drilling. A floating brace cuts the holes at a corner from two per fence line to one. Our guide to [setting posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/) covers the drilling side.

Two cautions. BC stresses that the foot of the diagonal must be free to slide a little on its block, or it tends to jack the corner post up and out. And on blackland clay, a foot block sitting on the surface can sink or shift through a few wet and dry cycles. On heavy clay, dig the second hole and build an H.

## Welded pipe corners

On a [pipe fence](/pipe-fence-waco/) the corners are pipe too. The Texas NRCS [fence practice specification](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/29884/382_TX_PS_Fence_2021) says all steel brace assemblies will be welded, with a bracketed "no weld" assembly allowed only by exception, such as a site a welder cannot reach. It also calls for pipe posts to be capped and painted or galvanized.

A welded pipe brace can be built as an H, a double H, or an N. The welded joints hold the frame square without the twisted brace wire a wood H depends on, and steel does not rot at the ground line. That makes pipe the usual pick for gate hinge posts, pens where cattle push on the fence daily, and game fence corners.

For sizing, our article on [what size pipe to use](/blog/what-size-pipe-for-pipe-fence/) compares 2 3/8 and 2 7/8 inch pipe. Going up a size at corners and gates matches BC's rule of thumb that brace posts should be one diameter size larger than line posts.

## Dead-man braces

"Dead-man" means two different things depending on who is talking.

The first is a buried thrust block. The UGA bulletin describes a large end post, 10 to 12 inches across and at least 4 feet in the ground, with a log buried against it so that when the post tries to lean it has to push the dead-man sideways through the soil.

The second is a buried anchor behind the post: a log, steel plate, or screw-in anchor tied to the corner post with wire or cable. BC says this design can equal a double span brace in suitable soil, that firm soil is needed for best results, that a corner needs two anchors (one per fence line), and that screw-type anchors are hard to install in rocky ground.

Both depend on the soil gripping the anchor. In firm ground they hold well. In wet clay or loose sand they creep, and a two or three post brace is the safer build.

## Corners, ends, in-line braces and dog-legs

- **Ends and gate posts.** The wire pulls one way. On the hinge side of a gate the brace also carries the gate's weight in the other direction, which is why BC puts two diagonals on that brace.
- **True corners.** A square corner is two end braces sharing one post, one down each fence line. BC says a shared-post corner works for direction changes of 60 to 90 degrees.
- **Dog-legs.** A shallow bend is harder than it looks, because the wire pulls the post toward the inside of the bend. The Texas NRCS spec requires a brace wherever the fence changes direction 20 degrees or more. For bends under 60 degrees, BC recommends separate end braces on each side or a brace on the bend post itself.
- **In-line braces.** A long straight run still needs a brace assembly about every 650 feet (UGA's figure) to tie off wire. The pull comes from both sides, so it gets two diagonal wires in an X, and BC says they must not be wrapped together where they cross.

## Choosing a brace for your ground and your fence

- Five-strand barbed wire, normal pull, firm soil: single H, or a floating brace in rock.
- Net wire or goat fence: double H at corners and ends.
- 8-foot game fence: double H or welded pipe, set deep.
- Pipe fence, pens, and gates: welded pipe braces.
- One to three electric wires: BC rates a single heavy, deep-set end post for that load; add a dead-man anchor where the soil is firm and the post needs more hold.
- Blackland clay that heaves: longer spans, deeper posts, and three posts rather than two when in doubt. Our post on [posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers depth and tamping.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the strongest type of fence corner brace?

A three-post brace, either a wood double H or a welded pipe double H, is the strongest common design. The BC Ministry of Agriculture puts three-post braces at almost double the capacity of two-post braces, which is why they are standard for net wire, high fence, long pulls, and soft ground. A single H is plenty for most five-strand barbed wire on firm soil.

### Is a floating brace as good as an H-brace?

Sources differ. A Virginia Tech extension agent reports virtually no difference in strength, BC rates it equal to a single span brace, and UGA puts angle braces a step below the H. In practice a well-built floating brace handles a normal barbed wire pull and saves a hole in rock. For net wire or long pulls, build a double H.

### What is an N brace on a fence?

An N brace is usually two posts joined by a diagonal rail instead of a horizontal one, so the side view looks like the letter N. It holds about the same load as a single H, but it resists pull from one direction only and puts more uplift on the corner post, so that post should go deeper. It works best welded in pipe.

### Which way does the brace wire go on an H-brace?

The diagonal wire runs from low on the corner post, where the fence wire ties off, to high on the brace post. The Texas NRCS specification puts it about 4 inches above ground on the anchor post and 4 to 6 inches above the rail on the brace post. Run it the other way and the wire goes slack as the corner leans, so it holds nothing.

### Do I need a brace at every bend in the fence?

Not every bend, but more than most people expect. The Texas NRCS fence specification calls for a brace wherever the fence starts, stops, or changes direction 20 degrees or more. Shallow dog-legs are where unbraced posts lean first, because the wire keeps pulling them toward the inside of the bend until they give.
