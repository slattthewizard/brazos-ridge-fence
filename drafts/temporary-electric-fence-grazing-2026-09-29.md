# Temporary Electric Fence for Rotational Grazing: Polywire, Step-In Posts and What Actually Holds

A temporary electric fence for grazing is one or two strands of polywire or polytape on step-in posts, powered by a portable charger and rolled out and back up on a reel. One person can set a new paddock in well under an hour. It holds trained cattle and horses inside a sound perimeter. It is a weak choice for goats and sheep unless you step up to electric netting, and it is never the fence between your cattle and a county road.

That last point is the whole design. Portable fence is a management tool, not a barrier. It works because the animal respects the shock, and only while the charge is up, the ground is good and the wire is off the weeds. Build the permanent fence to carry the load and let the polywire do the moving.

## Polywire, polytape and polyrope

All three are plastic fibers with fine metal filaments braided through them. The plastic gives strength and visibility; the metal carries the shock. The [University of Maine's temporary fence materials evaluation](https://extension.umaine.edu/livestock/pasture-course/lesson-3/fences-that-work-temporary-electric-fence-materials-evaluation/) notes that products carry 3 to 9 metal strands and that voltage drops sharply in steel and polyethylene wire more than half a mile from the charger. An aluminum and fiberglass product held its voltage at a mile.

On the ground that means:

- **Short paddock splits:** ordinary six-strand polywire is fine and rolls up easily.
- **Long runs:** use a heavier, more conductive wire, or park the charger in the middle so the pulse travels half the run each way.
- **Horses:** wide polytape. The [University of Tennessee fence planning guide (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) says tape is easier to see and works better for horses, and that black polywire is the hardest color for animals and people to see.

The same Tennessee guide adds two things worth remembering. Stainless filaments last longer but conduct less than aluminum, which conducts better but breaks more easily. And grass or weeds touching polywire on a low impedance charger can burn the fine wires in two, which on a wet spring pasture happens fast. Check the wire at every move.

Treat poly conductors as a short-life item. The Tennessee guide puts temporary fences at one to three years of life, and a Central Texas summer is a lot of sun on plastic.

## Step-in posts and spacing

Plastic step-in posts with a tread foot and molded clips are the default for daily moves. Fiberglass rods are stiffer, last longer and stand up better in wind. Use T-posts or existing pipe at ends, corners and anywhere the wire turns hard, because a step-in post pulled sideways just lays over.

Spacing has no single number. The Tennessee guide lists 40 to 75 feet between line posts for electric fence, but that table covers permanent fence with tight wire. Polywire on step-ins sags sooner. The [Noble Research Institute's solar polywire paddock walkthrough](https://www.noble.org/legacy/2021-winter/how-to-set-up-a-solar-charged-polywire-paddock/) sets fiberglass posts 10 to 11 yards apart, about 30 to 33 feet, which is a sensible working figure for most Central Texas pasture. Close it up on rolling ground (a post at every rise and dip), in a steady south wind, and in creek bottoms where cattle want to cross.

Blackland clay changes with the month. Wet, a step-in foot goes in easily and the post leans in the gumbo. In August the same clay is hard and cracked and a plastic foot bends before it goes in. West of the Brazos and in Bosque County, limestone and caliche under thin soil can stop a fiberglass rod a few inches down. A short steel pilot rod and a hammer to start the hole saves a lot of broken posts.

## Wire height and strands by species

**Cattle.** One strand holds trained mature cattle. Noble sets the wire at 36 inches for cows and 33 inches for stockers. The [NRCS Texas fence specification (Code 382)](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf) lists a single electrified wire for cattle and horses at an average height of 32 inches, for cross fence in a grazing system, not for exterior fence. Cows with calves at side need a second, lower strand, because calves walk under one wire set at cow height.

**Horses.** Wide tape at a height the horse sees clearly. A horse that hits a hot wire at a dead run goes through it. Portable tape is fine for strip grazing a trained horse inside a safe perimeter, not as a substitute for the fence in [the best fence for horses](/blog/best-fence-for-horses/).

**Goats and sheep.** Their hair insulates them, and goats test fence with head and body and slip between strands a cow would never try. Among temporary electric fence types, the NRCS Texas spec names net fencing as the one for sheep and goats, which in practice means electric netting, not two strands of polywire. A goat tangled in dead netting can strangle, so the charge has to stay up. For a permanent answer, see our [goat and sheep fence](/goat-sheep-fence-waco/) page.

**The shock.** The NRCS Texas spec, written for permanent electric fence, calls for a minimum at the farthest point of 1,600 volts for cattle and 2,000 volts for sheep and hair goats. Noble's walkthrough uses 4,000 volts as its floor. Test at the far end of the run, not at the charger, and if it reads weak, our [electric fence troubleshooting guide](/blog/electric-fence-not-working/) walks through the causes.

## Chargers and grounding for portable use

Most portable setups run a battery or solar charger clipped to a T-post. Buy low impedance. The Tennessee guide's sizing rule is to count total miles of electrified wire, add 25 percent for weed load, and buy a charger rated for at least that many miles. Treat a box's miles rating as optimistic and compare output joules where the maker lists them. Noble's advice is to buy a larger solar charger than you think you need, and that holds here, where summer heat and short winter days both work against a small panel and battery. Disconnect in a thunderstorm when you can: Tennessee notes lightning can travel as far as two miles on a fence.

The ground is where portable fence fails in Central Texas. The circuit closes when an animal touches the wire and current runs through its feet, the soil and back to the rod. Dry soil conducts poorly, which is why a fence that bit hard in April feels like a tickle in August. [Virginia Tech's grounding publication (SPES-691P)](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/SPES/spes-691/spes-691.html) says a single 3-foot portable rod works adequately for small solar units, but also that an effective rod needs at least 6 feet in moist soil. In a Texas July, cracked blackland and caliche make the second point the one that counts.

What works when the ground is dry:

- **Ground-return wire.** Run a second strand from the charger's ground terminal just below the hot wire. The animal touches both and completes the circuit through the wires instead of the dirt. Noble gives the same answer for dry ground, and the NRCS Texas spec calls for mixing positive and negative wires where the soil dries out too much to give a good shock. It means two reels, but it works in July.
- **A real ground at a fixed charger.** If the portable wire ties into a permanent charger at the barn, the NRCS Texas spec calls for at least three 6-foot galvanized rods spaced 10 feet apart. Keep it all galvanized; Virginia Tech warns that mixing copper and galvanized steel causes corrosion and poor conductivity.
- **Damp ground.** A portable rod near a trough overflow does better than one in bare dry clay.

If your perimeter already carries an offset hot wire, a reel can often tie into it with an insulated connector and run off that charger and its ground. [Electric fence vs barbed wire](/blog/electric-fence-vs-barbed-wire/) covers where that offset wire earns its keep.

## Train cattle before the first move

Do not unroll polywire across a pasture and turn naive cattle into it. The NRCS Texas spec recommends a training area inside a well-fenced pen, with at least 12 hours of exposure, and notes most animals are trained within 48 hours. Noble puts its training wire near the water trough, where every animal will touch it. Visibility matters as much as voltage: the Tennessee guide points out an animal needs to see the wire as it feels the shock, so flag thin wire during training.

## Running the rotation

A rotation that works here usually has three layers:

1. **Permanent perimeter and permanent water.** Water decides the layout. Either every paddock reaches a trough or you run a lane back to water with polywire on both sides.
2. **Permanent main cross fences** splitting the place into a few large units.
3. **Polywire subdivisions** inside those units, moved every few days, or daily in strip grazing.

In strip grazing the front wire gives the herd its next strip and the back fence keeps them off what they just grazed, so regrowth gets its rest. Skip the back fence and cattle walk back to nip the new growth, which defeats the rotation. Anchor both ends of every run to something solid, use an insulated gate handle where you drive through, move the wire before hungry cattle start leaning on it, and walk every new line once with the tester before you leave.

## Where temporary fence does not belong

Portable fence holds calm, fed, trained cattle standing next to a live wire. It does not hold panicked cattle, a bull after a heifer across the line, or anything next to a dead wire. Deer run through polywire and snap it. Feral hogs root under it. Lightning or a flat battery on a Friday can leave it dead all weekend.

So it belongs inside a real perimeter, never on road frontage. The NRCS Texas spec itself limits temporary electric fence to interior cross fencing and areas where livestock pressure is light. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How far apart should step-in posts be for polywire?
There is no single rule. The Noble Research Institute's polywire walkthrough sets posts 10 to 11 yards apart, roughly 30 to 33 feet, which suits most pasture. Add a post at every rise and dip so the wire stays at a steady height, tighten the spacing in wind and creek bottoms, and use T-posts at ends and corners where the wire pulls hardest.

### How high should a temporary electric fence be for cattle?
For mature, trained cattle, one strand at about 32 to 36 inches works. The NRCS Texas fence spec lists a single electric wire at an average of 32 inches for cattle cross fence, and the Noble Research Institute runs 36 inches for cows and 33 for stockers. Cows with young calves need a second, lower strand, because calves walk under a single wire set at cow height.

### Will polywire hold goats?
Not reliably. A goat's hair insulates it, and goats test fence with their heads and bodies and slip between strands. Among temporary electric fence types, the NRCS Texas spec names net fencing for sheep and goats, which in practice means electric netting with a strong charger and a good ground. For a permanent goat pasture, woven wire is the answer, and a hot offset wire helps keep them off it.

### Do I need a ground rod with a small solar fence charger?
Yes. Every charger needs a ground to complete the circuit. Virginia Tech says a single 3-foot portable rod works well for most small solar units, and also that an effective rod needs at least 6 feet in moist soil. In dry Central Texas clay or caliche the short rod struggles, so run a ground-return wire just below the hot wire so the animal completes the circuit by touching both, or tie into a permanent charger with a proper multi-rod ground.

### How long does polywire last?
Not long. The University of Tennessee's fence guide puts temporary fences at one to three years in most cases. Sun breaks down the plastic, weeds touching the wire can burn the fine filaments in two on a low impedance charger, and every hard knot or kink weakens the metal. Store reels out of the sun and replace any stretch that still tests weak after splicing.
