# Wildlife Friendly Fencing: How to Hold Cattle and Still Let Deer Cross

Wildlife friendly fencing is livestock fence built so deer can jump it and fawns can crawl under it without getting hung up. The published guidance comes down to four numbers: a top wire no higher than about 40 to 42 inches, at least 12 inches between the top two wires, a smooth (barbless) bottom wire 16 to 18 inches off the ground, and as few loose stays as you can get away with.

That design holds settled cows on decent grass. It does not hold goats, sheep, hogs, a bull with ideas, or anything you are trying to keep inside a high fence. So the real question on a Central Texas place is not how to build a wildlife friendly fence. It is which of your fences should be one.

## What actually kills deer on a wire fence

The clearest field numbers come from a Utah State University study of more than 600 miles of rangeland fence, summarized in Montana Fish, Wildlife and Parks' [A Landowner's Guide to Wildlife Friendly Fences](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/land-owner-wildlife-resources/a_landowners_guide_to_wildlife_friendly_fences.pdf). That was mule deer, pronghorn and elk country, not whitetail country, but a deer's hind legs work the same way in Bosque County. The findings that matter:

- Most of the animals found tangled (69% of juveniles and 77% of adults) were caught in the top two wires while trying to jump.
- 70% of the deaths were on fences taller than 40 inches.
- Juveniles were 8 times more likely to die in fences than adults.
- Woven wire with a single strand of barbed wire on top was the deadliest fence type, because a leg slips between the stiff mesh and the barb and twists.
- Of the carcasses found beside a fence rather than in it, 90% were fawns lying curled up, most likely separated from the doe when they could not cross.

That points to two places on the fence: the top two wires, where a jumping deer's hind legs catch, and the bottom wire, which decides whether a fawn goes under or gets stranded.

Timing matters too. TPWD's page on [the rut in white-tailed deer](https://tpwd.texas.gov/huntwild/hunt/planning/rut_whitetailed_deer/) puts 90% of Cross Timbers fawns on the ground by about June 15 to 20. Spotted fawns are trailing does through your fences from early summer on.

## The wildlife friendly spec, wire by wire

This is the design the Montana guide calls the livestock and wildlife compromise, with notes for Central Texas.

### Top wire: 40 inches preferred, 42 inches maximum

A whitetail can clear far more than that, so the point is not whether a deer can jump it. The point is a clean jump instead of a clipped one. The higher the top wire, the more often a jumping deer catches it with its hind legs, which fits that 70% figure above.

Slope changes the math. The guide works out that a 42-inch fence on a 10% slope is effectively 48.6 inches tall, and 62 inches on a 30% slope. In the broken country west of the Brazos, a fence that looks low on paper can be a tall one where it runs across a hillside or the side of a draw.

### Top two wires: at least 12 inches apart

This is the cheapest fix on the fence. When the top two wires are close, a hind leg goes between them, the wires twist, and the animal hangs. Spread them and the leg drops through.

The Texas NRCS fence specification (practice code 382, [TX-382, October 2015](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf)) handles it a little differently. Where whitetails occur and the fence is under 48 inches, it says to consider spacing the top two wires 10 inches apart, and it lets the landowner choose smooth wire for the top strand where barbed wire is used. Different number, same idea: do not crowd the top of the fence.

### Top wire: smooth, and easy to see

A barbless top wire will not tear a deer that brushes it, or a horse. Cows mostly lean on the middle wires, so giving up barbs on top costs little.

Visibility is the other half. The Montana guide suggests a top rail, a PVC sleeve, high-visibility polywire or tape, or flagging, and warns that smooth high-tensile wire can be close to invisible. The flagging minimum it passes on from Wyoming Game and Fish is two markers per 16 feet of top wire. Doing that only at known crossings is a small job.

### Bottom wire: smooth, 16 to 18 inches up

This is where the guidance splits by species. The Montana guide wants the bottom wire at 16 inches minimum and 18 preferred, smooth. TPWD's [Landowner's Guide to Pronghorn-friendly Fences](https://tpwd.texas.gov/publications/pwdpubs/media/pwd_lf_w7000_1787.pdf) says a smooth bottom wire at least 18 inches above the ground, and adds that its recommendations have held up without more calves crawling under. The Texas NRCS spec puts the bottom strand of a 4-strand cattle fence at 14 inches minimum, with the top at 44 inches or more.

Pronghorn are a West Texas and Panhandle animal, so the 18-inch number was written for an animal we do not have here. For whitetail fawns, 16 inches of clear, smooth space is a reasonable target. The catch is that the same gap is a hog road, covered below.

### Stays: fewer, or kept straight

Twisted wire stays hold strand spacing, but a bent-over stay folds the fence into a three-dimensional snare. The Montana guide prefers no vertical stays, stiff composite ones, or wire stays someone actually checks. On a Texas barbed wire fence, stays are standard and are not going away, so keep them straight and replace the bent ones when you ride the fence.

## When wildlife friendly is the wrong goal

The compromise gives up height on top and a tight bottom. Plenty of Central Texas fences cannot give up either.

### Road frontage and anywhere an escape is expensive

Depending on the road and your county's stock law, a cow on the road can turn into your liability, and a 42-inch fence with an 18-inch gap underneath is not what you want between a calf and traffic. Fence along a road or a neighbor's bull should be built to hold stock first. That usually means our standard 5-strand [barbed wire fence](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/) with the top wire at 48 inches or more. You can still use a smooth top wire and keep the top two wires spread apart. Those two changes cost almost nothing in control.

### Goats, sheep and anything that goes under

A goat will be through a 16-inch gap by lunch. Goat and sheep fence is net wire to the ground (the NRCS spec allows 39-inch net wire set at ground level for sheep or goats alone), and a fawn cannot get through it. That is a trade you accept. What you can avoid is the deadliest combination in the Utah study: woven wire topped with a single strand of barbed wire. On a [goat and sheep fence](/goat-sheep-fence-waco/), a smooth top wire or an offset electric wire tops the mesh without building that snare.

### Feral hogs

Hogs root and shove under a bottom wire set at 10 inches. A 16- to 18-inch gap is barely an obstacle to them. If hogs are working your place, and along the Brazos and Bosque bottoms they often are, a raised bottom wire is an open invitation. Stopping them is covered in our post on [feral hog fence damage](/blog/feral-hog-fence-damage/). Here you pick your battle fence by fence: raise the bottom wire on upland interior fences where hogs are not the problem, and keep it low on the creek bottoms where they are.

### High fence

An 8-foot game fence exists to stop deer, so none of this applies. See our pages on [game fence installation](/game-fence-installation-waco/) and [Texas high fence rules](/blog/texas-high-fence-rules/).

## Does a wildlife friendly fence help with a wildlife valuation?

Less than people hope. TPWD's [wildlife management plan form](https://tpwd.texas.gov/publications/pwdforms/media/pwd_885_w7000_open_space_agric_valuation_wildlife_mgmt_plan.pdf) for 1-d-1 open space valuation (PWD 885) does list fence modification under habitat control. But the target species printed on the form are pronghorn antelope and bighorn sheep, and the techniques listed are folding up the bottom of net wire and replacing net wire with barbed wire. Neither species lives around Waco. Its fence line management entry, under supplemental shelter, is about plants along the fence, not wire heights.

So do not count on a wildlife friendly rebuild to carry a plan around here. Your county appraisal district decides what qualifies, and a TPWD biologist can help write the plan.

## Retrofitting a fence you already have

You do not need to tear out a sound fence. The TPWD pronghorn guide's approach translates well: modify sections, not the whole line. It calls for raising the bottom wire for at least 20 yards every half mile, and near corners and travel corridors. Deer tell you where those corridors are. Look for worn trails that meet the fence, hair on the barbs, and bent top wires.

A reasonable retrofit list for a Central Texas cattle fence:

1. Replace the bottom barbed strand with smooth wire and raise it to 16 inches at known crossings, but only on fences where hogs and calves are not the bigger problem.
2. Move the second wire down (or the top wire, if the fence can spare the height) until there is 12 inches between them, and swap the top strand to smooth.
3. Straighten or replace bent stays and retension loose wire, which tangles legs more easily than tight wire.
4. Flag the top wire at crossings, creek bottoms and anywhere the fence runs across a slope.
5. Tear out fence you no longer use. TPWD's guide notes that pronghorn keep treating a line of leftover posts and stays as a fence, so pull the posts too.

## How we would lay it out on your place

Say you have 80 acres outside Clifton with a county road on one side, a creek bottom on another, and two interior pastures you rotate cows through. The sensible plan is three fences, not one:

- The road side stays a tight 5-strand barbed fence with the top at 48 inches or better, a smooth top wire, and 12 inches between the top two.
- The creek bottom stays low on the bottom wire because that is where hogs tend to travel, with a proper water gap where the fence crosses.
- The interior cross fences go to the wildlife friendly spec: smooth bottom at 16 inches, top at 40 to 42, smooth top wire, 12 inches between the top two.

Deer cross where it costs you nothing and stock stay where it matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What height should the bottom wire be for deer?
Aim for a smooth bottom wire about 16 inches off the ground so fawns can pass under. The Montana guide uses 16 inches as a minimum and 18 as preferred, and TPWD's pronghorn guide calls for at least 18. In Central Texas, weigh that against feral hogs and young calves, which also use the gap. A practical middle ground is raising it only at known crossings on interior fences.

### Why use smooth wire on the bottom of a fence?
A fawn or deer crawling under a barbed bottom strand drags its back across the barbs. Smooth wire lets it slide under without cuts. Grown cattle mostly push on the middle of a fence, so a barbless bottom strand costs little in control, though young calves do test the bottom. On goat or hog fence, the problem is the height of the gap, not the barbs.

### How far apart should the top two wires be on a wildlife friendly fence?
At least 12 inches, according to the Montana wildlife friendly fence guide, because deer that clip the top of a fence get a hind leg twisted between wires that are too close. The Texas NRCS fence specification suggests 10 inches where whitetails occur on fences under 48 inches. Either way, spread the top two wires apart and make the top one smooth.

### Will a wildlife friendly fence hold cattle?
It holds settled cows on decent grass in most situations. It is a poor choice for road frontage, bulls, weaned calves, or pastures next to a neighbor's herd, where you want a 5-strand fence with the top at 48 inches or more. Build the perimeter to hold stock and use the wildlife friendly spec on interior fences.

### Can I make an existing barbed wire fence wildlife friendly without rebuilding it?
Usually, yes. Swap the bottom strand for smooth wire and raise it at crossings, move a wire so the top two sit 12 inches apart, make the top strand smooth, straighten bent stays and retension slack wire. Focus on the spots deer already use rather than the whole line. Much of that is priced like repair work on our [fence repair](/fence-repair-waco/) page.
