# Highway Right of Way Fence in Texas: Who Maintains the Fence Along a State Highway

If your pasture runs along a U.S. or state highway in Texas, the fence between your livestock and the pavement is generally your job to build and keep up. That is the plain reading of the Texas A&M AgriLife fence law guide, which says that to keep livestock off interstates and state highways, it is the landowner's responsibility to build and maintain the fence. TxDOT does not normally show up to fix a landowner's pasture fence.

Generally is the key word. A fence put in as part of a highway project, a gate or cattle guard cut into the frontage, or work that reaches into the right of way itself can all involve TxDOT. This article covers what the law says about livestock and highways, how to find where your line actually is, and what to ask your TxDOT area office before you build. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Texas treats land along U.S. and state highways as closed range. The landowner is expected to keep livestock off the highway.
- The AgriLife guide puts the duty to build and maintain highway frontage fence on the landowner. A landowner with no livestock has no independent obligation to build one.
- Farm-to-market roads are handled differently from U.S. and state highways. Local stock laws can change the picture.
- Where a specific fence, gate or strip of right of way is in question, the answer lives in TxDOT's records for your tract. Check with your TxDOT area office.

## Right of way, in plain terms

A highway right of way is the strip of land the state holds on each side of the pavement. It is wider than the road. The ditch, the shoulder and often a good bit of grass past them can sit inside it. Your property line is the edge of that strip, not the edge of the asphalt.

That is why the first question on any highway frontage fence is where the line actually is. The fence you can see is not always on the line. Older fences wander. Some sit inside the right of way, and some sit well back on your own land. The AgriLife guide says it bluntly: without a survey showing where property lines end and begin, building a boundary fence is an inaccurate guess and could lead to future headaches. ## Who maintains the fence along a Texas highway

### The default: the landowner

The AgriLife guide (publication EAG-029, [A Landowner's Guide to Fence Law in Texas](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Texas-Fencing-Law-Information.pdf)) has a short section titled "Perimeter Fence between a Landowner and a State Highway." It says all interstate and state highways in Texas are closed range, and that to keep livestock off them, it is the landowner's responsibility to build and maintain a fence along the highway. It then adds the other half: if the landowner does not intend to have any livestock on the property, there is no independent obligation to build a fence.

So a hay field with no animals on it has one answer, and a pasture with cows on it has another.

### Where it varies

Every stretch of highway has its own history. Older roads, bypasses, widened lanes and interchanges can each leave a different arrangement behind. A strip of land may have been bought from a prior owner, a fence may have been moved during a project, and a deed or purchase agreement may say something specific about who handles what.

We can't tell you what is on file for your tract, and we aren't going to guess. The honest answer for any one fence is to check with your TxDOT area office. They can tell you what is on record for your frontage, and whether anything about it is unusual.

## Livestock on the highway: what the statute says

The guide quotes the Texas Agriculture Code on this point. A person who owns or has responsibility for the control of a horse, mule, donkey, cow, bull, steer, hog, sheep or goat "may not knowingly permit the animal to traverse or roam at large, unattended, on the right-of-way of a highway."

Three details from the guide are worth knowing.

### U.S. and state highways versus farm-to-market roads

The guide says all U.S. and state highways are closed range under Texas law, but farm-to-market roads are open range unless a local stock law changes that. That means a fence that is optional on a farm-to-market road in an open range county can be a real obligation along a state highway. It also says whether the surrounding area is open or closed range does not matter if the land includes a highway. Our post on [Texas stock laws by county](/blog/texas-stock-law-by-county/) covers the county side.

### What "knowingly" means

The word "knowingly" carries weight. The guide describes a court ruling in which an owner was found to have acted knowingly. He was aware the fences could not hold up in heavy rain, he knew cattle had escaped through them in storms many times before, police had told him his cattle were on the roadway, and he had not inspected the fences before the accident.

The guide also says an owner who keeps the gate locked and chained, and has no prior knowledge of his cattle escaping onto a road, would not be acting knowingly.

Read those two together. Knowing about a weak spot and doing nothing is the pattern that causes trouble. A fence that fails once in a flood is one thing. A fence that fails every spring while the owner never walks it is another. For a closer look at who pays when it goes wrong, see [cattle on road liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/).

### Emergency responders

The guide also notes that a landowner is not liable for damage from livestock escaping because of an act or omission of a firefighter or peace officer who entered the property. Its example is emergency crews cutting a highway fence to fight a fire. That is a narrow protection, and it does not cover a fence that was already failing.

## Fence work at the right of way line

Most ranch fence sits on the private side of the line. A few jobs push toward the edge, and those are the ones to ask about before you start rather than after.

### Gates and cattle guards off a highway

If you want a new entrance off a state highway, or you are changing one, ask TxDOT's area office what approval, if any, applies to your location. We don't want to guess at permit rules here, because they can differ by highway and by what is already on record. What we can say is that you should settle the question before you buy a gate or a cattle guard, not after.

Fence line clearing runs about $1 to $3 per foot here, roughly $5,300 to $15,800 a mile, and heavy cedar brake can run past that. The practical point is that clearing stops at your line. Brush and trees inside the right of way are not yours to remove on assumption. Ask the area office what you can trim and what you should leave.

## What to ask your TxDOT area office

Keep the call short and specific. These are questions, not claims. We can't answer them for you, but your area office may be able to.

1. Is there anything on record about the fence along my frontage, such as who built it or who maintains it?
2. Where is the right of way line on this stretch?
3. Is there a gate, pass or cattle guard on file for this tract, and does anything apply if I change it?
4. If I rebuild the fence, do you want notice first?
5. Are there limits on clearing or digging near the line?

Write down who you spoke with and when. If the answer changes how you build, ask for it in writing before the crew shows up.

## Building highway frontage fence that holds

Highway frontage is not the place for a minimum fence, because the cost of a failure is not just a repair. Some practical points for Central Texas ground:

- **Brace every corner and gate opening.** Highway frontage often has driveways, culverts and drainage that force breaks in the line. See [H brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/).
- **Plan for the soil.** On blackland clay, posts heave and lean through wet and dry cycles. West of the Brazos and in Bosque County, expect limestone and drilling.
- **Mind the low spots.** Highway ditches carry runoff, and creek crossings flood. A rigid wire run across a draw is a gap waiting to happen. See [water gap fences](/blog/water-gap-fence/).
- **Walk it after storms.** The court case in the AgriLife guide turned on an owner who knew his fence failed in the rain and did not look. A walk after a hard rain costs an hour.

Repair is often the larger part of highway frontage work. Most repair calls run $400 to $2,500. Splicing and retensioning a run is $400 to $900, resetting or replacing posts is $45 to $120 per post, and rebuilding a corner or H-brace is $600 to $1,400. If your fence is sagging, rusted or leaning, that is worth sorting out before the next wet spring. Our [fence repair page](/fence-repair-waco/) lists what repair calls usually cover.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does TxDOT maintain the fence along a state highway in Texas?

Generally the landowner does. The AgriLife fence law guide says it is the landowner's responsibility to build and maintain a fence along an interstate or state highway to keep livestock off it. A particular fence can have its own history, so check with your TxDOT area office about your tract.

### Do I have to fence my land along a Texas highway?

The AgriLife guide says that if a landowner does not intend to have livestock on the property, there is no independent obligation to build a fence. If you do keep livestock, state law says you may not knowingly permit them to roam at large, unattended, on a highway right of way. Farm-to-market roads and county roads follow different rules.

### Can I put a gate or cattle guard on a state highway?

We can't speak for TxDOT on that. Ask your TxDOT area office what applies to your location before you buy or build, because the answer can depend on the highway and on what is already on record. An installed cattle guard runs $3,500 to $8,000 here, so it is cheaper to ask first.

### Who pays if my cattle get on the highway?

Texas law says an owner may not knowingly permit livestock to roam at large on a highway right of way. Who is liable after an accident depends on the facts, and that is a question for a Texas attorney. Our post on [cattle on road liability](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/) explains the framework.

### Is the highway fence on my property line?

Not always. Old fences wander, and the right of way edge is the true boundary. A survey shows where the line sits, and your TxDOT area office can tell you what it has on record for the frontage.
