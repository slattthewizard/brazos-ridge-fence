## Why the Cheapest Fence Bid Usually Costs More

Here's the pattern we see over and over in McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties. Three bids come in. One is noticeably cheaper, sometimes 25 or 30 percent under the others. The landowner takes it, because a fence is a fence, right?

It isn't. Two bids that both say "5-strand barbed wire, 2,600 feet" can describe two very different fences. One might use 12.5 gauge Class 3 galvanized wire on treated posts with welded pipe H-braces at every corner. ## Questions to Ask a Fence Contractor About Price and Materials

The first four questions are about what you're actually buying. Get specific answers, in writing, every time.

**1. What exactly does the price per foot include?**

"Three dollars a foot" means nothing by itself. Does that include tearing out the old fence? Clearing brush off the line? Corner braces, gates, water gaps? Ask for an itemized bid that separates materials, labor, tear-out, and extras. When the bid is itemized, you can compare it against other bids line by line instead of guessing.

**2. What class and gauge of wire are you using?**

For barbed wire, the answer you want is 12.5 gauge, Class 3 galvanized. Class 1 wire can start rusting in a handful of years in our humidity, while Class 3 zinc coating commonly lasts decades. The price difference is small per roll and huge over the life of the fence. If the bidder can't tell you the class of the wire, that tells you plenty. We break this down in detail in our guide to [Class 3 galvanized wire](/blog/class-3-galvanized-wire/).

**3. What are the posts, and how far apart?**

Ask for species, treatment, diameter, and depth. Around here, a common solid answer is treated pine or cedar line posts set 2.5 to 3 feet deep, or T-posts with wood pulls, on 10 to 12 foot spacing for barbed wire. Wider spacing saves the contractor money and costs you tension. Our [fence post spacing guide](/blog/fence-post-spacing/) covers what's normal for each fence type.

**4. How will you brace the corners and gate openings?**

This is the single most important structural question on the list. Corners carry the entire pull of the fence, and a fence with weak corners fails no matter how good the wire is. The right answer involves H-braces, either wood with a diagonal brace wire or welded pipe. "We set a big corner post deep" is not an answer. It's a warning.

Here's what honest, itemized pricing typically looks like in the Waco area right now:

| Fence type | Typical installed price | What the bid should itemize |
|---|---|---|
| 5-strand barbed wire | $2.50 to $4.50 per ft | Wire class, post type and spacing, H-braces |
| Net wire (field fence) | $3.50 to $6.00 per ft | Knot style, top barbed strand, brace count |
| Pipe fence | $10 to $20+ per ft | Pipe size and schedule, new vs used pipe |

## Questions to Ask Your Fence Builder About the Crew and the Job

Materials are half the fence. The people building it are the other half.

**5. Who actually builds the fence, your crew or a sub?**

Plenty of good contractors use subcontractors. The problem is when the guy who bid the job never sets foot on it again. Ask who runs the crew day to day, and who you call when there's a question at 7 a.m. on a Tuesday.

**6. Can you show me proof of liability insurance?**

Not "yes, we're insured." An actual certificate of insurance, sent from the insurance company. If an uninsured crew hits your water line, breaks a septic lateral, or hurts somebody on your land, that bill can land on you.

Marcy runs goats on 30 acres near Valley Mills, and she skipped this question once. The crew she hired trenched through a buried water line to her stock tank, shrugged, and left. No insurance, no recourse, and an $1,800 repair bill she paid out of her own pocket. The certificate takes the contractor five minutes to send. Ask for it every time.

**7. How long will the job take, and what about my cattle in the meantime?**

A contractor who builds ranch fence for a living has an answer ready: how many feet per day the crew averages, how they'll sequence tear-out and rebuild so pastures stay closed, and what happens if rain shuts the job down. If you're running cattle on the place, staging matters as much as speed. See our post on [how long fence installation takes](/blog/how-long-fence-installation-takes/) for realistic timelines by fence type.

**8. What happens when you hit rock, or the creek crossing?**

Between the blackland clay east of I-35 and the limestone shelves out toward China Spring and Crawford, surprises underground are normal here. Ask how rock is handled and priced before it happens. Same for water gaps at creeks and draws, which need their own hardware and design. A contractor who says "we'll figure it out" is telling you the change orders will be figured out on your dime.

## Contract, Warranty, and Cleanup Questions Before You Sign

The last four questions protect you after the handshake.

**9. What's the payment schedule?**

A reasonable structure is a modest deposit to hold your spot on the schedule and cover materials, with the balance due when the work is done and you've walked the line. Be very careful with anyone wanting half or more up front.

Tommy, west of West up Highway 77, paid a $4,500 deposit, 60 percent of the job, to a crew that then went quiet for two months. He eventually got part of it back, but only after a lot of phone calls and a demand letter. The Texas Attorney General's office publishes solid guidance on hiring contractors and avoiding home improvement scams at [texasattorneygeneral.gov](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection). It's worth ten minutes of your time before any big job.

**10. What's the warranty, and is it in writing?**

Ask two separate questions: what does the manufacturer warranty on materials, and what does the contractor warranty on workmanship? A leaning post or a slipped brace six months in should be the contractor's problem, not yours. If the warranty isn't written into the contract, it doesn't exist.

**11. Who hauls off the old fence and cleans up the line?**

Old wire, rotten posts, brush piles, and concrete chunks don't disappear on their own. Tear-out and haul-off should be a line item with a price, not an assumption. Rolled-up old barbed wire left in a pasture is a vet bill waiting to happen.

**12. Can you give me addresses of fences you built three or more years ago?**

Anybody can show you a fence they finished last month. New fence always looks good. What you want is a fence that's been through a few Central Texas summers, a couple of ice storms, and a thousand head-rubs. Then drive by and look at the corners. Corners tell the truth.

Texas A&M AgriLife Extension also publishes good independent material on fence construction and livestock fencing standards at [agrilifeextension.tamu.edu](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/) if you want to study up before taking bids.

## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some answers should stop the deal on the spot, no matter how good the price looks:

- **No written estimate.** A verbal quote for a five-figure fence job is not a quote. It's a setup for a dispute.
- **Big cash deposit, right now.** Pressure plus prepayment is the oldest bad combination in contracting.
- **"Trust me" instead of specs.** Wire class, post depth, brace design, and spacing all fit on one page. A pro will happily write them down.
- **No local jobs to show.** If nobody within an hour of Waco will vouch for the work, there's usually a reason.
- **Vague answers on insurance.** "We've never had a problem" is not a certificate.
- **A bid far below everyone else.** Not always a scam, but always a question. Something is different, and you need to know what.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many bids should I get before hiring a fence contractor?

Three is the sweet spot for most ranch fence jobs around Waco. Fewer than that and you have no baseline for price or specs. More than four or five and you're mostly burning weeks for small differences. Make sure every bidder quotes the same footage, wire, and brace count so you're comparing like to like.

### Is a big deposit normal for ranch fencing work?

A modest deposit to cover materials and hold your place on the schedule is normal, especially on pipe fence where the steel order is a real cost. What's not normal is 50 percent or more up front, in cash, before a post is set. Keep the bulk of the payment tied to finished, inspected work.

### Do fence contractors need a license in Texas?

Texas doesn't issue a state license specifically for fence contractors, which is exactly why your questions matter so much. Since the state isn't screening anyone, the screening is on you. Insurance certificates, written contracts, local references, and aged fences you can drive past are how you vet a fence builder here.

### What should be in a fence contract before I sign?

At minimum: total footage, wire type and class, post type, spacing and depth, brace design and count, gates and water gaps, tear-out and haul-off, total price with payment schedule, a start window, and the workmanship warranty in plain words. If it's not written in the contract, treat it as not included. A one-page handshake deal protects the contractor, not you.
