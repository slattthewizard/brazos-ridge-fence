# Bump Gate: How It Works, How to Size and Set One, and When Something Else Fits Better

A bump gate is a drive-through gate that holds livestock without making you get out of the truck. You roll up, nudge the gate with your front bumper, keep rolling, and it swings shut behind you on its own. No latch, no chain, no stopping in the road with the door open.

It works well on a ranch road that sees pickups and ATVs. It works poorly for long trailers, fast drivers and anybody who forgets it's there. ## How does a bump gate work?

A conventional gate hangs on hinges and swings in one plane. A bump gate doesn't. It hangs from sleeves or collars that slip loosely around a tall post, so the gate can turn around that post like a door on a center pole. Cables run from the upper bar of the gate to a point high on the post.

When you push the gate open, it swings around the post and the cables wind up. That winding lifts the gate a little. Once you're through and let go, gravity lowers the gate back down, the cables unwind, and it swings home to the closed position. The weight of the gate does the work. There's no spring and no motor.

Builders vary the details. Some use a single tall post at the side of the road, some a post in the middle, and some hang the gate on a tilted pivot so its own weight swings it shut. The principle is the same: you supply the push, gravity supplies the close.

### Side-post and center-post layouts

Most swing-style bump gates come in one of two layouts.

- **Post at the side.** The gate hangs from a tall post on one side of the road and swings across the opening, like a very long hinged gate with lifting cables.
- **Post at the center.** One post stands in the middle of the road and the gate has an arm on each side of it. You hit one arm and the whole assembly pivots. The far arm swings around behind you, which is where the trouble starts if you hit it too hard.

The center-post version needs less room to one side and closes fast. The side-post version is gentler on a vehicle that doesn't know the gate.

## How to drive through a bump gate

The technique is dull, but it matters. Touch the gate with the bumper at a slow roll, then ease on the throttle and keep moving until the whole truck is clear. Don't stop with the gate resting on your hood. Don't charge it either. The gate is self-closing, and a hard hit can bend the gate, stretch the cables or mark up your front end.

Three habits keep it civil:

1. Hit it at the middle of your bumper, not with a corner, so it swings straight.
2. Keep moving until you can see the gate closing in the mirror.
3. Tell guests and the feed delivery driver ahead of time. A stranger who has never met one will either stop dead or floor it.

## Where a bump gate fits, and where it doesn't

A bump gate earns its keep on a ranch road you drive a lot and that sees mostly pickups and ATVs. It's the right tool when the alternative is somebody hopping out forty times a week to swing a pipe gate.

It holds more than a cattle guard does. A guard stops cattle by confusing their footing, and it won't turn sheep or goats, a point a Texas Farm Credit magazine piece on ranch gates makes outright ([Gates, Gaps and Guards](http://www.findfarmcredit.com/landscapes-articles/gates-gaps-and-guards)). That same piece says the King Ranch rarely used guards because calves could get a leg caught and broken between the pipes. A bump gate is a gate. There's no gap to put a leg through, which is a real reason to put one in where small stock or horses run near the entrance.

### Where it doesn't fit

- **Long rigs.** A stock trailer, a hay trailer or a gooseneck behind a dually is a lot of vehicle to pull through a gate that swings on a pole. The gate is built for the front of a vehicle, and a long trailer can catch it as it swings back. If trailers use the road, plan a second wide gate beside it, or skip the bump gate.
- **Horseback and on foot.** Nobody opens a bump gate from a saddle without a fuss. Put a walk-through gate or a pipe gate next to it for people, dogs and riders.
- **Public or rental traffic.** If strangers drive your road, somebody will hit it too hard. A gate that can't take a bad day will need repair.

## Sizing a bump gate for a driveway or ranch road

Size it to the widest thing you'll actually drive through, then add room. For a pickup with mirrors, plan on an opening wider than the truck by a good margin on each side, since the gate swings and nobody lines up perfectly every time. If a tractor with a loader or a wide utility vehicle goes through, size to that. For general widths and the pros and cons of each gate build, see [farm gate sizes and types](/blog/farm-gate-sizes-and-types/).

A few sizing rules we'd follow:

- **Gate height matched to your livestock.** For cattle, about the height of your fence's top wire works. Goats and sheep need a tighter lower half. Don't leave a belly gap a calf can crawl under.
- **Clear swing space.** The gate sweeps an arc around its post. Nothing can stand in that arc: no fence corner, no brace, no mailbox, no tree.
- **Level approach.** The gate wants a flat run of road for a vehicle length or two on both sides. If the approach tilts, the gate swings fast one way and hangs up the other.

None of this is a published rating. A bump gate isn't a stock item with a load class the way a cattle guard is, and whoever builds yours should size it to your road. That's the kind of thing worth settling on a walk of the entrance.

## Setting the post

The post is the whole gate. Every pound of the gate hangs from it, plus the lifting pull of the cables, plus whatever your truck hits it with. A line post will not do.

Use a heavy pipe or a big treated or cedar post and set it deep. The 2015 NRCS Texas fence specification calls for end and gate brace assemblies on both sides of a gate opening, which tells you how that post is meant to be treated: as an anchor, not a line post ([NRCS Texas Fence Code 382 specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf)). A bump gate asks more of that post than a hinged gate does, because it's hit from the side every time you drive through. Brace it like a corner. Our post on [H brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) shows the build.

Soil changes the job:

- **Blackland clay.** The clay swells and shrinks with the weather, and a post that moves out of plumb ruins a bump gate, because the sleeves bind. A deep footing and a good brace help.
- **Limestone and caliche, west of the Brazos and in Bosque County.** You'll be drilling rock. Plan on a drilled hole and a post grouted in. A shallow post set on a ledge will lean the first season.

The post has to stay straight. Once it leans, the gate hangs crooked, sticks on the open swing or won't come all the way home. If that's already happening on a gate you have, see [how to fix a sagging ranch gate](/blog/fix-sagging-ranch-gate/).

## Keeping it working

A bump gate has few parts, so the maintenance list is short. Check it a couple of times a year and after any hard storm.

- **Sleeves and collars.** Grease or oil the bearing points, and clean out dirt and mud wasp nests. A gate that binds on a collar closes slowly and stops short.
- **Cables.** Look for fraying, kinks and loose clamps where the cables meet the gate and the post. A frayed cable will quit at the worst time.
- **Alignment.** Swing the gate by hand. It should open freely and return to rest on its own. If it hangs up, look at the post first.
- **Paint and rust.** A bump gate takes bumper hits and Texas sun. Touch up bare steel before it rusts through.

- **Heavy equipment, long trailers, nobody wants to think about it:** a cattle guard with a gate beside it.

- **Goats, sheep or horses near the road:** a gate of some kind, not a guard alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a bump gate?
A bump gate is a self-closing drive-through gate for livestock property. You nudge it open with your front bumper, drive through, and it swings shut by itself. It hangs on sleeves around a tall post, and cables lift it as it opens so gravity brings it back. It lets you pass without leaving the vehicle.

### Can a bump gate work as a driveway gate?
Yes, for pickups and ATVs. It's a poor fit if long trailers or strangers use the drive, since the gate can swing back into a long rig or take a hard hit from someone who doesn't know it. For trailer traffic, a wide pipe gate or a powered gate operator is a better match. Have a builder walk the drive first.

### Are bump gates common in Texas?
They've been on Texas ranch roads for generations. You'll see them on ranch roads where drivers cross a fence line often and want a barrier that works without a stop.

### Is a bump gate better than a cattle guard?
It depends on traffic. A bump gate stops goats and sheep and has no gap to trap a leg, but it's poor for long trailers. A cattle guard handles heavy loads and long vehicles but doesn't hold small stock and needs its pit kept clean. A cattle guard installed through us runs $3,500 to $8,000.

### How do I keep a bump gate from sticking?
Keep the post plumb, grease the sleeves, and check the cables for fraying. Most binding comes from a post that has leaned in clay or a collar packed with dirt. Swing the gate by hand to see whether it comes home on its own, and fix the post before you blame the gate.
