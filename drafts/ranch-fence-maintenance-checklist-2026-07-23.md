## Why Ranch Fence Maintenance Beats Emergency Repairs Every Time

Fence problems are cheap when they're small and expensive when they're not. A loose staple costs nothing to fix. A leaning corner post that's been pulling for two years takes a full H-brace rebuild, and that's real money.

## The Spring Ranch Fence Inspection: Walk or Ride Every Foot

Spring is your big one. Winter freezes, saturated clay, and storm season all do their damage between November and March, and you want it found before summer bakes the ground hard.

Ride your entire perimeter, not just the stretches you can see from the county road. Take fence pliers, a hammer, staples, wire clips, and a can of spray paint to mark anything you can't fix on the spot.

Here's what to check:

- **Every corner and gate post.** Push on them hard. Any movement at all means the brace assembly is failing or the ground has let go. Corners carry the tension for the whole line, so a weak corner is a weak fence. If your braces look rough, our guide to [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) shows what a proper assembly looks like.
- **Wire tension on every stretch.** Grab the top strand mid-span. More than an inch or two of easy deflection means it needs a splice-and-stretch. Sagging wire is the number one thing cattle learn to push through, and our post on [fixing sagging barbed wire fence](/blog/fix-sagging-barbed-wire-fence/) walks through the repair step by step.
- **Line posts.** Look for leaners, snapped T-posts, and wood posts that wiggle at the ground line. Probe wood posts with a screwdriver right at the soil. If the tip sinks in, rot has started. Catch it early and you replace one post, not twenty. More on that in our [fence post rot](/blog/fence-post-rot/) article.
- **Staples and clips.** Loose staples let wire slide and sag. Reset them as you go. On T-posts, replace any missing wire clips.
- **Water gaps.** Every creek and draw crossing needs a hard look after spring rains. A washed-out [water gap fence](/blog/water-gap-fence/) is an open door, and cattle find open doors fast.
- **Gates and latches.** Swing every gate. Tighten hinge bolts, oil latches, and check that gate posts haven't heaved.
- **Grounding and chargers** on electric fence. Test voltage at the far end of every hot line, not just at the charger.

Marcy Templin, who runs stockers on leased ground outside China Spring, learned the water gap lesson the hard way. A spring flood took out a gap on Hog Creek and she didn't check it for three weeks. Fourteen head crossed onto the neighbor's wheat. Between gathering costs, a hired day of labor, and paying for grazed-out wheat, she figured it cost her about $1,900. The gap itself was a $450 rebuild. Now she checks every crossing within 48 hours of any hard rain.

## Summer Upkeep: Heat, Drought, and Brush on the Line

Summer maintenance is less about the fence and more about what's happening around it.

Drought is hard on fence in ways people don't expect. When our blackland clay dries out, it shrinks and cracks, and posts that were tight in April can be loose by August. Walk your corners again in mid-summer and check for movement. If you're on heavy clay, our post on [fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) explains why this happens and what depth and concrete work actually fix it.

Brush is the other summer job. Cedar, mesquite, and greenbriar growing into your fence line do three things: they hide damage, they hold moisture against your wire and speed up rust, and they give feral hogs cover to root right along your fence. Clearing a strip on both sides of the wire is some of the highest-value ranch fence maintenance you can do. It also makes every future inspection twice as fast because you can actually see the fence.

Summer is also when hog damage shows up, since dry conditions push hogs toward creek bottoms and stock tanks. Look for rooted-up soil under your bottom strand and stretched or popped net wire. Patch small breaches immediately, because hogs return to a working hole every night until it's fixed.

Two more quick summer items:

- Check any stretch of fence near a stock tank or pond. Cattle crowd there in the heat and lean on wire while they wait their turn.
- Look at your shade lines. Wherever cattle bunch up under oaks in July, the fence behind them takes a beating.

## Fall and Winter Prep: The Second Annual Fence Inspection

Your fall inspection mirrors the spring one, but the priorities shift. Now you're getting ready for winter weather and for the season when grass runs short and cattle start reaching through fences for whatever's green on the other side.

Work through this list in October or November:

- **Restretch anything marginal.** Wire that's borderline in October will be on the ground by February once ice storms and cattle pressure hit it.
- **Replace questionable posts now.** Setting posts is miserable work in a January cold snap. Do it in decent weather.
- **Walk every foot of fence between your herd and a road.** This is the stretch that matters most. Texas courts look hard at whether a rancher maintained his fences when cattle end up on a roadway, and our article on [cattle on road liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/) covers why that fence deserves your best wire and your closest attention.
- **Service gates before mud season.** A gate that drags in November will be dug into a mudhole by January.
- **Test electric fence weekly through winter.** Dry winter soil weakens grounding, and a charger that reads fine at the barn can be dead two pastures away.
- **Stockpile repair material.** Keep a roll of wire, a bag of staples, splice sleeves, and two spare posts in the barn. Storm repairs happen on the storm's schedule, not yours.

Roy Kubacak, who backgrounds calves on a place east of West, puts it simply: he spends two Saturdays every November on fence and hasn't had calves out in six years. His neighbor skipped fall prep last year, lost a stretch of rusted wire in a February ice storm, and spent $3,100 on an emergency rebuild plus a long cold night gathering calves off Highway 77 right-of-way. Same weather, same county, very different winter.

## When to Stop Patching and Call a Professional

Most of this checklist is honest DIY work, and we'd never tell a rancher to pay for something he can do with pliers and an afternoon. But there's a line where patching stops making sense, and knowing where it is will save you money.

Call a professional when:

- **Corners or H-braces have failed.** Brace assemblies carry serious tension. Built wrong, they fail again within a year, and a snapped brace wire under load can hurt you.
- **You're splicing the same stretch every month.** Wire that's rusted brittle can't be saved a splice at a time. At some point you're spending replacement money on repairs.
- **More than about a third of your posts in a section are bad.** Past that point, section replacement is cheaper per year of fence life than post-by-post patching.
- **Storm damage is widespread.** Trees on wire, multiple broken posts, and downed spans across a whole pasture need equipment and crew, not a weekend.
- **The fence holds cattle next to a road.** This is not the place for a marginal repair. The liability risk is too high to gamble on old wire.

One safety note we mean sincerely: never cut a wire that's under heavy tension without knowing where it will go, and stay clear of any fence tangled with downed power lines. Call the utility first, every time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I inspect my ranch fence?

Walk or ride the full perimeter twice a year, once in early spring and once in late fall. Add a quick check after every major storm, flood, or hard freeze, paying special attention to water gaps and any fence along a road. Most serious fence failures give weeks or months of warning to anyone who's looking.

### How long should a well-maintained barbed wire fence last?

With good posts, quality galvanized wire, and regular upkeep, 30 to 40 years is realistic in Central Texas, and some fences go longer. Neglected fence often needs major work in half that time. The biggest life-extenders are keeping brush off the wire, fixing sags before cattle exploit them, and replacing rotting posts one at a time instead of letting rot spread.

### What's the most important part of the fence to maintain?

Corners and gate ends, without question. They carry the tension for every strand on the line, so one failed corner can put a quarter mile of fence on the ground. After corners, prioritize water gaps and any stretch separating your cattle from a public road, since those failures cost the most when they happen.

### Is it worth paying someone to do fence maintenance?

Routine work like stapling, clipping, and minor splices is well within most ranchers' reach. Paying a professional makes sense for brace rebuilds, large restretches, storm damage, and any job where more than a third of the posts or wire in a section is failing. A good contractor will give you a written estimate and tell you honestly which repairs can wait.
