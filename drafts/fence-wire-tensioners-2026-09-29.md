# Inline Strainers, Tension Springs and Fence Stretchers: How Wire Gets Tight and Stays Tight

An inline strainer is a small ratchet drum that sits in the middle of a wire run and holds tension for the life of the fence. You wind the wire onto it with a handle, a pawl locks it, and later you add a click when the wire relaxes. It is a holding tool. The heavy pulling is done by something else: a bar-type fence stretcher, a come-along with a wire grip, or a stretcher bar and chains for woven wire.

How tight depends on the wire. High-tensile smooth wire is set to a measured 200 to 250 pounds and held with a strainer and a tension spring. Ordinary barbed wire wants a similar running tension, but almost nobody measures it. It gets stretched by feel with a bar stretcher, and cranking it harder does it no favors.

## Two jobs: pulling tension and holding it

People call every one of these tools a "wire tightener," and that is where a lot of fence trouble starts. There are two jobs.

**Pulling** takes a strand from loose to tight over a long run. That needs leverage and a grip that will not slip or kink the wire. Fence stretchers, come-alongs and stretcher bars do this, then come off the fence.

**Holding** keeps the tension after the pulling tool is gone. On a conventional barbed wire fence, the wraps at the brace post and the brace itself do the holding. On a high-tensile fence, a permanent inline strainer holds it, and a tension spring shows you how much is there.

Use a strainer to drag a quarter mile of slack out of old wire and you usually end up with a bent handle and a drum full of wire. Use each tool for its job.

## Inline ratchet strainers

### How they work

The common inline strainer is a steel or aluminum body with a slotted drum. You feed the wire end into the drum, turn it with a strainer handle (a short lever that fits the drum), and a spring-loaded pawl drops into the ratchet teeth so it cannot unwind. To let tension off, you take the load on the handle first, then lift the pawl and ease it back.

They are cheap, they stay on the fence, and one handle in your pocket adjusts every wire. Some people call them fence ratchets or wire tighteners. Same idea.

### Where they go

Near the middle of the pull, not at the corner. Oklahoma State University's extension [fencing chapter](https://extension.okstate.edu/programs/meat-goat-production/site-files/docs/chapter-11-fencing.pdf) says in-line strainers should sit near the middle of the fence line so tension is the same in both directions. Pull from one end of a long run and the far end lags, because the wire drags across every post and clip between you and it.

One strainer per wire per pull section is the norm. The Illinois NRCS fence specification ([Code 382](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/5590/382_IL_PS_Fence-Barbed_Wire_Woven_Wire_or_Suspension_Fence_2019)) puts strainers on high-tensile smooth wire in the center of each pull section, and makes an exception for stretches shorter than 600 feet. On a run that short there is little drag to even out, so the strainer can sit closer to one end where it is easy to reach.

### On barbed wire and brace wire

Strainers are built for smooth wire. You can put one on barbed wire so you can re-tension without a stretcher, but the barbs catch as the drum winds and it is fussy work. On most barbed fences, a good stretch at build time and a proper tie-off at solid braces does the job, with a stretcher brought back out when it needs attention.

A better use on a barbed fence is the diagonal wire on an H-brace. The Illinois spec lists a strainer or wire tightener as an accepted way to tighten brace wire, alongside the twist stick. It lets you snug a brace back up without re-twisting. Our post on [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) covers why corners fail.

## Tension springs

A tension spring is a heavy coil spring set in line with the wire, usually close to the strainer. It does two things. It gives a little when a cow leans on the fence, a limb comes down across it, or the wire contracts on a cold night, so the whole shock does not go straight to the braces. And it works as a gauge: as tension rises the coils close up, and marks on the spring tell you roughly how many pounds you are holding. Calibration is not universal, so go by the instructions on the spring you bought.

You do not need one on every wire. The Oklahoma State chapter says to use one tension spring on one wire per fence, set that wire to the proper tension, and bring the others up to match by feel or sound, like tuning a guitar. It adds that the spring usually goes on the second wire, and that putting it on the top wire gives extra give against falling limbs. On a Central Texas line with live oak or hackberry hanging over it, the top wire is worth considering.

## Pulling tools

### Bar-type fence stretcher

The classic fence stretcher, often sold as a barbed wire stretcher, is a steel bar with a ratchet or lever action, a chain or hook at one end that goes around a post, and a jaw at the other that bites the wire. The harder it pulls, the harder the jaw grips. One person can run it on most barbed wire work, and it handles barbs better than a strainer drum. Hook it to a solid post, ideally the brace you are about to tie off to, not a line post that will lean toward you.

### Come-along with a wire grip

A come-along will pull a strand, but only with a proper wire grip, the kind that clamps tighter as load rises. A loop of wire twisted around the strand, or a pair of locking pliers, will slip or kink it, and a kink is a weak spot you just built into the fence. Come-alongs have more pull than a single barbed strand needs, so go easy on the handle. That is how wire gets stretched thin and braces get pulled over.

### Stretcher bar for woven wire

Net wire and field fence are stretched as a whole panel. A stretcher bar clamps across the end of the roll, chains run from the top and bottom of the bar to a puller or come-along, and the full width comes tight at once.

Woven wire has small waves pressed into the line wires, called crimps or tension curves, and they tell you when to stop. Stay-Tuff's guide on [how to stretch fence wire](https://staytuff.com/blog/how-to-stretch-fence-wire/) says to keep tensioning until about half of the crimp is pulled out. Pull it flat and the wire has no give left for an animal or a cold night. Some crews pull woven wire with a tractor. A tractor has no feel, so go slow and have someone watching the crimp. ## How much tension each wire wants

**High-tensile smooth wire** is made to run tight and stay there. The Oklahoma State chapter says each wire is tightened with a permanent in-line strainer and set at 200 to 250 pounds. The Illinois NRCS spec gives the same range for high-tensile wire run above woven wire, with a tension spring on at least one wire as a guide.

**Standard 12.5-gauge barbed wire** is softer, low-carbon steel. Overload it and it stretches, and that stretch does not come back. You thin the wire, crack the zinc coating and put extra pull on every brace. The Texas NRCS [fence specification (Code 382)](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/29884/382_TX_PS_Fence_2021) gives no pound figure for a standard fence. It says wire is stretched to sufficient tension before it is fastened to posts, and that temperature has to be considered. The British Columbia agriculture ministry's [fence wire factsheet](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/agriculture-and-seafood/farm-management/structures-and-mechanization/300-series/307100-2_fence_wire_-_dispensing_stapling.pdf) does give one: about 250 pounds for any nonelectric wire fence, barbed included. It also has new two-strand barbed wire stretched briefly to about 600 pounds to seat the strands and barbs, then let back to about 250 before stapling. That is one extension office's method, and few crews here carry a gauge for barbed wire. In the field, each strand runs straight with no dip and springs back when you push it.

**Suspension barbed wire**, with posts far apart and twisted stays between, is where sag gets measured. The Texas spec allows no more than 3 inches of sag between posts set at 100 feet, and 1.5 inches at 50 feet.

| Wire | Pull it with | Hold it with | How tight |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-tensile smooth, 12.5 gauge | Strainer handle, or a stretcher for the first pull | Inline strainer, one tension spring per fence | About 200 to 250 lb per wire |
| Standard barbed, 12.5 gauge | Bar stretcher, or come-along with wire grip | Wraps at a solid brace | Straight with no dip, not banjo tight (BC factsheet: about 250 lb) |
| Suspension barbed | Bar stretcher | Wraps at the brace | Max 3 in sag per 100 ft span (Texas NRCS) |
| Woven wire / field fence | Stretcher bar and chains | Tied off at the brace | About half the crimp pulled out |

## Heat, cold and blackland clay

The Texas NRCS spec puts it plainly: wire tightens in cold weather and expands in hot weather. Around Waco that swing is large. A strand stretched tight in a January cold snap hangs looser after an August afternoon, and a strand stretched tight in August pulls tighter still in a January freeze, right when an ice storm may hang extra weight on it.

That is a strong argument for strainers and springs on high-tensile fence. You can back off a click before winter and take it up in spring, and the spring absorbs part of the swing on its own. On barbed wire with no strainer, stretch to firm, not to the limit, so there is room for a cold snap.

The ground moves too. Blackland clay shrinks and cracks in a dry summer, swells when it rains, and pushes posts around with it. A corner that leans an inch toward the run takes tension out of every wire tied to it, and no amount of cranking on a strainer fixes a brace that is moving. If a fence keeps going slack within weeks of being tightened, check the braces before the wire.

## Working around a loaded wire

A tight fence wire stores real energy. When a strand or a grip lets go, the wire whips back along the line and curls.

- Wear leather gloves and eye protection every time, and long sleeves around barbed wire.
- Stand to the side of the wire you are pulling, never in line with it or behind the stretcher.
- Check the grip before you lean on the handle. A jaw set on a barb or a rusted spot can slide.
- Old, rusted wire can snap under a stretcher even when it looks sound. If it breaks in the first few clicks, it is done.

## When tightening is not the answer

Tensioners fix slack wire. They do not fix a leaning corner, rotted posts or wire rusted through. Retension a fence, find it loose again in a month, and something upstream has failed, most often a brace. Tightening harder only pulls that brace over faster.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an inline strainer used for?

An inline strainer is a ratchet drum that stays in the fence wire to hold and adjust tension. You wind the wire onto the drum with a strainer handle and a pawl locks it. It is mainly used on high-tensile smooth wire, placed near the middle of a long pull section so tension is even both ways, and it lets you tighten or loosen the wire later without cutting it.

### How tight should high-tensile fence wire be?

Extension and NRCS guidance puts high-tensile smooth wire at roughly 200 to 250 pounds of tension per wire. The usual method is to set one wire using a tension spring as the gauge, then bring the other wires up to match by feel and sound. Check the spring maker's marks, because calibration differs between brands.

### Can I use an inline strainer on barbed wire?

You can, and some people do so they can re-tension without a stretcher. It is awkward because the barbs catch on the drum as it winds. On most barbed wire fences, a bar-type stretcher or a come-along with a proper wire grip, followed by a good tie-off at a solid brace, is simpler and holds just as well.

### What is the difference between a fence stretcher and a wire tightener?

A fence stretcher is a pulling tool. It grips the wire, hooks to a post, takes the slack out, and then comes off the fence. A wire tightener such as an inline strainer is a holding device that stays on the wire and keeps the tension there. Most fences are built with a stretcher and held by a tightener or by the wraps at the brace.

### Do I need a tension spring on every wire?

No. The common practice is one tension spring on one wire per fence. That wire is set to the right tension using the spring as a gauge, and the other wires are matched to it by feel or sound. The spring often goes on the second wire, though putting it on the top wire gives extra give when a limb lands on the fence.
