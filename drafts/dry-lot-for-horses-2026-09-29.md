# Dry Lot for Horses: How to Size, Fence and Surface One in Central Texas

A dry lot for horses is a small fenced pen with no grass to speak of, where you hold horses on hay and water so the pasture can rest. People also call it a sacrifice area, because you give up that patch of ground to save the rest. Extension guidance puts the floor at 400 to 500 square feet per horse, and the lot needs a stronger fence than your pasture, footing that does not turn to soup, water, and shade.

Around Waco it earns its keep twice a year. Once in the wet months, when blackland clay will not carry a horse without getting torn up. Again in a dry July, when every bite a horse takes pulls the grass closer to the dirt.

## Why a dry lot pays for itself

### It saves the pasture

Oregon State's extension guide to [pasture-associated laminitis](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9354-horse-owners-guide-pasture-associated-laminitis) notes that grasses store their sugars in the lower 3 inches of the plant and tells owners to avoid overgrazing. That bottom 3 inches is what regrows the stand. Leave it and the pasture recovers after a rain. Graze it off and you are replanting.

A dry lot gives you somewhere to put horses when you pull them off. Without one, "resting the pasture" means nothing.

### It helps with founder risk

Easy keepers, overweight horses and horses that have foundered before are the ones that get in trouble on grass. The Oregon State guide tells owners to confine horses to dry lots or use a grazing muzzle when forage is high in sugar, and to put any horse showing foot soreness in a dry lot until it improves.

Two details from that guide matter in Texas. It reports that grass sugar runs lowest from about 3 a.m. to 10 a.m., and suggests confining at-risk horses from 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. It also says drought-stressed grass may carry more sugar than unstressed grass of the same kind. So a short, brown pasture in August is not automatically safe for a founder-prone horse. Your vet sets the plan for the horse. The dry lot is the tool that makes the plan possible.

### It gets you through drought

When the grass stops growing, horses keep eating what is left, right down to the roots. A dry lot lets you move them to hay early, before the pasture is gone. The same dry spells shift posts in clay ground, which is its own problem, covered in our piece on [drought fence problems](/blog/drought-fence-problems/).

## How big should a dry lot be?

Penn State's Ann Swinker, writing on [drylots for horses](https://horses.extension.org/drylots-for-horses/) for the extension horse network, gives a minimum of 400 to 500 square feet per horse. That is a floor. It gives a horse room to stand, eat and turn around. Horses living in it for weeks need more.

| Horses | Extension minimum (400 to 500 sq ft each) | A lot that works day to day (example) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 400 to 500 sq ft | 40 ft x 60 ft (2,400 sq ft) |
| 2 | 800 to 1,000 sq ft | 50 ft x 100 ft (5,000 sq ft) |
| 3 | 1,200 to 1,500 sq ft | 60 ft x 120 ft (7,200 sq ft) |
| 4 | 1,600 to 2,000 sq ft | 60 ft x 160 ft (9,600 sq ft) |

The right-hand column is our judgment, not an extension figure: roughly 2,400 square feet per horse. A 60 by 120 foot lot is about a sixth of an acre. Going larger is rarely a mistake, and our [horse pasture fencing layout guide](/blog/horse-pasture-fencing-layout/) talks about a half acre to an acre on a small place.

Two things change the number.

**Temperament.** A herd that gets along can share a tighter lot. A bossy gelding and a timid mare need room for the timid one to get away, and a second hay station so she can eat.

**Shape.** Long and narrow beats square. The same Penn State guidance puts water and the shed at opposite ends so horses walk between them. That spreads the wear. A square lot with the trough next to the hay rack gets beaten flat in one corner.

### What about paddock paradise?

Paddock paradise is the name people use for a track system. Instead of one open pen, you fence a lane around the edge of a pasture and the horses live on the track, with water at one point and hay at others so they keep moving. The middle can be grazed, cut for hay, or rested.

It works as a dry lot that happens to be long. The trade-off is fence: a track needs an inside line and an outside line, close to twice the footage of a simple pen. The outside line takes the pressure and should be built like a perimeter. The inside line can be lighter, often a visible electric tape. Keep the track wide enough for two horses to pass without one getting pinned.

## Fencing a dry lot: build it heavier than the pasture

People often fence the pasture properly, then throw a few cattle panels around the dry lot because it is small. That is backwards.

The dry lot is where the fence takes the most abuse. [Penn State's fence planning guide for horses](https://extension.psu.edu/fence-planning-for-horses) says strong, safe fencing belongs where horses congregate or crowd each other, near gates, feed, water and shelter, and that a lighter fence can do in very large pastures. A dry lot is all of those places at once. Horses stand at the fence waiting for hay, lean over it, and get pushed into it by whoever outranks them. The same guide recommends horse fence 54 to 60 inches above ground, with gates as tall as the fence.

### The three fences that hold up

**Welded pipe.** It does not stretch or sag, and a horse can lean on it all summer. Three or four rails, pipe posts set in concrete. It costs the most and is the one you are least likely to touch again. See our [pipe fence page](/pipe-fence-waco/).

**No-climb mesh with a pipe top rail.** A 2 by 4 inch mesh a hoof cannot go through, stretched tight, with a pipe rail across the top so horses leaning over do not push the wire down. This is the one we would point most small-acreage owners toward. Our [no-climb horse fence](/blog/no-climb-horse-fence/) article covers the mesh itself.

**No-climb mesh alone.** Fine in a pasture. In a dry lot, horses lean on the top edge until it rolls over. If you go this way, run a hot wire on offset brackets along the inside top.

What does not belong around a dry lot: barbed wire or field fence with wide openings. Penn State's dry lot guidance does list highly visible permanent electric as an inexpensive option, and with a small, quiet herd it can work. In a crowded lot with a pushy horse, we treat electric as a helper line, because a hot wire alone will not physically stop a horse that gets shoved into it.

### Corners, gates and posts

Penn State's guide says to eliminate corners and dead ends where more than one horse is kept, so a dominant horse cannot trap another. Round tight corners off or fence across them.

The Penn State dry lot guidance puts the lot right next to the pastures, with a gate opening into each one, so moving horses on and off grass is one gate, not a walk down the road. Put in one gate wide enough for a tractor with a hay spear or a skid steer, because you will be scraping manure and dropping bales in there for years. Add a walk gate near the barn. Penn State puts horse-and-handler gates at no less than 4 feet wide, with 5 preferred.

Gate posts and corners in blackland clay are where lots fail. The clay swells wet and shrinks dry, and a shallow post leans. Set pipe posts deep in concrete and brace where the wire pulls. Our guide to [setting posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) explains why.

## Footing and drainage in blackland clay

The fence is the easy part. The ground is what makes a dry lot a mud hole. Bare blackland clay turns to sticky, hoof-deep mud in a wet spell, then bakes into ruts hard enough to bruise a sole.

### Pick the high ground

The University of Kentucky's extension bulletin on [high traffic area pads for horses](https://publications.mgcafe.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/ID-164-High-Traffic-Area-Pads-for-Horses--Remediated.pdf) says to build on a summit rather than a slope where you can. Keep the lot away from ditches, ponds and the well, since manure piles up fast in a small space.

In Central Texas, add creek bottoms to the list. A low spot along a creek looks shady and handy until a flash flood comes through. Put the lot where water leaves, not where it arrives.

The Penn State dry lot guidance adds two drainage points: keep a strip of grass about 25 feet wide around the lot to filter runoff, and direct the water off the barn roof away from the lot. A lot set under a roof edge with no gutter will stay wet all winter.

### Build a pad where horses stand most

You may not need to surface the whole lot. The gate, the trough, the hay feeder and the shed go to mud first. Those get a pad. The Kentucky bulletin's method suits heavy clay:

1. Dig out the topsoil to stiff, stable subsoil. It says about 9 inches is typical.
2. Lay geotextile fabric so the rock does not sink into the clay and mud does not push up through it.
3. Spread about 6 inches of No. 4 or No. 2 crushed limestone.
4. Top it with 2 to 3 inches of dense graded aggregate, sloped slightly to shed water, and compact it with a smooth-drum roller.

The Penn State dry lot guidance lists a similar surface: crush and run under screenings, ground limestone or No. 78 gravel. That is dirt work, not fence work, so price it separately. But plan the fence around it, and set the gate where the pad will be.

Penn State also says to pick manure and old hay out of a dry lot every 1 to 3 days. Manure and wasted hay trampled into clay is how a dry lot becomes a swamp.

## Water and shade in a Texas summer

A dry lot is where a horse spends the hottest hours of the hottest months.

**Water.** One trough with a float valve is the minimum. With more than two horses, or a herd with a bully, put in a second so the low horse can drink. Keep troughs out of corners and on a pad. A trough set in a fence line can serve the lot and the pasture beside it from one water line.

**Shade.** A run-in shed is the usual answer. Penn State's sizing is 12 by 12 feet for 1 to 2 horses and 12 by 24 feet for 3 to 4. Horses in a small lot chew bark and pack the ground over roots, so do not count on one tree as your only shade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many square feet does a horse need in a dry lot?

Extension guidance puts the minimum at 400 to 500 square feet per horse. That is standing and eating room, not living room. For horses that stay in the lot for days or weeks, we plan on several times that, around 2,000 to 2,500 square feet per horse, so they can move and a low-ranking horse can get away from a bossy one.

### What is the difference between a dry lot and a sacrifice area?

Most people use the two names for the same thing: a small fenced area where horses are held off pasture and fed hay. "Sacrifice area" describes what happens to the ground, which gets given up to protect the pasture. "Dry lot" describes the pen. Either way it needs strong fence, water, shade and footing that stands up to traffic.

### What is the best fence for a horse dry lot?

Welded pipe is the toughest and the most expensive. No-climb 2x4 mesh with a pipe top rail is a strong middle option that keeps hooves out of the wire and stops horses leaning the top edge down. Plain mesh can work with a hot wire on the inside. Barbed wire and loose field fence do not belong around a dry lot.

### Can I keep a foundered horse in a dry lot?

Oregon State University Extension recommends confining horses to a dry lot when grass is high in sugar, and putting any horse showing foot soreness in a dry lot until it improves. The lot is a management tool, not a treatment. Your vet and farrier should set the plan for the horse, including how much hay and what kind.

### How do I keep a dry lot from turning to mud?

Put it on high ground, away from creeks and drainage paths. Build rock pads at the gate, water, feeder and shed: dig out the topsoil, lay geotextile fabric, add crushed limestone, then top it with fine compacted gravel. Pick up manure and wasted hay every one to three days so it does not get trampled into the clay.
