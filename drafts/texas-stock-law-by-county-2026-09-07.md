# Texas Stock Laws by County: Who Has to Fence, and Where

A fellow west of Crawford called us one March after his neighbor's cows spent three nights in his winter pasture. He wanted to know who owed him a fence. The answer started with a question he had never once thought about in twenty years of owning that place: does the stock law where his land sits cover cattle, or does it not?

That is the whole game with Texas stock laws by county. Texas starts from open range, where the burden is on you to fence animals out. A county stock law flips that and makes the animal's owner keep them in. Which rule applies to your dirt depends on an election that probably happened before your grandfather was born.

We build fence in McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties, not law. Nothing here is legal advice. Talk to an attorney about your specific tract before you rely on any of it, because these records are old and they are local.

## Open Range Is the Default and a Stock Law Is the Exception

Texas never adopted the fence-in rule that most states use. Under Texas common law, a livestock owner has no general duty to keep animals from wandering off. If you want your neighbor's cattle out of your hay meadow, the default rule says build a fence that keeps them out.

Then the Legislature gave local voters a way out of that. Under Chapter 143 of the Texas Agriculture Code, a county or a subdivision of a county could hold an election to make it unlawful for certain animals to run at large. Where one of those passed, the range is closed and the burden moves to the animal's owner.

Most of the settled counties in Central Texas closed the range on cattle generations ago. That does not mean yours did, and it does not mean it covers every animal. You can read the statute yourself on the [Texas Legislature's site](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/AG/htm/AG.143.htm), which is a short read and worth twenty minutes if you run stock.

## Texas Stock Laws by County Are Not Something You Can Download

Landowners ask us for a map. There isn't one, at least not one you should bet a lawsuit on. What exists is a pile of county-level records going back to the 1890s, some of them handwritten in a clerk's minute book.

Three things make the county-by-county picture messier than people expect.

The elections were animal specific. One vote might cover cattle and horses and say nothing about hogs, sheep, or goats. Plenty of counties held separate elections years apart for different animals.

They were not always countywide. The law allowed subdivisions, so a stock law can apply to one commissioner precinct, or to an area described by old survey lines and roads that no longer exist by that name.

And they stayed on the books. A stock law adopted in 1912 is still the rule unless a later election repealed it, so age tells you nothing about whether it applies.

Every online list of closed-range counties we have seen carries a disclaimer, and it should. Treat those lists as a starting point and nothing more.

## How to Check the Stock Law in Your County and Precinct

This is a phone call and a courthouse visit, not a research project. Here is the order that works.

Start with the county clerk. Ask for the commissioners court minutes and any recorded stock law elections for your precinct, and ask which animals each one covered. Some clerks have this indexed and will read it to you over the phone.

Call the sheriff's office next. Deputies handle loose-stock calls every week and they generally know how the county treats them, which is useful even though it is not a legal opinion.

Then check your deed and survey against the precinct boundaries. If your tract straddles a line, you can genuinely have two answers on one piece of property.

If money or liability is on the table, pay an attorney or a title company to pull the records and give you a written answer. Texas A&M AgriLife's [Texas Agriculture Law blog](https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/) is the best free plain-English resource on this subject, and it will save you time before you ever call a lawyer.

## Highways Have Their Own Rule, Stock Law or Not

Here is the part that catches people. Separate from any county stock law, the Agriculture Code makes it an offense to knowingly permit livestock to run at large on a highway. That applies whether the range around you is open or closed.

The catch is the definition. For that section, the code defines highway narrowly, as U.S. and state highways. Numbered farm-to-market roads and county roads are treated differently, and Texas courts have declined to read a general duty to fence into those roads for open-range areas.

So the honest summary is this. Frontage on a U.S. or state highway carries a duty that does not depend on your county's stock law. Frontage on an FM road or a county road depends on the local stock law and on the facts of the wreck. Either way, a jury looking at a night-time collision will hear about the condition of your fence.

A stock law does not build fence and it does not decide who pays for a boundary fence. That is a separate question with its own rules, covered in [who pays for the fence between neighbors in Texas](/blog/who-pays-for-fence-between-neighbors-texas/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I find out if my county has a stock law?

Call the county clerk and ask for recorded stock law elections covering your precinct, and ask which animals each one named. The sheriff's office can tell you how loose-stock calls are handled in practice. For anything with money on it, have an attorney or title company pull the records and put the answer in writing.

### Does a stock law mean my neighbor has to pay for the boundary fence?

No. A stock law governs whether animals may run at large. Who pays for a shared fence is a separate matter that usually comes down to agreements, prior use, and what the two of you work out. Get any cost-sharing arrangement in writing before the first post goes in.

### Is Texas open range or closed range?

Both, depending on where you stand. The statewide default is open range, and counties or precincts that passed stock laws are closed for the animals those elections named. That is why there is no single reliable map for the whole state.

### What fence do I need if my county is closed range?

The law does not specify a fence type. It says keep your animals in, so build what your stock actually respects: five-strand barbed wire at $2.50 to $5.50 a foot for cattle on pasture, net wire at $3.50 to $8 for small stock and horses, pipe at $12 to $28 where the pressure is highest.
