# How to Hang a Farm Gate Right the First Time

A farm gate is only as good as the post it hangs on. To hang a tube or pipe gate so it stays level, set a heavy hinge post at least 3 feet deep (4 is better in our clay), brace it back into the fence line, mount the hinges as far apart as the gate allows, and block the gate at its final height before you tighten anything. Set the latch last, after the gate has hung a day or two.

Most gates that drag by their second summer were hung on a line post, set shallow, or left unbraced. None of that shows the day you hang it. It shows after a few hundred swings and one dry August on blackland clay.

## Plan the opening before you buy the gate

Size the gate to the widest thing that will ever go through it. Penn State Extension's [fence planning guide for horses](https://extension.psu.edu/fence-planning-for-horses) puts vehicle and tractor gates at 12 feet minimum, up to 16, and walk-through gates at no less than 4 feet with 5 preferred. The University of Georgia's [Fences for Horses](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) bulletin says a pasture gate should be 14 to 16 feet if equipment needs in. On a cattle place, picture a shredder, a hay trailer, or a gooseneck making the turn off the road. Nobody regrets a 16-foot opening.

Settle three more things on paper:

- **Hinge side.** Put the hinges where the swing clears the ground and the latch ends up where you stand. If you open it from a truck, the latch wants to be on the driver's side as you pull up.
- **Swing direction.** One way or both. Swinging into the pasture keeps the gate off the road side.
- **Swing room.** Walk the full arc. A 16-foot gate sweeps a quarter circle with a 16-foot radius, and any rise in that ground is where the latch end will hit dirt.
- **Location.** For cattle, University of Tennessee Extension's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) advises putting gates in corners rather than the middle of a straight fence, so stock move through easily. Penn State leans the other way for horses led in one at a time, so a horse is not trapped in a corner by the gate.

Then measure the actual gate end to end, tape on steel, not the number on the tag, and set the post spacing from that plus the hinge offset and latch gap the hardware calls for. Two inches off means a gate that will not latch or a gap a calf can work through.

## Size and set the hinge post

The hinge post carries the whole gate as a lever. Hang a 16-foot gate off a 4-inch line post and it leans a little more every week. This is the one post on the place worth overbuilding.

The NRCS Texas fence specification (Practice Code 382), which Texas A&M AgriLife Extension hosts as a [PDF on its Leon County site](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf), sets the minimums for cost-share fence work. Gate posts are the uprights of an end/gate brace assembly: wood with at least a 6-inch top diameter or steel at least 2 3/8 inches outside diameter, buried at least 3 feet unless driven. Driven pipe from 2 7/8 to 4 1/2 inches goes 4 feet.

Those are minimums for cost-share work, not targets. The Tennessee bulletin sets the bar higher for wood, saying corner and gate posts should be at least 8 inches in diameter. Here is how we would size a hinge post.

| Gate | Hinge post we would use | Depth |
|---|---|---|
| 4 to 6 ft walk gate | 2 3/8 in pipe or 6 in wood | 3 ft |
| 10 to 12 ft tube gate | 2 7/8 in pipe, or 6 in wood at minimum and 8 in better | 3.5 to 4 ft |
| 14 to 16 ft tube gate | 2 7/8 in pipe at minimum, 3 1/2 or 4 1/2 in better, or 8 in wood | 4 ft |
| 16 ft heavy pipe gate or driveway gate | 4 1/2 in pipe or larger | 4 ft or more |

Cap every pipe post. The spec requires steel pipe to be permanently capped to keep rainwater out, and an open pipe full of water rusts from the inside at the ground line where you cannot see it.

### Blackland clay versus rock

Along the I-35 corridor around Waco, Lorena and West, and east toward Marlin, the hinge post is fighting shrink-swell blackland clay. It grips a post when wet, cracks away from it in August, and heaves it in between. Go deeper rather than wider, and do not trust a shallow post in a big plug of concrete. The NRCS spec calls for a concreted hole at least 12 inches across, and on blackland soils it allows the concrete to stop 12 inches below the surface with soil backfilled on top. Our guide to [setting posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) explains why the clay moves.

West of the Brazos, toward Crawford, McGregor and Valley Mills and up into Bosque County, you often hit limestone or caliche a foot down. Driving a big pipe 4 feet is not happening there. You drill with a rock auger, set the post in concrete, and let the brace do more of the work. Our [rocky ground post guide](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/) covers the equipment.

## Brace both sides of the opening

A gate opening is two fence ends. The NRCS Texas spec requires end/gate brace assemblies on both sides of every gate opening and says gates attach directly to a brace assembly post. So the hinge post is the upright of a brace, not a lone post with a gate on it.

That post gets pulled two ways. The fence wire pulls it away from the opening. The gate pulls the top toward the opening and pushes the bottom away. An H-brace set back into the fence line resists both. For steel, the spec calls for a horizontal brace 6 feet long inside the uprights, set no lower than two-thirds of the top wire height, welded and painted. On a pipe fence, a top rail welded solid to the hinge post and the next post down often does that job.

The latch post needs its own brace. It holds the fence tension on its side and takes a hit every time a cow shoves the gate. Our [H-brace guide](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) walks through building one.

## Pick hinges that fit the post

Most tube gates come with a hinge set: a pin that mounts to the post and a strap or collar on the gate that drops over it.

- **Lag (screw-in) hinges, wood posts.** The pin screws into a pilot hole. Quick and adjustable, since you turn it in or out to move the gate. Fine on a sound 6-inch post under a light or medium gate. Drill the pilot square to the post, or the gate hangs crooked forever.
- **Bolt-through hinges, wood posts.** The pin passes through the post with a nut and washer on the back, so the load bears on the far face instead of on threads in wood. This is the better choice for any 14 or 16 foot gate on wood.
- **Weld-on hinges, pipe posts.** Grind the galvanizing off the weld area first and keep the fumes blowing away from you, since burning zinc makes fumes you do not want to breathe. Tack both pins, hang the gate, check it, then finish the welds and paint them.
- **Clamp-on hinges, pipe posts.** They bolt around the pipe with no welding, handy when no welder is around or the gate may move later. Keep the bolts tight. A clamp that creeps down the post is a sagging gate.

Whatever the type, spread the hinges as far apart as the gate frame allows, because wider spacing gives the gate less leverage on the top pin. Point the top pin down and the bottom pin up so the gate cannot be lifted straight off by a bull, a horse or a thief. And match the pin diameter to the gate strap; a loose fit wears into slop within a season.

## Hanging it, step by step

Use two people. A 16-foot gate hung alone is how fingers get smashed.

1. **Let the posts sit.** Let concrete cure and tamped dirt settle before the gate goes on.
2. **Mount the bottom hinge** at a height that gives the clearance you want under the gate (see below), measured to the bottom rail, not to the strap.
3. **Block the gate at final height**, on blocks or a jack at both ends, bottom rail level. Give the latch end half an inch to an inch of extra lift, because every gate settles some.
4. **Drop the strap over the bottom pin**, then mark the top hinge straight above it.
5. **Mount the top hinge and seat the gate.** Put a level on the gate's hinge-side upright. You want it plumb both ways.
6. **Pull the blocks and swing it.** It should hold still anywhere in the swing. If it drifts open or shut, the hinge line is not plumb.
7. **Adjust, then tighten everything** and check it again.

## Set the swing and the ground clearance

If both hinge pins sit dead in line vertically, the gate balances anywhere and stays where you leave it. Tilt that line and the gate always swings to one resting spot, wherever it hangs lowest. A self-closing tilt is fine on a small walk gate. On a 16-foot gate it swings a heavy gate into you or your horse, so keep it plumb and let the latch hold it.

For clearance, the latch end has to clear the highest ground in its arc, plus a little for settling and the next rain. On flat ground that is a few inches. On a slope you either grade the arc level or swing the gate downhill.

Clearance is also where stock and hogs get out. A calf or goat will work under a gap you would call small, and feral hogs will root out any low spot under a gate. In hog country, a threshold across the opening on the gate line helps: a buried pipe, a treated timber or a short concrete sill. It keeps washouts and rooting from opening the gap. Ask the hog question before you pick the hinge height.

## Set the latch last

Most latch trouble on a new gate comes from setting the latch before the gate settled.

- **Wait.** Let the gate hang a day or two, swing it a few dozen times, then set the latch where the gate actually sits.
- **Add a stop on the latch post** if the gate opens one way only, so cattle push against the post rather than the chain or spring latch.
- **Set it for how you use it.** Penn State's guidance is that a properly designed gate can be unlocked, opened, shut and locked with one hand. From horseback or a truck window, that may mean a higher chain or a different latch.
- **Close the gaps.** The Georgia bulletin warns against gaps between a gate and its posts, where a hoof or a foal's head can get caught.
- **Padlock road gates.** The latch is for cattle. The lock is for people.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep should a gate post be set?

At least 3 feet, which is the minimum in the NRCS Texas fence specification for gate and brace posts. For a 14 or 16 foot gate we set the hinge post about 4 feet. In blackland clay, depth counts for more than a big plug of concrete, because the clay shrinks and swells around the top of the post. In rock you drill, concrete the post, and lean harder on the brace.

### What size post do I need for a 16 foot gate?

For a 16 foot tube gate, use at least 2 7/8 inch steel pipe or an 8 inch treated or cedar post, set about 4 feet deep and braced into the fence. The NRCS Texas spec allows a 6 inch wood gate post, but University of Tennessee Extension calls for at least 8 inches on corner and gate posts, and a long gate is where that matters. A heavy pipe gate or a driveway gate wants 4 1/2 inch pipe or larger.

### Which way should gate hinges face?

Point the top hinge pin down and the bottom hinge pin up. With the pins opposed, the gate cannot be lifted straight off its hinges by livestock or by someone who wants to take it.

### Does a gate post need a brace?

Yes, for any tube or pipe gate of real length. The NRCS Texas specification requires end/gate brace assemblies on both sides of every gate opening and calls for gates to hang directly on a brace assembly post. The brace keeps fence tension and gate weight from pulling the hinge post over, and the latch post needs one as well.

### How much gap should be under a farm gate?

Enough for the latch end to clear the highest ground in its swing, plus a little for settling. On flat ground that is a few inches. Keep it as small as the ground allows if you run calves or goats or have feral hogs, and consider a buried pipe, timber or concrete sill across the opening so rooting and washouts cannot open the gap.
