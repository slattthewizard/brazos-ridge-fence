# Cattle Guard Wings: How to Tie Your Fence Into the Guard and Add a Bypass Gate

A cattle guard only stops cattle across the width of the pit. Everything beside the pit is the wings' job. Wings are the fence panels or walls that run from each end of the guard back to your fence line, and they have to be tight, braced and as tall as the fence they join. If they aren't, cattle skip the guard and walk around it.

So when a guard seems not to work, check the wings first. Often the rails are fine, the pit is open, and a cow has found a gap at the end post. ## What cattle guard wings actually do

The guard is a gap in the ground that cattle won't cross. Wings close off the only other way through, which is the ground beside it. Without them you have a pit in the middle of an open drive.

Wings do three jobs:

- **Close the sides.** The fence has to meet the guard with no space big enough for a calf's head, a hog's snout or a cow's shoulder.
- **Carry the fence load.** At the guard the fence stops being a continuous line. Wire tension has to go somewhere, which is why the post at each wing end is built like a fence corner.
- **Guide traffic.** A wing that flares back from the guard also steers a trailer toward the middle of the pit instead of the edge.

The U.S. Forest Service range specifications describe it the same way. A manufactured metal guard sits over a pit with treated timbers, concrete or rock on either side, the ends are left open for drainage, and end-barriers connect the guard to the fence ([Specifications for Structural Range Improvements, USDA Forest Service PNW-GTR-250](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/26287.pdf)). Notice the open ends. The pit needs to drain and you need to be able to clean it, so the wing ties in to the guard's sides, not across the ends of the pit.

## Wing styles: panels, walls and pipe

There are three common builds, and each one fits a different entrance.

### Pipe or welded wire panel wings

Pipe wings are the common choice on ranch entrances around Waco. Pipe posts set in concrete at the guard end, with rails or a welded panel running back to the fence line. They take a trailer strike better than wire, they don't sag, and they match a pipe fence or a pipe entrance. If the rest of the entrance is pipe, the wings should be too.

### Wire wings on wood or steel posts

Where the fence is barbed or net wire, wings can be built the same way as the fence, with a braced post at the guard end and wire stretched back. This is the cheapest wing and it works for cattle. The weak spot is the bottom wire. Wire wings sag and open a gap at the ground, and that is exactly where hogs and calves go.

### Poured wing walls

Concrete wing walls are the heaviest option. They don't leave a ground gap and they shed water, and they cost more and are harder to change later. Walls make sense on a busy drive where wings get hit often, or where a washout has to be held back.

Whichever you pick, match the wing height to the fence it joins. A 5-wire cattle fence meeting a 3-foot wing has a step in it, and cattle read that step as a way over.

## Tying the fence into the guard

This is where wings fail, so build the tie-in like a fence corner.

### Brace the post at each wing end

Every wing ends in a post that takes the pull of the fence. Treat it as an end post. The University of Tennessee's fence guide says corner and gate posts should be at least 8 inches in diameter and that a corner post needs a brace assembly for each fence leading to it ([UT Extension PB1541, Planning and Building Fences on the Farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf)). A wing end post with wire pulling on it is the same situation. Our post on [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) explains why a bare post leans and a braced one doesn't.

On blackland clay, the shrink-swell cycle works a post loose and leans it toward the pull. West of the Brazos, in limestone and caliche, the problem is getting depth at all. Either way, the guard end is the last place you want a shallow post.

### Close the gap at the guard

The wire or panel should meet the guard rail frame or the guard's own wing plate. Leave no space you could put a fist through. Check three spots:

- the top, where the last wire meets the guard frame
- the ends, where the wing meets the first fence line post
- the bottom, where the wing meets the ground

The bottom is usually the one that's missed. Ground slopes toward a drive, the wing is set straight, and a gap opens that a calf slides through. Fill it with a bottom rail, a buried apron or a short wall, not loose wire.

### Flare, don't butt

Run the wings back from the guard at an angle instead of square off the end. A common builder's rule of thumb is to flare each wing so the entrance narrows toward the guard, which funnels traffic to the middle and keeps trailers off the end posts. There's no single Texas standard for the angle or length, so set it to your drive: the swing of the longest trailer that comes in, the slope, and where the fence line already sits. A gentle flare that stays inside your property line works better than a sharp angle that throws wing posts into the bar ditch. If your fence runs beside a county road, check that you're building inside your own line before you set anything. Get the road right-of-way line pinned down first.

## Wing height and length

Height is simple. Make the wings the same height as your fence, and not shorter. For cattle, that is usually whatever your top wire runs at. For horses, goats or a high-fence property, it is the taller number, because a guard is not a barrier for deer or for a determined horse.

Length is a judgment call. The wing has to be long enough that a cow cannot walk around the end of it and has to reach a braced post you can trust. Short wings that stop a few feet from the guard are the common mistake. The animal walks along the guard, finds the end and steps past it.

| Feature | What to aim for |
|---|---|
| Wing height | Same as the fence it joins |
| Gap at ground | None you could slide a calf or hog through |
| End post | Braced like a corner, not a line post |
| Pit ends | Open for drainage, not closed with fill |
| Wing attachment | Tied to the guard frame or its wing plate |

## Add a bypass gate

A guard handles daily vehicle traffic. It doesn't handle:

- a horse you're leading across
- cattle you're moving on foot
- a hay baler or implement wider than the guard
- the day the pit is being cleaned out

Plan the gate when you build the wings, because it shares the wing's end post. The Forest Service specifications say a gate should be constructed close to every cattle guard so livestock and wildlife can get through ([PNW-GTR-250](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/26287.pdf)). In practice that means one gate, hung beside the guard and tied to the same braced post as the wing.

Some things we'd check on a bypass gate:

- **Width.** Match it to the widest thing that will go through. See our guide to [farm gate sizes and types](/blog/farm-gate-sizes-and-types/) for common widths.
- **Latch.** Horses and cattle will test a chain. A gate beside a guard gets pushed on more than most.
- **Hinge post.** It carries the swing weight and the wing pull. Make it your biggest, best-set post.
- **Swing direction.** Hang it so it opens away from the pit and doesn't strike the wing or the guard.
- **Habit.** A gate left open turns the guard into decoration, so latch it every time.

The gate also keeps the guard from being used for the wrong job. Stock that are being moved should go through the gate, not be pushed over the rails.

## Common wing failures

### Cattle walking the wing edge

If cattle are stepping around the end of a guard, look at the end of the wing. It is too short, it has drifted away from the guard, or the last panel has been knocked loose. Walk it from the pasture side. Cattle go around when they find a spot where the ground, the post and the panel line up to make a gap.

### Washout at the ends

The guard's open pit ends are designed to drain, and so water finds them. After a flash flood or a hard rain the ground beside a wing can wash out and leave a hollow under the panel. That hollow is a gap. Check wing ends after heavy rain. Fixing it usually means compacted fill, rock or a short wall, and making sure the culvert or crown of the drive isn't throwing water onto the wing.

### Hogs rooting the bottom

Hogs work a loose bottom edge until it opens. Wings on a guard have a lot of loose edge: a post, a panel and a pit in the same spot. Keep the bottom tight to the ground or tie in an apron.

### Leaning wing posts

A wing post that leans in toward the pit usually has no brace, a shallow set or both. Resetting a post is $45 to $120 per post, and rebuilding a corner or H-brace runs $600 to $1,400. See our [fence repair](/fence-repair-waco/) page for the ranges.

### Trailer strikes

Wings get hit. A trailer swinging in off a county road clips the wing end, bends a panel and knocks a post out of line. That's the case for pipe over wire, and for setting wing end posts back from the traffic line.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are cattle guard wings?

Wings are the panels, rails or walls that run from each end of a cattle guard back to your fence line. They close off the ground beside the pit so stock can't walk around it. A guard without sound wings only protects the width of the pit, and cattle will find the edge.

### How tall should cattle guard wings be?

Match the height of the fence they join. If your cattle fence is 4 feet at the top wire, the wing should be 4 feet too. A shorter wing creates a step in the line, and horses, goats or a high-fence property need wings at their taller fence height.

### Do I need a bypass gate next to a cattle guard?

Yes, in most cases. A guard won't pass stock on foot, wide implements or a horse you're leading, and it needs a way around when the pit is being cleaned. The Forest Service range specifications recommend a gate close to every cattle guard. See [farm gate sizes and types](/blog/farm-gate-sizes-and-types/) for widths.

### Can cattle go around a cattle guard?

They can if the wings are short, gapped at the ground, or knocked out of line. Cattle that won't cross the rails will test the edges. A tight, braced wing at each end, with no gap at the bottom, is what keeps them from stepping around.
