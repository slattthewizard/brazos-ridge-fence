# Fence on Leased Land in Texas: Who Builds It, Who Fixes It, Who Keeps It

On leased land, the lease decides who builds the fence, who repairs it and who owns it when the lease ends. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension says the same thing in its grazing lease materials: a lease should name who inspects and repairs fences, who pays for parts and labor, and whether the tenant can build improvements at all. If your lease is silent, you're negotiating after the wire is already down.

## Why the fence is the weak spot in most leases

A pasture lease hands the tenant exclusive use of the ground. The AgriLife leasing handbook notes that, unless the landowner reserves the right, the landowner grants exclusive possession of the property to the lessee. The fence is the one piece of that property that both people care about for different reasons. The landowner wants the asset kept up. The tenant wants cattle, horses or goats to stay in.

Fences wear out on their own schedule. A corner loosens in blackland clay, a cedar falls across a line after an ice storm. In a handshake deal each side usually assumes the other person fixes it.

Texas A&M's guidance leans hard on one point: put it in writing. The AgriLife Texas Grazing Lease Checklist (EAG-007) says all agricultural leases should be in writing to be enforceable, to record the parties' understanding and to protect both sides. The same checklist notes that Texas requires a lease of real property lasting for more than one year to be in writing to be an enforceable contract. The handbook cites Chapter 26 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code for that rule. From a fence standpoint, a written lease is the only way a "who fixes what" answer survives a bad winter.

## What AgriLife says a lease should cover on fences

The [AgriLife grazing lease checklist](https://mytexas.ag.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Texas-Grazing-Lease-Checklist.pdf) and the [Ranchers' Agricultural Leasing Handbook](https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/files/2021/01/Ranchers-Agricultural-Leasing-Handbook.pdf) cover fences in several places.

### Inspection and repair

Both documents say a lease should address who is responsible for inspecting and repairing fences, particularly where the leased property abuts a highway. They also say the lease should set out which party makes the inspections and how often. That's the part most handshake deals skip: not just who fixes the fence, but who walks it and how often.

Walking the line is the cheap half of maintenance. For the walk itself, our [ranch fence maintenance checklist](/blog/ranch-fence-maintenance-checklist/) lays it out by season.

### Parts and labor

The handbook's sample grazing lease shows one way to split it. In the sample, if repairs to fences are necessary, the lessor provides the materials and the lessee provides the labor, and the lessee agrees to reasonably inspect perimeter fences and notify the lessor if repairs are necessary. That's a sample clause, not a rule.

A tenant with a post driver can reset a few leaning posts. A rebuilt corner is a different job. Our [repair page](/fence-repair-waco/) lists typical Central Texas ranges: most repair calls run $400 to $2,500, resetting or replacing posts runs $45 to $120 per post, and rebuilding a corner or H-brace runs $600 to $1,400. If a lease says "the tenant maintains the fence," write down what that means. Is a $900 storm repair maintenance, or the landowner's?

### Building new fence and improvements

The checklist says a lease should address whether the lessee has the right to erect improvements, and it names fences and pens among the examples (along with buildings and water delivery systems). It says permanent improvements generally stay on the land after the lease ends, so a tenant who builds a half mile of barbed wire on someone else's ground should assume the landowner keeps it unless the lease says otherwise. For that reason a landowner may want input on location and specifications, and some leases require written permission first. The checklist's advice is to specify whether the lessee has the right to remove improvements at the end of the lease, and to set a deadline for removal. The handbook's sample grazing lease adds a simple version: before erecting permanent improvements, the lessee gets written consent from the lessor.

So, can you build a fence on leased land? The lease decides. If it says nothing, get a signed answer before you set a post.

### Damage to fences

Both documents suggest the lease prohibit damage to the property and require the lessee to repair or pay for damage, including harm to fences, gates or improvements. The checklist also suggests a security deposit to cover damage to the property, improvements, fences, crops or livestock.

### Gates and breachy animals

The checklist suggests requiring that gates be kept closed at all times, and making the lessee liable for the death or injury of other livestock, or damage to a third party, caused by a gate left open. It also suggests removing any "breachy" animal that frequently escapes by jumping or breaking through fences. The sample lease turns that into a count: if an animal is found outside the pasture a set number of times, the lessor may request its removal.

## Highway frontage is where the stakes jump

The checklist and handbook both flag fences where the property abuts a highway. The reason is in AgriLife's [Landowner's Guide to Fence Law in Texas](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Texas-Fencing-Law-Information.pdf) (EAG-029). That guide says the Texas Agriculture Code states that a person who owns or has responsibility for the control of a horse, mule, donkey, cow, bull, steer, hog, sheep or goat may not knowingly permit the animal to traverse or roam at large, unattended, on the right-of-way of a highway. It says that to keep livestock off interstates and state highways, it is the landowner's responsibility to build and maintain a fence along an interstate or state highway.

The same guide has a section titled "Lessee Liability." It says many Texas livestock producers lease the land they run livestock on, which raises the question of who is responsible for fencing: the landowner or the lessee. And it says that absent an agreement allocating responsibility between the two, these laws could apply to both the landowner and the lessee who runs the livestock.

That sentence is the reason to write the fence clause carefully. If a gap in the highway fence is nobody's job on paper, these laws could reach both of you. Our post on [cattle on the road and liability](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/) covers the liability side.

## Hunting leases and deer leases

The handbook treats hunting leases separately. Its sample hunting lease has the lessee take proper care of the property and improvements, and be liable for damage to domestic livestock, fences or other property of the lessor caused by the lessee or guests. It says the lessee leaves all gates as found and uses proper care in crossing fences. It also requires the lessee to remove blinds and tree stands when the lease ends. Its guidance also says to address whether the lessee can erect improvements such as deer blinds, feeders or tree stands.

The sample doesn't say who builds or pays for a boundary fence on a hunting lease, so the lease you write has to. If the plan involves high fence for deer or exotics, it's a bigger conversation than a gate clause. High fence is permanent and expensive, so price it on the [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page before anyone signs: 8-foot game fence runs $4.50 to $10 per foot, $25,000 to $50,000 per mile.

## What to put in a lease before anyone touches a post

A builder's list drawn from the AgriLife items above. Your attorney will turn it into language.

1. **Who inspects fences, and how often.** Name the person and the interval.
2. **Who pays for materials and who provides labor** on routine repairs. Consider a dollar line: the tenant handles small repairs, the landowner anything bigger.
3. **Who fixes highway-frontage fence,** and how fast.
4. **Whether the tenant can build new fence,** with written consent, location and specs approved first.
5. **What happens to improvements at the end:** stay, go, or get bought out, and the deadline if they go.
6. **Gates:** closed at all times, who holds keys, who is liable if one is left open.
7. **Damage:** tenant repairs or pays for damage they or their animals or guests cause.
8. **Breachy animals:** how many escapes before an animal is removed.
9. **Condition at the start.** Walk the whole perimeter together and write down what you find. A photo log with dates beats memory.

Item 9 is a builder's suggestion, not from the AgriLife documents. A fence that was tired when the lease started shouldn't become the tenant's repair bill in month four.

## Money: what a lease fence actually costs

Before a tenant agrees to "maintain the fence," run the numbers. Our published ranges for a 5-strand barbed wire fence are $2.50 to $5.50 per foot, which works out to $13,200 to $29,000 per mile. A quarter mile (1,320 feet) comes to $3,300 to $7,260. A half mile (2,640 feet) comes to $6,600 to $14,520. Fence line clearing runs $1 to $3 per foot, and heavy cedar can run past that. Spending that on fence the landowner keeps, when the lease isn't renewed, is a real loss.

Practical points from the building side:

- **Term matters.** A short lease rarely justifies new perimeter fence. A long one, with a rent credit or written payback, might.
- **Cheap and removable beats permanent** if the tenant is paying and might not stay. Drive-and-pull t-posts and wire move with the tenant. Posts set in concrete don't. See our [barbed wire fence page](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/) for what a standard cattle fence involves.
- **Clear first.** On an overgrown line, [fence line clearing](/fence-line-clearing-waco/) is often the first bill. Decide up front who covers it.
- **Ag valuation is a separate question.** Our [ag exemption fencing post](/blog/ag-exemption-fencing-texas/) covers that angle.

A lease between landowner and tenant doesn't change what either owes the adjoining owner. Our post on [who pays for a fence between neighbors](/blog/who-pays-for-fence-between-neighbors-texas/) covers that side.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is responsible for fence repair on leased land in Texas?
The lease decides. AgriLife's grazing lease checklist says a lease should address who is responsible for inspecting and repairing fences, which party inspects, and how often. Its sample lease has the landowner supplying materials and the tenant supplying labor, but that is only one example. If the lease is silent, talk to an attorney before a repair turns into a dispute.

### Can a tenant build a fence on leased land?
Only if the lease allows it. AgriLife says a lease should address whether the lessee may erect improvements, and some leases require the landowner's written permission first. It also says permanent improvements generally stay on the land after the lease ends, so a tenant who builds should get the removal and payback terms in writing.

### Who owns a fence a tenant built when the lease ends?
Generally, permanent improvements stay on the land after the lease terminates, according to the AgriLife checklist. The same document says to specify whether the lessee can remove improvements at the end and to set a deadline. If you plan to take a fence with you, write that into the lease before building.

### Does a grazing lease have to be in writing?
AgriLife says all agricultural leases should be in writing, and that Texas requires a lease of real property lasting more than one year to be in writing to be an enforceable contract. For a shorter lease, ask an attorney. Either way, a written fence clause is the best protection for both sides.

### Who is liable if leased cattle get out onto a highway?
The AgriLife fence law guide says that absent an agreement allocating responsibility between landowner and lessee, the fence laws could apply to both. It also says state law bars knowingly permitting livestock to roam on a highway right-of-way. Put the highway fence duty in the lease and have an attorney review it. Our post on [cattle on the road](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/) goes deeper.

### Do hunting leases cover fences too?
They can. The handbook's sample hunting lease makes the lessee liable for damage to fences caused by the lessee or guests, requires gates left as found, and requires blinds and tree stands removed at the end. Whether the hunters or the landowner build or pay for boundary fence is something the lease has to say.
