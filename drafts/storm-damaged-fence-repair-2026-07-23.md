# Storm Damaged Fence Repair: What to Do in the First 24 Hours

The wind finally dies down, you step outside, and there's a 60-foot live oak laying across 200 feet of your barbed wire. Your cows are already eyeballing the gap like somebody left the gate open on purpose.

What you do in the next 24 hours decides whether this is a $600 fix or a $6,000 headache with a highway liability problem stacked on top. We've handled storm damaged fence repair all over McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties for years, and the ranchers who come out of a storm cheapest all follow the same basic playbook. Here it is, hour by hour.

## Hour One: Find Your Cattle Before You Worry About the Fence

Fences can wait a few hours. Loose cattle can't wait ten minutes, especially if your place fronts a farm-to-market road.

Before you touch a single wire, do a head count. Drive or ride your pastures and confirm every animal is where it belongs. If something's missing, check the obvious escape routes first: water gaps, corners near downed trees, and any stretch of fence that was already sagging before the storm. We wrote a whole guide on [what to do when cattle get out](/blog/cattle-getting-out-of-fence/), but the short version is simple. Get them contained first, in any pen or pasture that still holds, even if it's not where they normally live.

This matters more than most folks realize. Texas is technically open range, but most of Central Texas sits under local stock laws, and a jury can still find you negligent if your cattle cause a wreck and you knew your fence was down. We break down the legal side in our post on [cattle on the road and liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/). The one-sentence takeaway: once you know the fence is open, the clock is running on you to do something about it.

## Hours Two to Four: Walk the Line and Assess the Storm Fence Damage

Once your animals are secure, walk or drive every foot of affected fence line. Not just the spot where you can see the downed tree from the house. Straight-line winds do sneaky damage a quarter mile from the obvious mess.

Bring three things: your phone for photos, a notepad or notes app, and flagging tape or spray paint to mark trouble spots. You're building two lists at once. One for the repair work, one for the insurance claim.

Here's what to look for:

**Downed trees and limbs on wire.** The big obvious stuff. Note whether the wire underneath is snapped or just pinned. Wire that's pinned but intact often survives once the tree comes off. Wire that's stretched past its limit won't hold tension again.

**Leaning or snapped posts.** Push on every post near the damage. Saturated ground plus 60 mph wind rocks posts loose even where nothing fell on the fence. A post that wiggles now will lean worse in a month. Our guide on [fixing leaning fence posts](/blog/leaning-fence-posts/) covers which ones can be reset and which need replacing.

**Blown-out water gaps.** If you got the kind of rain that usually rides along with Central Texas storms, check every creek and draw crossing. Water gaps are built to give way in a flood, which means they're usually the first thing gone.

**Damaged braces and corners.** An H-brace that shifted even a couple inches will let the whole stretch go slack. Sight down your fence lines and look for wire that's lost its snap.

**Debris you can't see past.** Don't climb through or over tangled brush to inspect. If a tree is under tension on top of stretched wire, it can shift without warning. Mark it, photograph it from a safe distance, and move on.

One safety note that's not optional: if any fence wire is touching a downed power line, or even close to one, stay away from the entire fence. Wire carries current a long way down the line. Call your electric co-op first and let them kill the line before anyone touches that fence. The National Weather Service has a good rundown of post-storm electrical hazards at [weather.gov/safety/thunderstorm](https://www.weather.gov/safety/thunderstorm).

## Hours Four to Eight: Temporary Fixes That Actually Hold

You don't have to rebuild anything today. You just have to make the fence hold cattle until a proper repair happens. Here's what works.

**Cattle panels are your best friend.** A few 16-foot panels wired to solid posts on either side of a gap will hold most cattle for weeks. Keep four to six panels on hand year-round. At roughly $25 to $35 apiece they're the cheapest insurance on your place.

**Splice snapped wire with a figure-eight knot or crimp sleeves.** For a clean break in barbed wire, a proper splice restores most of the strength. Twist splices done in a hurry pull apart the first time a cow leans on the fence. If the wire is old and rusty enough that it snaps while you're splicing it, that stretch was on borrowed time anyway. Take the time to do the splice right and re-tension the whole stretch while you're at it.

**T-posts and a couple strands.** For longer gaps, drive temporary t-posts every 10 to 12 feet and run two strands of barbed or smooth wire. It's not pretty, but cattle respect it if it's tight.

**A strand of electric over the damage.** If you've got a solar charger, one hot wire on step-in posts across a damaged section changes cattle behavior fast. They'll quit testing the weak spot.

What not to do: don't try to chainsaw a large tree off tensioned wire by yourself. When the weight comes off, stretched wire can whip back hard enough to take out an eye, and the trunk can roll onto your legs. Trees under tension kill people every storm season. If the tree is bigger around than your thigh and it's laying on tight wire, that's a job for a crew with the right equipment.

## Before Dark: Document Everything for Insurance

Most farm and ranch policies cover fence damage from named perils like wind, hail, and falling trees, but the adjuster wasn't there when the oak came down. Your photos are the whole case.

Take wide shots that show the full damaged stretch, close-ups of broken posts and snapped wire, and photos of whatever caused the damage while it's still sitting on the fence. Get pictures before you do temporary repairs if you can do it safely, then more photos after. Note the date, time, and location of each damaged section.

Then call your agent and report the claim within a day or two. Ask specifically what your policy covers on fencing, because coverage varies a lot. Some policies pay replacement cost, some pay depreciated value, and some cap fence payouts at a number that won't touch a long rebuild. FEMA's guidance at [ready.gov/severe-weather](https://www.ready.gov/severe-weather) recommends documenting all storm damage before cleanup for exactly this reason.

A written repair estimate from a contractor gives the adjuster a real number to work with instead of a guess. ## When to Stop DIY and Call a Storm Fence Repair Pro

Plenty of storm damage is a Saturday morning fix for a handy rancher. Some of it isn't. Here's the honest line between the two.

**Handle it yourself if:** the damage is a few broken strands or a couple of leaning t-posts, no trees bigger than a fence post are involved, and nothing is near a road or a power line. Splice it, tighten it, move on.

**Call a professional if any of these are true:**

- A tree larger than your leg is resting on tensioned wire
- Corner posts or H-braces are broken or shifted, because everything downstream depends on them
- The damaged stretch borders a public road, where the liability math changes completely
- More than about 100 feet needs full rebuilding
- Any wire is anywhere near a downed power line

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does insurance cover storm damaged fence repair?

Usually yes, if you carry a farm and ranch policy that lists wind, hail, or falling objects as covered perils. Standard homeowners policies often exclude agricultural fencing or cap it at a low limit, so read your declarations page. Photograph everything before cleanup and get a written repair estimate to give the adjuster a real number.

### How fast can a storm damaged fence be repaired?

Temporary containment can happen the same day with panels or a couple strands of wire. Most repair jobs, like resetting posts and splicing wire, take one to two days once a crew is on site. Full rebuilds of a quarter mile or more typically run three days to a week depending on clearing and terrain.

### Can I just splice broken barbed wire back together?

For a clean break in decent wire, yes, a figure-eight splice or crimp sleeves will restore most of the original strength. If the wire snaps again while you're tensioning the splice, the whole stretch is too far gone and splicing is throwing good time after bad. Wire that's badly rusted or stretched past its limit needs replacing, not patching.

### Am I liable if my cattle get out through a storm damaged fence in Texas?

Not automatically, but you're on thin ice once you know the fence is down and do nothing. Most Central Texas counties operate under local stock laws that require you to keep cattle fenced, and knowing about an open gap and ignoring it looks like negligence to a jury. Contain your cattle first, patch the gap the same day, and document what you did and when.
