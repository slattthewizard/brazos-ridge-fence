# T-Post Sizes Explained: Weight, Length, Depth, Clips and Caps

A steel t-post is sold by two numbers: its weight in pounds per foot and its length in feet. For a cattle fence in Central Texas, the answer most of the time is a 1.33 lb per foot studded post with an anchor plate, 6.5 to 7 feet long, driven about 24 inches so the anchor plate sits fully below ground. Lighter 1.25 lb posts will do on interior fence with moderate pressure. The light 0.95 lb posts belong in a garden.

Length follows fence height. Take the height of your top wire, add 2 inches of post above it, add the depth you plan to drive, and buy the next size up. Below: that math for each fence type, how many clips a post needs, when tie wire beats a clip, and why horse owners cap every post.

## T-post weight: 1.25 vs 1.33 lb per foot

Farm stores mostly stock 1.25 and 1.33 pound per foot studded t-posts, plus a light garden post. The weight is for the steel section per foot of length, not counting the anchor plate. [Franklin Industries' t-post spec page](https://franklinindustriesco.com/t-post/) lists studded posts at 0.95, 1.10, 1.25, 1.33 and 1.50 lb per foot, with anchor plates standard on 1.10 lb and up and not available on the 0.95 lb post. The same page says its 1.33 lb post is also produced to ASTM A702, the steel fence post standard.

The October 2015 edition of the Texas NRCS [TX-382 fence specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf) calls for high carbon steel posts weighing at least 1.25 pounds per foot, exclusive of the anchor plate, and says 1.33 lb posts are preferred. It also requires an anchor plate and studs, embossing or punching for wire attachment.

What does the extra 0.08 pound per foot buy? On a 6.5 foot post, 1.33 x 6.5 is about 8.6 lb of steel and 1.25 x 6.5 is about 8.1 lb. Half a pound per post, about 6 percent more steel. On a quiet interior fence, that is close to nothing. It shows up in bending: a cow leaning through for grass, a bull rubbing, a limb dropping in an ice storm. The heavier section resists kinking a little longer, and once a t-post kinks it stays kinked.

Our rule of thumb:

- **1.33 lb:** perimeter fence, road frontage, bulls, traps and anything next to a pen where stock bunch up.
- **1.25 lb:** interior cross fence with moderate pressure, where saving a little per post adds up over a long run.
- **0.95 lb and other light posts:** garden fence, temporary lines, marking. Not a cattle fence.

### Painted or galvanized

Most farm store t-posts carry a baked enamel paint, usually green. Galvanized posts cost more and hold up better where they sit in water, and the Texas NRCS spec allows them in low-lying wet areas. On upland ground around Waco, painted is normal. In a creek bottom that floods every spring, galvanized is worth a look.

## T-post length: match the post to your fence height

The Texas NRCS spec gives the formula in plain terms: minimum post length allows for the setting depth, the fence height, and at least 2 inches of post above the top wire. It also sets a normal minimum of a 6 foot post set 24 inches deep, or a 5.5 foot post set 18 inches deep in rocky soil.

Say you are building a 5-strand barbed wire fence with the top wire at 52 inches:

- 52 in (top wire) + 2 in (above top wire) + 24 in (in the ground) = 78 in
- 78 in divided by 12 = 6.5 ft

A 6.5 foot post fits with nothing to spare. If the ground is uneven, or you expect to drive a little deeper in soft spots, go to 7 feet.

| Fence type | Typical top wire height | Post length (driven 24 in) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-strand interior barbed wire | 42 to 46 in | 6 ft | Leaves 48 in above ground |
| 5-strand perimeter barbed wire | 48 to 54 in | 6.5 to 7 ft | 7 ft gives room on rolling ground |
| 47 in net wire with a barbed strand on top | about 52 in | 6.5 to 7 ft | Net wire shows every dip, so plan extra length |
| Goat fence, 48 in net wire plus a top strand | about 53 in | 7 ft | Goats climb, so posts take more abuse |
| Horse fence | 54 to 60 in | 7.5 ft | T-posts are a poor choice for horses; see caps below |
| Rocky ground, low fence | up to 46 in | 5.5 ft driven 18 in | The NRCS allowance for rock |
| 8 ft high game fence | 96 in | longer than 10 ft, or pipe | A 10 ft post driven 24 in leaves exactly 8 ft, with no margin |

The high fence row trips people up. A 10 foot t-post sounds like plenty for an 8 foot fence until you do the subtraction. Franklin makes t-posts up to 14 feet for tall game and deer fence, and many high fence builds use pipe line posts instead. The [game fence installation](/game-fence-installation-waco/) page covers how those lines are built.

A t-post holds wire at height. It does not hold tension; [t-post vs wood post](/blog/t-post-vs-wood-post/) covers where steel belongs and where it never stands in for wood. ## How deep to drive a t-post

The short answer is 24 inches, and never less than the anchor plate. The University of Georgia's [Fences for Horses bulletin](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) says to drive studded t-posts until the anchor plate is beneath the surface, and to mark your driver so every post goes in to the same depth. A strip of bright tape on the driver sleeve does it.

The anchor plate is the steel fin near the point. Buried, it resists the post being pulled up and rocked. Half out of the ground, it does almost nothing.

Ground changes the job:

- **Blackland clay east of the Brazos.** Wet, posts drive easily. In August it sets up like brick and a manual driver bounces. Clay shrinks and swells with the seasons, which loosens shallow posts, so drive the full 24 inches even when dry ground makes it slow.
- **Limestone and caliche west of the river and into Bosque County.** You may hit rock at 10 or 12 inches. The 2015 NRCS allowance of a 5.5 foot post set 18 inches is for this ground. If you cannot get 18 inches, you need a drilled hole or a different post, not an anchor plate sitting on top of the rock.
- **Creek bottoms and sandy loam.** Posts drive fast but hold less. Go the full depth and use the 1.33 lb post.

### Stud direction and line

Drive every post with the studs facing the side the wire goes on. UGA's guidance is to attach wire to the side of the post nearest the livestock, except where appearance matters more. Then a cow pushing on the wire presses it against the post instead of popping the clips.

Run a string line between braces. A t-post that goes in crooked stays crooked, and pulling it straight loosens the soil around it.

## T-post spacing: the short version

Spacing gets its own full treatment in [fence post spacing](/blog/fence-post-spacing/). For reference, the Texas NRCS table caps line post spacing on a cattle barbed wire fence at 20 feet without stays and 30 feet with two or more stays, and at 15 feet without stays for goats. Those are maximums, not targets. Hills, draws and road frontage pull spacing tighter.

Spacing also sets your post and clip count. A quarter mile is 1,320 feet. At 12 foot centers, that is 110 line posts, less whatever the braces take up.

## T-post clips vs tie wire

### How clips work

A t-post clip is a short piece of preformed wire that hooks around the back of the post and wraps over the fence wire on the front. The studs on the post keep the wire from sliding down. Franklin notes five wire fasteners come with each painted t-post, which lines up with a 5-strand barbed wire fence. On net wire you will usually clip the mesh at three or four horizontal wires and use one more clip for the barbed strand above it.

The Texas NRCS spec asks that clips be galvanized and at least as strong as the fence wire they hold. Thin clips that bend open by hand will bend open under a cow.

Clips go on after the wire is stretched, not before. Set the wire on the stud at the right height, hook the clip behind the post, and close it with fencing pliers or a clip bender, a hand tool that wraps the clip in one turn. A few habits help:

- Close each clip snug without crushing the wire. Crushing damages the zinc coating and starts rust.
- Use the stud that gives the correct wire height, not the nearest one. Wire heights that wander post to post invite calves through.
- In a dip, where the wire wants to lift, seat the wire under a stud so it cannot slide up.

### When tie wire is better

Tie wire (a length of smooth galvanized wire wrapped around the post and fence wire) is the old way, and it still has a place: heavy pressure points, wire crossing a post at an angle on a curve, or a spot where clips keep pulling loose. Where wire ties off at a steel end post, the NRCS spec calls for it to be wired, since a clip is not meant to carry that load.

For electric fence, neither clips nor tie wire should touch a hot wire. UGA's bulletin is plain about it: electric wire goes on insulators, or it shorts.

## T-post caps and horse safety

A t-post is sheared to length at the mill, which leaves a sharp top edge. For cattle, that is rarely a problem. For horses, it can be a serious one.

Penn State Extension's [Fence Planning for Horses](https://extension.psu.edu/fence-planning-for-horses) says metal and fiberglass t-posts pose a serious risk of impalement and are not recommended for horses. The same page says that with a plastic safety cap on top, t-posts may be used cautiously in very large pastures where horse contact is rare. The Texas NRCS spec says something similar: for horses, consider avoiding barbed wire and steel t-posts when possible, especially where the confinement area is small.

That gives you a clear line:

- **Small pens, paddocks, turnouts and alleys for horses:** no t-posts. Build with wood, pipe or no-climb mesh on proper posts. The [horse fence](/horse-fence-waco/) page covers the options.
- **Large pastures where horses rarely touch the fence:** caps on every post, no exceptions.
- **Cattle fence that horses share:** cap the posts and consider an offset hot wire to keep horses off the line.

Caps come in plain vinyl and in styles that hold an electric wire or tape on top as an insulator. Walk the line after storms, because caps split in the sun and get knocked off, and a missing cap is only obvious if you look for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What size t-post should I use for a barbed wire fence?
For a 5-strand cattle fence with the top wire around 52 inches, use a 6.5 or 7 foot, 1.33 lb per foot studded t-post driven 24 inches. A 4-strand interior fence with a lower top wire can use 6 foot posts. The Texas NRCS spec sets 1.25 lb per foot as the minimum and prefers 1.33, so the heavier post is the safer choice on perimeter and road frontage.

### How deep should a t-post be in the ground?
About 24 inches in most soil, and always deep enough that the anchor plate is fully buried. The 2015 Texas NRCS fence spec allows a 5.5 foot post driven 18 inches in rocky ground. In blackland clay, get the full depth, because shrink-swell loosens shallow posts.

### How many clips do I need per t-post?
One per wire. A 5-strand barbed wire fence needs five clips per post, which matches the five fasteners Franklin packs with each painted post. Net wire with a barbed strand on top usually takes four or five. Buy extra, since clips get dropped in the grass and a few always break when you bend them.

### Do I need caps on t-posts?
For cattle, caps are optional. For horses, they are close to mandatory. Penn State Extension warns that t-posts carry a serious impalement risk for horses and should only be used, capped, in very large pastures where horses rarely touch the fence. In small pens and paddocks, skip t-posts entirely and build with wood or pipe.

### Can I attach electric wire directly to a t-post?
No. A hot wire touching a steel post shorts the fence to ground and drains the charger. Use insulators made for t-posts, either clip-on line insulators or cap insulators on top. The same applies to offset brackets that hold a hot wire out from an existing barbed wire or net wire fence.
