# Electric Fence for Cattle: How Many Strands, What Height, and How to Build It

A trained cow will respect one hot wire at about nose height, roughly 30 inches off the ground. That single strand is enough for a cross fence between two pastures of grown cattle. Add a second wire around 14 to 24 inches if you run calves, and go to four or five wires if the electric fence is doing a boundary job. The strand heights come from the Texas NRCS fence spec, and they are a sound starting point.

## How many strands and how high: the Texas NRCS numbers

The Natural Resources Conservation Service publishes a Texas fence standard (Code 382) for the fences it helps fund. It is the most useful single reference for wire heights in this state. Read the whole [NRCS Texas Fence Code 382 specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf) for yourself. For cattle and horses on smooth 12.5-gauge high-tensile electric wire, it sets these heights:

| Wires | Wire heights above ground | Where the spec allows it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | one wire at 24 to 40 in (also covers an offset on an existing fence) | cross fence in a grazing system |
| 2 | top 30 to 42 in, second 14 to 24 in | cross fence in a grazing system |
| 3 | top 34 to 48 in, middle 24 to 36 in, bottom 12 to 24 in | not limited to cross fence |
| 4 | top 42 to 60 in, then 34 to 48, 20 to 34, and 10 to 20 in | interior or boundary |
| 5 | top 50 to 60 in, then 40 to 50, 30 to 40, 20 to 30, and 10 to 20 in | boundary |

Two things in that spec are worth reading twice. The one-wire and two-wire designs are marked for cross fencing and "not recommended for exterior fences." And for the four- and five-wire designs, the spec says the spacing has to keep the animal from getting its head through without being shocked. That is the whole design rule for a multi-wire hot fence in one sentence.

### One strand for grown cows

A cow tests a fence with her nose first, so set the wire where her nose meets it, not her chest. For most commercial cows that is around 30 to 32 inches. Too high and she learns to duck under. Too low and she steps over it.

### Two strands when calves are on the ground

A calf's nose sits well under a 30-inch wire, and a calf that walks under it pulls its mama to the fence line bawling. The second strand at 14 to 24 inches fixes that. On pairs, set it toward the low end of that range.

### Bulls, weaned calves and anything under pressure

One or two hot wires make a cross fence for settled cattle. Between a bull and cycling heifers, or weaned calves and their mothers, you want a physical fence with a hot wire on it.

## Perimeter fence vs cross fence

A cross fence failure means cows in the wrong pasture. A perimeter failure means cows in the bar ditch or on the neighbor's oats. Electric only works while the charger is firing and the ground system is carrying current. A lightning hit on the energizer, a dead battery, a cedar limb after an ice storm, or bone-dry July clay can each drop a hot fence to nothing, and cattle figure that out fast.

That is why a common split around Waco is a boundary of five-strand [barbed wire](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/) or net wire with hot wire inside it. If you do want an all-electric perimeter, build it to the four- or five-wire heights in the table, on real corner braces, with a charger sized for the full length of energized wire. Road frontage raises the stakes: see [cattle on road liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/).

## Which wires are hot, and the dry-ground problem

The NRCS spec is plain about this. On any fence with two or more wires, the top wire is always hot. With two wires, the bottom can be hot or grounded. With three or more, the wires alternate hot and ground, with the top and bottom wires both hot.

Ground wires matter more here than in wetter country. The shock completes its circuit through the animal's feet, into moist soil and back to the ground rods at the charger. Blackland clay in August is cracked and dry, and thin soil over limestone west of the Brazos is no better. The spec notes that where the soil dries out enough to weaken the shock, you should run a combination of positive and negative wires. A cow touching a hot and a ground wire together gets the full shock even when her feet are on dry dirt.

On the charger end, the spec calls for at least three galvanized ground rods, half an inch in diameter and 6 feet long, set 10 feet apart, with no copper in the system. Mixing copper and galvanized steel corrodes the connection. A weak ground is the most common reason a fence reads fine at the box and does nothing at the far end. ## Posts and insulators

Electric fence for cattle carries very little physical load, so line posts can sit much farther apart than on barbed wire. The NRCS spec allows up to 150 feet with two or more stays and 100 feet without, and University of Tennessee Extension allows as much as 150 feet on very flat ground. On broken Central Texas ground, with draws, rock ledges and a cedar every few steps, we set them a good deal closer so the wires hold their height over the dips.

### Line posts

- Steel T-posts are fine, but only with UV-stabilized plastic insulators. The spec allows T-posts and other conductive posts only on that condition.
- Fiberglass rods (old sucker rod works) need to be at least 3/8 inch for one or two wires and at least 5/8 inch for more.
- Wood line posts with nail-on insulators are the stoutest option, and they belong at the high and low points where the wire pulls hardest up or down.

### Corners and ends

This is where cheap electric fences fail, because tensioned high-tensile wire pulls hard on its end posts. The spec allows a single wood post (6-inch top, 3 feet deep in concrete) or a single capped steel pipe for fences of two wires or less. Three or more wires, or a heavy gate, need a braced assembly with two posts in line. In rock that means drilling. In blackland it means depth, or the post heaves and leans.

### Insulators that survive a Texas summer

The NRCS spec calls for porcelain, ceramic, or high-quality UV-stabilized polyethylene, rated to at least 10,000 volts. The University of Kentucky's extension guide on [electric offsets](https://publications.mgcafe.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/ID269.pdf) adds a few rules we agree with:

- Start and end each run on an end-strain or bull nose insulator made for high-tensile wire.
- Never use wrap-around insulators. They fail early and leave you hunting for shorts.
- Make every connection with a clamp or crimp sleeve, never a wrapped twist, and use double-insulated cable (not house wire) for lead-outs and under gates, buried in conduit.

Walk the line after the first summer and replace any insulator that has gone chalky or cracked.

## Adding an offset hot wire to an old barbed wire fence

A hot wire on offset brackets keeps cattle from leaning, rubbing and reaching through an aging perimeter. Here is how to hang it:

1. **Height.** Mount offsets at nose height. The Kentucky guide puts that at around 30 inches for cattle. The NRCS spec says two-thirds the height of the animal, which comes out close to the same place.
2. **Spacing.** The NRCS spec puts offset brackets every 50 to 60 feet. University of Tennessee Extension adds that brackets hold better next to posts than hanging off sagging wire between posts.
3. **Bracket type.** Wire-mount offsets twist onto the existing strands and hold the hot wire about 10 inches off the fence. They flex with old wire, which cuts down on shorts. Use rigid wood-post offsets at the start and end of runs and on problem posts.
4. **Wire.** Run smooth 12.5-gauge high-tensile wire with Class 3 galvanizing on the offset. Do not hang a barbed strand on it.
5. **Keep it hot.** The Kentucky guide says livestock should never have access to an offset that is not electrified, because they rub on it until it fails.

An offset only works if the fence behind it is standing, so fix broken strands and walking corners first. ## Training cattle to a hot wire

Cattle that have never met an electric fence will walk right through one. Sale barn cattle need training before you trust a single strand with them.

The NRCS spec recommends a designated training area: a well-fenced pen with an electric wire run across or around it. It calls for at least 12 hours of exposure and says most animals are trained within 48 hours. University of Maine Extension's page on [training livestock to electric fences](https://extension.umaine.edu/livestock/pasture-course/lesson-3/training-livestock-to-electric-fences/) describes the same setup (a woven wire or board perimeter with the hot wire inside it) and adds three points worth keeping:

- Let the cattle find the wire on their own. Do not push them into it.
- Animals rarely challenge a fence a third time. One that keeps challenging it should be culled.
- Retrain every spring.

A set of [corrals or working pens](/corrals-working-pens-waco/) with a hot wire run inside the pipe makes a good training trap. Make the wire easy to see, too. Tennessee Extension's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) points out that animals learn fastest when they see the wire as they feel the shock, and suggests tying white cloth or bright plastic ribbon on hard-to-see wire. Polytape is easier to see than polywire or bare steel.

## Keeping it hot through a Central Texas year

The NRCS spec sets a minimum of 1,600 volts at the farthest point of a cattle fence, from a low-impedance charger that can hold at least 4,000 volts with the fence under its maximum expected load. Tennessee's guide gives lower floors (700 volts for short-haired cattle, around 2,000 for long-haired). Aim well above either, 3,000 volts or more at the far end, because voltage drops fast as weeds come up. Test at the far end, not at the charger.

Beyond dry soil, two things drain a cattle fence here:

- **Grass and weeds.** Johnsongrass and a wet spring can load a bottom wire in weeks. Mow or clip under the fence. On new lines, clear the cedar and mesquite before the wire goes up. We price [fence line clearing](/fence-line-clearing-waco/) at $1 to $3 per foot, and heavy cedar brake can run past that.
- **Storms.** Lightning finds energizers. Tennessee's guide recommends lightning arrestors and a ground system for the arrestor at least as good as the charger's.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many strands of electric fence do I need for cattle?
One hot wire at about 30 inches holds trained grown cows on a cross fence. Use two wires, with the second at 14 to 24 inches, when calves are on the ground. For a boundary fence, the Texas NRCS spec's boundary designs use four or five wires, with the top wire at 42 to 60 inches and the bottom wire 10 to 20 inches off the ground.

### How high should an electric fence be for cattle?
Put the main hot wire at nose height, which is about 30 to 32 inches for most cows. The NRCS Texas spec allows a single wire anywhere from 24 to 40 inches depending on the size of the cattle. Lower is better for calves and small breeds. Higher only makes sense on a multi-wire fence where lower wires cover the gap underneath.

### Will one strand of electric fence hold cattle?
It holds trained, settled cattle on an interior fence. It will not reliably hold untrained cattle, calves, bulls near cycling heifers, or any cattle once the fence goes dead. The NRCS spec marks one-wire and two-wire designs for cross fencing and does not recommend them for exterior fences, so keep a physical fence on your boundary.

### Can I use T-posts for an electric cattle fence?
Yes, with UV-stabilized plastic insulators rated for at least 10,000 volts. Steel T-posts conduct, so the insulator is the only thing keeping the wire from shorting to ground. Use a proper braced corner at the ends, not a T-post, because a tensioned high-tensile wire will pull a light end post over.

### How long does it take to train cattle to an electric fence?
Usually a day or two. The NRCS spec calls for at least 12 hours in a well-fenced training pen with a hot wire inside it and says most animals are trained within 48 hours. Let them find the wire on their own, and plan to refresh the training each spring, especially with new cattle bought from a sale barn.
