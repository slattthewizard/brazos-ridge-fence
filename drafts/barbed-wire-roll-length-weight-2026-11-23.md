# How Long and How Heavy Is a Roll of Barbed Wire?

A standard roll of barbed wire is 1,320 feet long, which is a quarter mile or 80 rods. In the common 12.5 gauge, a roll weighs about 80 pounds. Some Class 3 four-point rolls run heavier, and one manufacturer lists its roll at just over 89 pounds. Light high-tensile wire weighs roughly half as much for the same length.

That's the short answer. The rest of this guide covers why the numbers vary, how many rolls a mile of fence takes, how to get an 80-pound spool down a fence line without getting cut, and what to do with the old wire you pull out. ## What is in a standard roll

Barbed wire is sold on a spool, and the common length is 1,320 feet. The Bekaert page for its [12.5 gauge, 4-point, 5-inch spacing Class 3 barbed wire](https://fencing.bekaert.com/en/fence-products/12-5-ga-4-point-5-spacing-standard-barbed-wire-c3-dot) lists the roll at 1,320 feet and the item weight at 89.276 pounds. A British Columbia Ministry of Agriculture fact sheet, [Commonly Used Wire for Agricultural Fences](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/agriculture-and-seafood/farm-management/structures-and-mechanization/300-series/307100-1_commonly_used_wire_for_agric_fences.pdf), says barbed wire is sold in rolls of 1,320 feet weighing approximately 80 pounds.

Those two numbers don't match, and neither one is wrong. Weight depends on gauge, barb style, barb spacing and how much zinc is on the wire. Use 80 pounds as the working figure and expect a 12.5 gauge roll to land somewhere in the 80s. If you're loading a trailer or deciding whether one person can lift it, check the tag or the spec sheet for the exact wire you're buying.

Why 1,320 feet? It's a quarter of a mile (5,280 divided by 4), and it's 80 rods at 16.5 feet each. Old fence math was done in rods, and the roll length stuck.

### Weight by wire type

| Wire | Roll length | Approximate roll weight |
|---|---|---|
| 12.5 gauge, standard barbed | 1,320 ft | about 80 lb (BC fact sheet) |
| 12.5 gauge, 4-point, Class 3 (one manufacturer's spec) | 1,320 ft | about 89 lb |
| 15.5 gauge high-tensile barbed | 1,320 ft | roughly half of the 12.5 roll |

The "roughly half" line comes from the same BC fact sheet, which says two-strand 15.5 gauge high-tensile barbed wire has the same breaking strength as common barbed wire at about half the weight. Our post on [barbed wire gauge](/blog/barbed-wire-gauge/) goes through why a thinner wire can break at a similar load and what it demands from your corners.

Roll weights are approximate. A figure from a shipping scale may include the spool and wrap, and a catalog figure may be rounded. Use them to plan a truck load, not to settle an argument.

## Class 1 vs Class 3 on the roll tag

Weight isn't the only thing printed on the tag. Look for the galvanizing class. The University of Georgia circular [Fences for the Farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/) notes that a light gauge Class 3 barbed wire is commonly stocked alongside a heavier gauge Class 1 barbed wire. That means you can't judge a roll by how heavy it feels. A heavy roll with a thin zinc coat can rust out before a lighter roll with a good coat.

Central Texas is hard on wire. Heat, humidity along the Brazos bottoms and the occasional ice storm all work on it. We cover the coating difference in our post on [Class 1 vs Class 3 galvanized wire](/blog/class-3-galvanized-wire/). For a fence you want to leave alone for a couple of decades, read the coating class first and the weight second.

## How many rolls does a fence take?

A roll covers 1,320 feet of one strand. A fence has several strands. So the math is:

**Rolls = feet of fence x number of strands / 1,320**

For a mile it works out like this:

- One mile is 5,280 feet, which is 4 rolls per strand (5,280 / 1,320 = 4).
- A 4-strand fence takes 16 rolls a mile.
- A 5-strand fence takes 20 rolls a mile.
- At about 80 pounds a roll, 20 rolls is about 1,600 pounds of wire for a mile of 5-strand fence.

A quarter mile of 5-strand fence is exactly 5 rolls on paper. Buy at least one extra. You use wire on every corner wrap, every brace, every gate and every splice, and the leftover roll becomes your repair stock.

### What a half mile weighs on the truck

Say you're hauling wire for a half mile of 5-strand fence on 20 acres outside Lorena. Half a mile is 2,640 feet. Times 5 strands is 13,200 feet of wire. Divided by 1,320, that's 10 rolls. At about 80 pounds each, you're hauling roughly 800 pounds of wire, plus posts and staples. That's no problem for a flatbed or a stock trailer, but it's worth knowing before you load a small utility trailer. Strap the rolls down so they can't shift or roll.

## Moving a roll without getting cut

An 80-pound spool with sharp barbs is an awkward load. It isn't too heavy for one person. It just has no good handle, and the barbs find your forearms.

- Wear heavy leather gloves, long sleeves and eye protection every time you touch it.
- Lift with two people when you can. If you're alone, stand the roll on its edge and roll it along the ground, or use a hand truck, instead of a bear hug.
- Keep the retaining ties on until the roll is on the unroller. The wire in the spool is springy, and a cut tie can let it flare out.
- When you do cut a tie, keep the spool held down or already mounted, and stand to the side, not over it.
- Don't stack rolls on loose ground where they can tip.

## How to unroll barbed wire

Dragging a roll across a pasture and letting it spin loose is how wire ends up in knots. A kink in barbed wire is a weak spot that can break under tension later, and a tangle of loose wire is how people and livestock get cut.

### Use a spinning jenny or a pipe through the roll

The standard tool is a wire unroller, often called a spinning jenny. It holds the roll on a spindle so it pays out as you walk or drive away. Some carry by hand, and some mount on a three-point hitch or a receiver. No unroller on hand? Slide a length of pipe through the center of the spool and support both ends, on a truck bed, a tractor bucket or a trailer, so the roll spins freely. Our [fence building tools](/blog/fence-building-tools/) post covers the unroller options.

### Pay it out along the line

1. Set the roll on the unroller at one end of the run, near a braced corner or end post.
2. Cut the ties only once the roll is on the spindle.
3. Tie the free end off to the first brace before you start moving. Don't just wrap it loosely.
4. Walk or drive down the line slowly and let the wire come off the spool in a straight line.
5. Keep tension light. You want the wire to lay straight, not bounce or loop.
6. At the far end, stop and cut with the wire held so it can't whip back. Don't tension it yet.

Barbed wire is two strands twisted together. The BC fact sheet says the two strands need to be prestretched so they work as one wire: tension up to about 600 pounds, then relax to about 250 pounds before stapling. Our guide on [how to build a barbed wire fence](/blog/how-to-build-a-barbed-wire-fence/) has the full build order, and our post on [how to splice barbed wire](/blog/how-to-splice-barbed-wire/) covers joining the end of one roll to the start of the next.

### Where roll length shows up on the job

The 1,320-foot figure matters for more than math. A quarter mile is also a sensible length for one pull on straight, flat ground, so a roll and a stretch often line up. When the run is longer, or the ground rolls or the line turns, you add a brace and start a new pull. Every splice you leave in the middle of a pull is a spot to check later.

## What to do with old barbed wire

Pulling an old fence leaves a lot of steel. A mile of 5-strand fence is 20 rolls' worth of wire, and even rusty, it adds up.

- Pull the wire off the posts as you go and roll it up rather than leaving it in the grass. Rusted wire snaps, and loose strands in tall grass catch tires, hooves and mower decks.
- Roll it tight around a pipe or onto an empty spool and tie it off in several places.
- Call a local scrap yard before you haul it in. Some take steel wire, some want it baled, and rules change. Ask what they'll accept.
- Don't bury it on the place. It's a hazard for the next owner and the next set of tires.
- Pull the staples before you save any posts.

## Buying wire: a short checklist

- Gauge and type: 12.5 low-carbon or 15.5 high-tensile. Don't mix them in one strand.
- Galvanizing class: Class 3 for a long life. Class 1 is fine for a short-term line.
- Roll length: 1,320 feet is standard, but read the label before you do your math.
- Barb points and spacing: 2-point or 4-point, and the distance between barbs.
- Weight on the tag: compare it to what you expect for the gauge.
- One extra roll beyond what the math says.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a roll of barbed wire weigh?

A standard 1,320-foot roll of 12.5 gauge barbed wire weighs about 80 pounds. Some Class 3 four-point rolls run heavier, and one manufacturer lists a roll at about 89 pounds. High-tensile 15.5 gauge wire weighs about half as much for the same length. The tag or spec sheet on the roll you're buying has the exact weight.

### How many feet are in a roll of barbed wire?

The standard roll is 1,320 feet, which is a quarter mile or 80 rods. That's one strand's worth. A mile of one strand takes 4 rolls, and a mile of 5-strand fence takes 20. Read the label before you do your math, in case the roll you're buying is a different length.

### How many rolls of barbed wire do I need per mile?

Four rolls per strand. A 4-strand fence is 16 rolls a mile, and a 5-strand fence is 20. Add at least one extra roll for wraps at braces, gates and splices. For a smaller tract, multiply feet by strands and divide by 1,320. Our post on [how much fence per acre](/blog/how-much-fence-per-acre/) shows the math by acreage.

### What's the easiest way to unroll barbed wire?

Put the roll on a spinning jenny, or run a pipe through the center and support both ends, then walk or drive down the line so the wire pays out straight. Tie the free end to a brace first, cut the ties only after the roll is mounted, and wear heavy gloves and eye protection.

### Can one person carry a roll of barbed wire?

An 80-pound roll is within what many people can lift, but it's awkward and the barbs catch skin and clothing. Use two people if you can, wear gloves and long sleeves, and roll the spool on its edge instead of hugging it. Keep the ties on until the roll is on the unroller.
