# Fencing Across an Easement in Texas: Pipelines, Power Lines and Road Right of Way

In most cases you can build a fence across an easement in Texas. You still own the ground. What the easement holder owns is a right to use a strip of it for a stated purpose, and your fence is fine as long as it does not get in the way of that purpose or break a term written into the easement. The recorded easement document decides the details, so read it before the first post goes in.

On a pipeline that usually means a gate for the company, no deep holes over the line, and a locate before any auger turns. Under a power line it means keeping equipment clear of the wire. Along a road it means knowing where the right of way stops.

## What an easement is, and what it is not

An easement lets someone else use a defined part of your land for a defined purpose. Your land is the servient estate. The holder might be a pipeline company, an electric co-op, a water supply corporation, the county, or a neighbor with a road easement to his back 40.

The holder uses the strip for its stated purpose. You keep using the land but cannot interfere with the holder's use. That second rule is the one a fence runs into.

Most easements you will fence around are written and recorded in the county deed records. Some are not written at all (implied or prescriptive easements), and those are harder to pin down. A pipeline marker or a power pole usually has a recorded instrument behind it. Pull a copy from the county clerk in McLennan, Bosque or Falls County, or ask the title company that handled your purchase.

## Read the recorded easement before you fence

A lot of trouble comes from fencing on what a pipeline rep said at the gate twenty years ago. The written document is what counts. Look for these things:

- **Width and location.** Is it a fixed strip (say 30 feet, 15 on each side of the pipe centerline) or a "blanket" easement that floats across the whole tract? A defined width tells you exactly where your crossing is.
- **Access language.** Does it grant "free and uninterrupted" use, or just a right of ingress and egress? This matters for gates.
- **Gate and fence terms.** Many pipeline easements say who builds the gates, who keeps the keys, whether gates stay locked, and whether the company has to brace the fence before it cuts it.
- **Surface use reserved to you.** Some easements spell out that the landowner may graze, cross, build roads or fence over the strip. Others restrict structures on the right of way.
- **Restoration and damages.** Does the holder have to repair fences, restore the surface and pay for damage when it digs up the line or works on its poles?

AgriLife's [Texas Pipeline Easement Negotiation Checklist (EAG-009)](https://agecoext.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2016/08/Texas-Pipeline-Easement-Negotiation-Checklist.pdf) is written for landowners signing a new easement, but it works as a reading guide for an old one too. It urges landowners to require the company to fence the easement area to stated specs: fence type, the number and type of H-braces, and wire strength. If your easement already has that language, you have something to hold the company to.

## Gates across an easement

A 2021 post on AgriLife's Texas Agriculture Law blog, [Can a landowner place a gate across a "free and uninterrupted" easement?](https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/2021/05/10/questions-from-tiffanys-desk-can-a-landowner-place-a-gate-across-a-free-and-uninterrupted-easement/), walks through the Texas cases. Where an easement grants "free and uninterrupted" use, courts have held that the landowner may not put up gates or fences that hinder the holder's passage. Where that phrase is missing, courts have looked at the parties' intent and how the easement was used, and the results have gone both ways. Nobody can give you a blanket yes or no.

From the fence side, a few practical rules:

- **Build the gate wide enough for the holder's equipment.** A pipeline crew with a trackhoe on a trailer or a utility bucket truck may need more than a 12-foot gate; ask the holder what they run. A 16-foot opening, or a pair of gates hung on one set of posts, is common where trucks cross.
- **Settle locks in writing.** If you lock the gate, the holder needs a way through. Many places use a daisy chain of padlocks, so the landowner and each company can open it with their own key. Put the arrangement in a letter or email so there is a record.
- **Brace it like a corner.** A gate post in a fence line takes the full pull of the wire on one side. Set it in an H-brace, not as a lone post, or it will lean within a season. Our [H-brace basics](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) cover the assembly.
- **Do not block a neighbor's road easement.** A locked gate your neighbor cannot open on his own road easement is the fastest way into a lawsuit. Give him a key or a combination and keep a written note of it.

## Crossing a pipeline with fence posts

This is where the real danger is: posts, and maybe an auger, within a few feet of a pressurized line.

### Know how deep the line might be

AgriLife's checklist notes that easements usually call for 36 inches of cover over a pipeline, and warns that erosion will make a line buried at 36 inches shallower over time. On Central Texas ground, terracing, old stock tanks, dozer work and creek washouts all change the cover.

Now compare that with fence post depth. The NRCS Texas fence specification ([Code 382](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf)) calls for T-posts set 24 inches deep, brace posts buried at least 3 feet unless driven, and driven 2 3/8-inch pipe brace posts set 5 feet deep. A driven pipe post on a brace, sitting on top of a pipeline with 36 inches of cover or less, can hit the pipe. So can a hydraulic auger on a skid steer.

### Call 811, even if you think you are exempt

Texas's damage-prevention law is [Chapter 251 of the Utilities Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/UT/htm/UT.251.htm). It defines "excavate" as using powered equipment of any kind, augering included, to disturb soil to a depth of 16 inches or more. That reaches a tractor or skid steer auger and very likely a hydraulic post driver. An excavator has to notify the one-call center (Texas811, reached by dialing 811) no more than 14 days and no less than 48 hours before digging, not counting weekends and legal holidays. Operators of the main class of lines (gas, petroleum, electric, telecom) generally have 48 hours, again not counting weekends and holidays, to mark them.

Section 251.156 of the same chapter exempts "activities performed on private property in connection with agricultural operations" from that notice duty, with agricultural operations defined by reference to the Tax Code's agricultural use. So a rancher building his own cross fence may not be required to call. Do it anyway. The statute says that if a person covered by that exemption chooses to comply and the line operator does not, that person is not liable to the facility owner for damage to the line. A free call buys that protection.

A few rules for any pipeline crossing:

- Locate first, then call the pipeline company's number on the marker post and ask whether they want a representative on site.
- Line markers tell you a pipeline is nearby. They do not tell you exactly where or how deep it is.
- Leave a gap in the post spacing directly over the line. Ask the operator how close to the pipe they will allow a post, and whether they want any hole near it dug by hand.
- Set the H-braces and gate posts well off the marked line, never on top of it, even if that means an odd post spacing through the crossing.

If the easement has language on structures inside the strip, it controls.

## Power line easements

Texas [Health and Safety Code Chapter 752](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.752.htm) covers high voltage overhead lines (more than 600 volts). It bars anyone from doing work where a person, tool, machine or material could come within six feet of the line, unless the work is made safe through an arrangement with the line owner, and it requires 48 hours' notice to the operator before work closer than that. On a fence job the risks are a post driver mast, a skid steer boom, or a 21-foot joint of pipe swung into place for a pipe fence.

Other points to check with the co-op or utility before you build:

- **Guy wires and poles.** Leave room to reach the pole and anchors, and ask before tying any fence to a pole or a guy wire.
- **Access for bucket trucks.** Same gate logic as a pipeline. If the right of way runs down your fence line, the utility will need to get along it.
- **Long fences under transmission lines.** A long wire fence running parallel beneath a big transmission line can pick up induced voltage. If your fence will run for a long stretch directly under a transmission line, ask the line owner whether they want it grounded.
- **Brush clearing.** Utilities often clear their own right of way. Coordinate so you are not paying to clear a strip they are about to mow. Our [fence line clearing guide](/blog/clearing-fence-line-brush/) covers the clearing side.

## Road right of way and county roads

Along a county road or a state highway, the question is where the right of way ends. A fence built inside a public road right of way sits on ground whose use the county or TxDOT controls, and it may end up having to come out.

Before building a road frontage fence, find the right of way line on your survey or plat, or ask the county road department. Old fences along county roads are often not on the true line. If you plan a cattle guard where your drive meets a county road, or anything else inside that strip, the commissioners court has a say; our [cattle guard guide](/blog/cattle-guard-cost-installation/) covers the county process.

## When the easement holder damages your fence

Pipeline and utility crews cut fence during construction, line repairs and right-of-way work. What they owe you depends on the easement's restoration language. When it happens:

1. **Photograph it the day you find it.** Date, location, how many feet, which braces are down, and any livestock that got out.
2. **Call the number on the marker or the utility's right of way office.** Ask for the easement or project number and a named contact.
3. **Point to the easement terms.** If it says the company restores fences, ask for the repair spec in writing: brace type, horizontal length, wire gauge and strand count. A short, light brace will sag within a year.
4. **Get a repair estimate.** Even if the company does the work, it gives you a number to hold them to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I put a fence across a pipeline easement in Texas?
Usually yes. You still own the land, and a fence that does not interfere with the pipeline company's use is normally allowed. The recorded easement controls, so read it for gate, fence and surface-use terms. Expect to give the company a gate, keep braces off the line, and have the line located through 811 before any digging or driving.

### Do I have to put a gate where my fence crosses an easement?
If the holder needs to travel the easement, a fence with no gate interferes with their use, so in practice yes. Whether you can lock it depends on the easement wording. Texas courts have barred gates that hinder passage where the easement grants "free and uninterrupted" use. Where that language is missing, results vary. Give the holder a key or a lock in the chain and keep a written record.

### Do I need to call 811 to set fence posts in Texas?
Chapter 251 of the Utilities Code applies to powered digging 16 inches deep or more, which covers an auger and likely a hydraulic driver. It exempts agricultural operations on private property, but calling still protects you: if an exempt landowner complies and the operator does not, the landowner is not liable to the facility owner for damage to the line. Call at least 48 hours ahead, not counting weekends and holidays.

### How deep are pipelines buried in Texas?
AgriLife's pipeline easement checklist says easements usually call for 36 inches of cover, and warns that erosion can make a line shallower over time. Never assume a depth. Get the line located and ask the operator before any post goes in near it.

### Who fixes my fence if the pipeline company cuts it?
That depends on the easement. Many require the holder to restore fences and pay surface damages, and some spell out the brace and wire spec. Photograph the damage, call the number on the line marker, and cite the easement terms. A written repair estimate helps if you and the company disagree on the cost.
