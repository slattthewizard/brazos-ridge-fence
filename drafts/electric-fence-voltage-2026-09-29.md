# Electric Fence Voltage: What Your Fence Should Read and How to Test It

For cattle and horses, most Central Texas electric fences should read at least 3,000 volts at the far end of the line. Goats, sheep, hogs and coyotes need more, and 4,000 volts or better is a sensible floor for them. Those numbers sit well above the published minimums, and there is a good reason for the gap, which we will get to.

The number only means something if you measure it in the right place. A reading at the charger tells you the charger works. A reading at the far end, on each hot wire, tells you what an animal will actually feel. Most people test the wrong end and trust a fence that has already gone soft.

## How many volts should an electric fence have?

There are two kinds of numbers floating around. One is the minimum that will still turn an animal. The other is what you should keep on the wire so you have room for grass, dry ground and a hungry animal. The table shows both.

| Animal | NRCS Texas minimum at the farthest point | UT Extension minimum shock | What we want to see on a working fence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cattle (short hair) | 1,600 volts | about 700 volts | 3,000 volts or more |
| Horses | 1,200 volts | about 700 volts | 3,000 volts or more |
| Sheep and hair goats | 2,000 volts | about 2,000 volts | 4,000 volts or more |
| Meat goats | 1,200 volts | about 2,000 volts | 4,000 volts or more |
| Hogs | 1,200 volts | about 700 volts | 4,000 volts or more |
| Coyotes and dogs | not listed | not listed | 4,000 to 5,000 volts |

The first column comes from the [NRCS Texas Fence (Code 382) specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf), which asks for a charger with enough joules to hold those minimums at the farthest point of a permanent electric fence. The same spec wants the energizer able to put out at least 4,000 volts with the fence under its maximum expected load, and it recommends a digital readout voltmeter go with every energizer.

The second column is from University of Tennessee Extension's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf). It puts the minimum shock at roughly 700 volts for short-haired cattle, pigs and horses, and around 2,000 volts for long-haired cattle, sheep and goats.

For coyotes, Ohio State's fact sheet on [preventing and controlling coyote problems](https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/W-4) says a fence needs a minimum of 3,000 volts to get through a coyote's long hair and hide, and that 4,000 to 5,000 volts may be better depending on fence length.

The right-hand column is our working target, not a published standard. It is simply the published minimums plus room for the things that eat voltage on a Central Texas place.

## Why the minimums and the working number are different

A published minimum is the point where the fence stops working. Here is what eats the gap on a Central Texas place.

**Hair and wool insulate.** A goat in winter coat or a sheep carrying fleece has a layer of dry fiber between the wire and its skin. That is why both sources set the bar higher for them.

**Footing matters.** The shock only lands when current runs through the animal and back to the charger through the soil. A cow standing on bone-dry blackland clay in August, or on a thin skin of soil over limestone west of the Brazos, makes a poor connection with the ground. Your tester may still read 3,500 volts while the animal feels much less.

**Motivation beats a weak shock.** A cow with good grass on her side will respect a modest fence. A feral hog that smells a corn feeder, a coyote after kid goats, or a bull with a heifer across the wire will push through a shock that would stop them on an ordinary day.

**Grass grows back.** Every stem touching a hot wire bleeds off a little voltage. A fence that reads 5,000 volts in March can read half that by June once johnsongrass and ragweed lean into the bottom wire. Starting high gives you a cushion before the next mowing.

One more point. Voltage is the pressure on the wire; joules are the energy behind it. A weak charger can show a high number on a clean fence and collapse once weeds load it. The UT publication says as much about high-impedance chargers: the first weed or blade of grass on the wire drains the power. Watch what the reading does in June.

## Where to test: charger, near end, far end, every wire

Testing in order tells you where a problem lives.

1. **At the charger, disconnected.** Unhook the fence lead and the ground lead, turn the charger on, and test across its two terminals. Premier1's [digital voltmeter instructions](https://www.premier1supplies.com/p/digital-voltmeter) say the tester should read 6,000 volts or more this way. That figure is for their tester and the energizers they sell; your charger's manual gives its own open-circuit rating. If it does not, the charger or its battery is your problem, not the fence.
2. **At the charger, connected.** Hook everything back up and test the fence terminal to the ground terminal. The drop between step 1 and step 2 is the load your fence is putting on the charger.
3. **At the first corner or gate.** Test on the far side of any gate, jumper or underground lead-out. A big drop right past a gate points at the jumper, not the whole fence.
4. **At the far end.** This is the number that counts, and the one to compare against the table above.
5. **On every hot wire.** Each strand can have its own problem. A top wire can read 4,500 volts while the bottom wire, lying in the grass, reads 900.

Write every number down with the date. If the far-end reading falls by a third over a month, go look before the cows find out.

## How to take a reading you can trust

A tester measures the difference between the wire and its own ground probe. If the probe is in bad dirt, the number is wrong.

- **Push the ground probe all the way in**, into soil with some moisture, and a good step away from the charger's ground rods. In a dry July, find a low spot, the shady side of a post, or pour a little water on the spot first.
- **On a hot and ground wire fence, clip across the two wires** instead of using the probe. That reads what an animal touching both wires would get, and it sidesteps the dry-soil problem entirely.
- **Check the tester's own battery.** Premier1 notes that the tester's internal battery affects the accuracy of a digital reading, and that a dim display or readings that drop with each pulse at the same spot are signs to change it.
- **Test at the same time of day.** A fence wet with morning dew in tall grass reads lower than the same fence at 3 in the afternoon. Pick one time and stick to it so your log compares like with like.

Never test a fence by grabbing it, touching it with a blade of grass, or letting a dog or a kid find out. You learn nothing useful about the number, and a shock off a big ranch charger is no joke, least of all for a child.

## Digital vs neon fence testers

### Neon (light) testers

A neon tester has a row of small lamps, each marked with a voltage step on the case. Touch the probe to the wire and the lights that come on give you a range. They are cheap, simple and tough enough to live in a truck door pocket. The downsides: they give you a band, not a number, and the lamps can be hard to see in full Texas sun at noon. For a horse owner checking one paddock, a neon tester is often all you need.

### Digital voltmeters

A digital fence voltmeter shows the pulse as a number, usually in kilovolts. A reading of 2.7 kV means 2,700 volts. The exact number is what makes a logbook useful, because you can see a slow slide from 5.2 to 4.6 to 3.9 that a neon tester would blur. This is the tool NRCS Texas recommends pairing with every energizer, and it is the one we would buy for a cattle or goat operation running more than a single pasture.

### Fault finders

A fault finder reads voltage and also the current flowing down the wire, and points toward where it is leaking. It is a troubleshooting tool more than a testing tool. On one paddock, a digital voltmeter and a walk will do.

Do not use a household multimeter on fence wire. It is built for low voltage and a fence pulse can damage it.

## What the reading means on a Central Texas place

**In summer.** Dry blackland clay cracks and pulls away from ground rods, and caliche and thin rocky soil conduct poorly. If your reading holds but animals are walking through, the shock is not reaching them through dry footing. The NRCS Texas spec covers this directly: where the soil dries to the point that it will not give enough shock, it calls for a mix of positive and negative wires instead of an all-hot fence. That is the hot and ground wire setup, and on thin rocky soil in Bosque County it is often the fix.

**For goats and sheep.** Plan on the high end and keep it there. Goats test fences daily, and once one learns a wire is soft the whole herd follows. If you are building for small stock, our [goat and sheep fence](/goat-sheep-fence-waco/) page covers the wire options that hold them.

**For horses.** Horses respect a lower number than goats do, but they panic harder when caught. Our [electric horse fence guide](/blog/electric-fence-for-horses/) covers the wire, tape and charger choices that keep a hot fence safe for them.

**For feral hogs.** Texas A&M's [feral hog preventative options](https://feralhogs.tamu.edu/preventative-options/) page recommends at least two strands, placed to hit juvenile and adult pigs on the nose at about 8 and 18 inches, with all vegetation cleared so the fence does not ground out. It also warns that hogs can become desensitized when food is scarce. That is why we like a high number on any hot wire meant for hogs: a hungry hog that gets a weak pop learns it can take it. Our post on [feral hog fence damage](/blog/feral-hog-fence-damage/) covers the physical side of stopping them.

**When the far end is soft but the charger end is strong.** That is usually vegetation or a leak partway down the line, and knocking the line back often gains more than a bigger charger would. If brush has taken over, see our [fence line clearing](/fence-line-clearing-waco/) service.

## When the number will not come up

If you have tested in order, cleared the line and checked the ground rods and the far end still reads low, the fence itself may be the problem: cracked insulators, rusted splices, a failed buried lead-out, or a layout that has outgrown its charger. Sometimes a hot wire is the wrong tool for that pasture and a permanent fence is the better spend.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many volts should an electric fence have for cattle?

NRCS Texas sets 1,600 volts as the minimum at the farthest point for cattle, and University of Tennessee Extension puts the minimum shock for short-haired cattle around 700 volts. Those are failure points, not goals. Keep 3,000 volts or more at the far end so you have a cushion for grass, dry ground and bulls that like to test a fence.

### What voltage should an electric fence read for goats and sheep?

Aim for 4,000 volts or better at the far end. NRCS Texas lists 2,000 volts as the minimum for sheep and hair goats, and wool and heavy coats blunt the shock. Goats also test fences constantly, so a fence that sits near the minimum tends to fail the day one of them finds the soft spot.

### Why does my fence read high at the charger but low at the far end?

The charger is fine and something along the fence is draining it. The usual causes are grass and weeds on the wire, a cracked insulator touching a steel post, a bad splice, or a failed jumper or lead-out at a gate. Test past each gate and on each wire to narrow it down, then see our guide to an [electric fence that is not working](/blog/electric-fence-not-working/).

### Is a neon fence tester good enough?

For one horse paddock or a small goat pen, yes. A neon tester shows a voltage range with a row of lights, which is enough to tell a hot fence from a soft one. For a larger operation, a digital voltmeter is worth it because it gives an exact number you can log, so you can see a slow drop over weeks before stock start pushing on the wire.

### How often should I test electric fence voltage?

Test the far end at least weekly in the growing season, and any time you move stock, after a storm, or when animals start leaning on the fence. In winter, when the grass is dormant, every couple of weeks is usually enough. Write each reading down with the date so a gradual decline is easy to spot.
