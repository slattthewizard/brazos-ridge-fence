# Electric Fence for Goats: Strands, Heights, Netting and Charger Strength

An electric fence for goats works when it is built tighter and hotter than a cattle fence. Plan on four to six strands, the bottom wire no higher than about 6 inches off the ground, narrow gaps down low, and a top wire at 36 inches or more. The charger has to hold real voltage at the far end of the line, and long-haired goats need more of it than cattle do.

For a permanent perimeter, most goat places around Waco do better with woven wire plus a hot wire or two on offset brackets. All-electric works well for cross fencing, rotational paddocks and brush lots, as long as someone keeps it hot every day. ## Why goats test electric fence differently than cattle

A cow bumps a hot wire, feels it, and backs off. A goat investigates. She pushes her head between strands for the weeds beyond, stands up on the fence, and tries the low gaps. Kids go under anything they fit under.

Three things make goats harder to shock.

**Hair coat.** Hair insulates. The University of Tennessee bulletin [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) puts the minimum at about 700 volts for short-haired cattle, pigs and horses, and around 2,000 volts for long-haired cattle, sheep and goats. A slick Boer in August and an Angora in full fleece are not the same load for the wire.

**Heads and horns.** If the strands are far enough apart for a goat to get her head through before her nose touches a hot wire, she will learn to do exactly that. A horned goat has a harder time pulling it back out. The fix is spacing: narrow gaps low, where goats work the fence, and a hot wire where the nose arrives first.

**Dry ground.** The shock runs through the animal and back through the soil to the charger's ground rods. A goat standing on cracked blackland in late summer, or on thin soil over caliche west of the Brazos, is a poor path back. The tester at the charger can read fine while the goat feels little.

## How many strands for goats, and how high

The published specs agree: more wires than cattle need, tight low, wider up top.

| Setup | Wires | Heights above ground | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Texas NRCS 4-wire smooth | 4 | 36 in top (hot), 24 in, 14 in, bottom hot no higher than 6 in | Cross fence only, trained goats |
| Texas NRCS 5-wire smooth | 5 | 36 in top (hot), 26 in, 18 in, 12 in, bottom hot no higher than 6 in | Interior or boundary, heavier pressure |
| High tensile 6-wire (FAMU) | 6 | At least 48 in tall, 6 to 8 in apart near the ground, 8 to 12 in apart up top | Permanent all-electric fence |
| Electric netting | Full mesh | 35 to 42 in, by product | Rotational grazing, brush paddocks |
| Offset on woven wire | 1 or 2 | Inside at 18 to 24 in, plus 6 to 8 in if they go under; outside low wire at least 8 in off the ground | Climbers, leaners, dogs, coyotes |

The first two rows come from the Texas NRCS [Fence (Code 382) specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf), which allows the lower wires to be hot or to alternate hot and ground. The spec marks the four-wire layout for cross fencing only and does not recommend it for exterior fences; the five-wire layout carries no such limit. The six-wire layout and the netting heights come from Florida A&M's extension bulletin [Establishing the Meat Goat Facility](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/Meat-Goats.pdf), which calls for six wires of 12.5-gauge high tensile, a mix of charged and grounded wires, and a fence at least 48 inches tall.

Some working notes:

- **The bottom wire does most of the work.** Every layout above keeps it at 6 inches or less. On uneven ground, set line posts at the high and low points so the wire follows the dips.
- **36 inches is a floor.** It holds a lot of trained goats. Spanish-type goats, a buck that smells does across the fence, or a herd that has learned to jump will push you toward the taller six-wire layout.
- **Hot and ground wires matter here.** When a goat touches a hot wire and a ground wire together, the shock no longer depends on the soil under her feet. The NRCS spec says that where soil dries to the point it will not deliver enough shock, a mix of positive and negative wires should be used. That describes a lot of Central Texas in August.

## All-electric or woven wire with a hot wire

For a **perimeter**, especially along a county road, we build woven wire and add hot wire to it. Chargers fail in thunderstorms, limbs drop across lines, and goats notice within hours when a fence goes quiet. Woven wire still stands when the power does not. The NRCS spec itself describes permanent electric fence as mostly used for interior cross fencing, though it can serve on boundaries.

For **cross fences, rotational paddocks and brush lots**, all-electric makes sense. It goes up faster and trained goats respect it.

Our guide to the [best fence for goats and sheep](/blog/fence-for-goats-and-sheep/) covers mesh size, knot type and perimeter height, so we will stick to how the hot wire goes onto the woven wire.

### Offset hot wire on woven wire

An offset bracket holds a smooth hot wire several inches off the face of the fence. For goats it has two jobs.

- **Inside offset.** This stops goats leaning, rubbing, standing on the mesh and reaching through for grass. The Texas NRCS spec puts offset brackets every 50 to 60 feet at two-thirds the height of the animal being controlled. University of Kentucky's guide to [electric offsets](https://publications.mgcafe.uky.edu/sites/publications.ca.uky.edu/files/ID269.pdf) puts the wire at nose height, 18 to 24 inches for goats and sheep, with a second offset at 6 to 8 inches if they go under. The Tennessee bulletin adds a good detail: fasten brackets next to posts, where they hold steady, not on sagging wire mid-span. Measure a few goats before you set the height.
- **Outside low wire.** This is for coyotes, loose dogs and feral hogs working the bottom. Florida A&M puts it on the outside of the post at least 8 inches above the ground, where it catches a digging animal on the nose.

The Tennessee bulletin also describes offsets as a way to get more years from an old woven wire fence. Run smooth wire on offsets, never electrified barbed wire.

## Electric netting for goats

Electric netting is plastic twine mesh with conductive strands and built-in step-in posts. Florida A&M describes it as 8 to 13 pounds per 164 feet, with posts every 12 feet, vertical struts every 12 inches, and a height of 35 to 42 inches. It follows curves and dips without bracing, which makes it a strong tool for turning goats onto a cedar or mesquite patch and moving on. It is not a perimeter fence, and it has two weak points.

**Entanglement.** A goat tangled in netting can be badly burned or killed. Florida A&M lists the rules that prevent most of it:

1. Never let goats be on both sides of the netting at once.
2. Never use netting to separate does from their kids.
3. Never drive animals into the net.
4. Never leave it off while the herd is near it, or they lose their fear of it.
5. Confirm the charger is on and holding voltage before the herd goes into a new paddock.

Horned goats have more to catch in the mesh. If your herd carries horns, set netting where you will see it every day, and use strand fence or offsets on woven wire for anything left alone for long stretches.

**Grass load.** Netting sits low and touches everything. A paddock set in tall bermuda or johnsongrass after spring rain can drain enough voltage that the fence quits. Mow a strip first. Florida A&M also warns that some small solar chargers cannot handle heavy weed or brush contact.

## Charger strength and grounding in Central Texas soil

The charger and ground rods fail more goat fences than the wire does.

### How much voltage

The Texas NRCS spec calls for a low-impedance energizer that produces at least 4,000 volts, and sets these minimum readings at the farthest point of the fence: 2,000 volts for sheep and hair goats, 1,600 for cattle, and 1,200 for horses, hogs and meat goats. Florida A&M says a charger putting out at least 4,000 volts is generally sufficient for goats. Those are floors. On a working goat fence we want 4,000 volts or more at the far end, not just at the charger, because hair and dry footing both eat into what the goat feels. Take the reading on a dry day at the far end of the line, not at the charger on a cool morning.

### How big a charger

The Tennessee bulletin and the UGA circular [Fences for the Farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/) give the same rule of thumb: count miles of electrified wire, not miles of fence, and add 25 percent for weed and grass drain.

Say you have 20 acres outside Lorena, roughly square, and want a five-strand all-hot goat fence around it. Twenty acres is 871,200 square feet, so each side is about 933 feet and the perimeter about 3,734 feet, or 0.71 miles. Five hot strands times 0.71 is about 3.5 miles of hot wire. Add 25 percent and you want a charger rated for at least 4.4 miles. Mileage ratings on the box assume clean wire, so treat that as the bare minimum and buy headroom above it.

### Grounding in clay and caliche

The NRCS spec calls for at least three galvanized ground rods, half an inch thick and 6 feet long, 10 feet apart near the charger, with no copper mixed in. Chargers of 14 joules or more need at least 3 more feet of rod per joule. The Tennessee bulletin keeps rods at least 50 feet from any utility ground rod or buried power or phone line.

- **Blackland clay** grounds well wet and badly once it has dried and cracked. If voltage drops every August, add rods and consider a hot and ground wire system.
- **Limestone and caliche** in Bosque County and west of the river can stop a rod a foot down. Where rods cannot be driven, the Tennessee bulletin lays them end to end in a deep trench, tied together. Some places need a rock drill.

For lightning, the NRCS spec calls for an arrestor or choke with its own four ground rods about 65 feet from the charger's rods, and insulators rated for at least 10,000 volts. If your fence keeps losing voltage, see our guide to an [electric fence not working](/blog/electric-fence-not-working/).

## Training goats to a hot wire

Untrained goats should meet electric fence in a small, secure pen, not on a long perimeter with nothing behind the wire. The NRCS spec recommends a training area with at least 12 hours of exposure and notes most animals are trained within 48 hours.

Run a hot wire a few inches inside a woven wire or panel pen at nose height, so a shocked goat jumps back into solid fence instead of forward through a gap. Florida A&M notes that after a few shocks goats learn to respect the line.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many strands of electric fence do goats need?
Four is the practical minimum for trained goats on a cross fence, with the bottom hot wire no higher than 6 inches. The Texas NRCS spec gives four- and five-wire smooth layouts with a 36-inch top wire. For a permanent all-electric goat fence, Florida A&M recommends six strands of 12.5-gauge high tensile, 6 to 8 inches apart low and 8 to 12 inches apart up top.

### How high should an electric fence for goats be?
A top wire at 36 inches holds many trained goats on interior fences, and that is the height the Texas NRCS smooth-wire layouts use. For a permanent all-electric fence, jumpers or athletic breeds, plan on at least 48 inches, the height Florida A&M's goat bulletin calls for. Electric netting runs 35 to 42 inches depending on the product.

### How many volts does a goat electric fence need?
The Texas NRCS spec asks for at least 2,000 volts at the far end of the fence for sheep and hair goats, and 1,200 volts for meat goats, from a charger capable of at least 4,000 volts. Those are minimums; we like to see 4,000 volts or more at the far end. Check it with a digital voltmeter on a dry day, since long hair and dry soil both cut the shock a goat feels.

### Is electric netting safe for goats with horns?
It can be used, but horned goats have more to catch in the mesh, and a tangled goat can be badly hurt or killed. Keep the net hot at all times, never let goats get on both sides of it, never split does from kids with it, and check it daily. For fences left alone for long stretches, strand fence or offset hot wire on woven wire is the safer choice.

### Can I add a hot wire to my existing woven wire goat fence?
Yes, and it is often the cheapest fix for goats that climb, lean or reach through. Offset brackets hold a smooth hot wire off the face of the fence. The Texas NRCS spec sets them every 50 to 60 feet at two-thirds of the animal's height. A second low wire outside, at least 8 inches off the ground, helps keep coyotes and dogs from working under the fence.
