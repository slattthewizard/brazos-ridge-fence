# How to Build a Cattle Guard: An Honest Look at the Homemade Version

You can build a cattle guard in a weekend with a welder, a stack of pipe and a skid steer. A homemade cattle guard will stop cattle on a private pasture crossing, and plenty of ranchers have built them for exactly that. What it will not do, unless somebody who can run the numbers designed it, is carry a loaded feed truck or a fire engine without a rail folding or a beam sagging.

So the first question is not how to weld it. It is what will drive across it. A pickup and a side-by-side is one job. A loaded gooseneck, a hay truck and a fuel delivery is another job entirely. This guide covers the build for the first case, shows where the second case goes wrong, and gives you the numbers to decide.

## Decide what crosses it before you cut any steel

Everything about the build follows from the heaviest vehicle that will ever use the crossing. Not the daily driver. The heaviest one, including the delivery you only see twice a year.

Cattle guard load ratings borrow the highway bridge notation. An HS-20 design truck has a 32,000 pound rear axle, which puts 16,000 pounds on each wheel. For comparison, the federal weight limits on the Interstate are 20,000 pounds on a single axle and 34,000 on a tandem. A pickup carries a fraction of any of that.

Here is a rough way to sort the jobs:

- **Light duty.** Ranch pickup, ATV, side-by-side, a light stock trailer. An interior pasture crossing. A homemade pipe guard on a level bed can handle this.
- **Medium duty.** Loaded gooseneck, round bale trailer, a tractor with a loader. A homemade guard needs a real foundation and beams sized by someone who can calculate them, or you buy a rated guard.
- **Heavy duty.** Feed truck, fuel truck, concrete truck, loaded cattle pot, fire apparatus, anything on a county road. Buy a guard with a rating and a drawing behind it.

If the guard sits in a county right-of-way or is the only way in for emergency vehicles, your rural fire district can tell you what it needs to cross, and your county commissioners can tell you what they will allow in the right-of-way. Ask both before you buy steel, not after.

## What a cattle guard is made of

| Part | What it does | Typical homemade choice |
|---|---|---|
| Rails (runners) | The part animals will not walk on | Round steel pipe or tube, 2 7/8 inch or larger |
| Beams | Carry the load from the rails to the foundation | I-beam or wide-flange steel across the width |
| Foundation | Holds the beams level and keeps the load on solid ground | Concrete footings, or gravel and a box frame for light use |
| Pit | The open space under the rails that makes the guard work | 12 to 18 inches deep, with a drain |

The rails are what people see, but beams and foundation are what fail. Usually one end settles, the rails go out of level, and the next heavy axle finds the weak spot.

## Rail material, size and spacing

Use round pipe or tube. Round rails roll under a hoof, which tells an animal the footing is wrong. Angle iron and channel iron are cheap and turn up in homemade plans, but a hoof that drops between them can get stuck, and a stuck animal often breaks a leg. Sucker rod is another shortcut that looks tempting and is a poor choice under wheel loads.

For rail size, 2 7/8 inch pipe is a common choice on guards that see trucks, and larger pipe carries load better across a wider guard. Thin pipe bends, so use the heaviest wall you can get and keep the beams close enough that rails do not span far.

### The gap between rails

This is where builders disagree, and the disagreement is real. Spacing in the plans you will find runs from about one pipe diameter up to around 5 inches of clear opening, depending on who built the guard and what it is meant to hold.

Narrow gaps keep a hoof from dropping in and ride smoother under a truck. Wide gaps let an animal see the depth below, which helps turn it back, but they let a foot slide in, so wide spacing and a deep pit do not belong together.

A workable middle for mature cattle is a clear opening somewhere in the 3 to 5 inch range, with a pit deep enough that the animal does not think it can reach the bottom. If you run calves, go toward the tight end. If you run horses, skip the guard and hang a gate, because a horse that tries to cross and slips can be badly hurt. ## Pit depth, foundation and the clay problem

The pit is the working part. A pit that fills with silt turns the guard into a bridge, and cattle figure that out quickly. Plan for 12 to 18 inches of open space under the rails when the guard is finished, and plan to dig it out every few years.

### Light duty: a box on a gravel bed

For a low-traffic pasture crossing, a welded box that sits on the ground works. Weld a frame of heavy pipe, set the rails on it, close the ends with caps and skirt the sides so dirt and gravel do not push in under the rails. Set it on a crushed rock bed, level, with the top above the surrounding ground so water runs away from it.

The downside is the pit. A box on the ground has little room under it, so it fills with dirt and weeds, and once the rails are hidden a cow will try to walk them.

### Heavier duty: footings and beams

Anything that carries real weight goes on concrete: two continuous footings under the ends, beams on top, rails welded across the beams. The footing has to bear on undisturbed ground, not backfill. Poured concrete needs about a week before it takes heavy traffic and roughly 28 days to reach design strength, so plan another way in during that time.

Central Texas soil makes this harder than the plans suggest.

- **Blackland clay** swells when wet and shrinks when dry, so a footing that is shallow or poorly tied down can heave one end and settle the other. Our guide to [setting fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) shows how that movement works, and a guard footing is subject to the same thing.
- **Limestone and caliche** west of the Brazos mean the pit comes out with a hammer, not a bucket. That is the biggest variable in what an installed guard costs.
- **Flash-flood creek bottoms** will wash out a guard set low. If water has nowhere to go, the pit becomes a stock tank and the footing gets undermined.

Put a drain in the low corner of the pit, and slope the approaches so runoff goes around the guard instead of into it.

## The load rating reality

Here is the part that matters when you are deciding whether to build or buy.

A load rating is not a feature of the pipe. It is the result of a calculation that links beam size, beam spacing, span, the strength of the steel, the quality of the welds and the bearing capacity of the soil under the footing. Change any one and the rating changes.

The beam math is bending: the moment on a beam grows with both the load and the span, so a plan that works at 8 feet by 10 feet does not scale to 8 feet by 16 feet just by cutting longer pipe.

A homemade guard has no stamp, no drawing and nobody standing behind the number. That is fine on your own pasture when you are the only person at risk. It becomes a problem in three places.

1. **A county road.** If any part of the guard is in a county right-of-way, expect the commissioners to want a say in the design.
2. **A lender or insurer.** If someone else has a stake in the property, ask them what they expect at the entrance before you build, not after a loss.
3. **A heavy truck you do not control.** You can tell your own crew to unload on the far side of the guard. You cannot tell a fuel hauler or a hay trucker with a 40,000 pound load.

A simple fix is to build the guard for light traffic and put a gate beside it for everything heavy. Wide or loaded vehicles use the gate, and the guard never sees the weight it was not built for. ## Wings and the fence tie-in

A guard without wings is a guard with a gap on both sides. Cattle do not cross a pit they can walk around.

Wings are fence panels that flare out from each end of the guard and tie back to the fence line, so stock cannot step around the end. Build them from pipe or panel, tight to the ground so hogs cannot root under, and tall enough to match the fence they join.

The tie-in is where most homemade guards fall short. The wing meets the fence at an angle, and that spot takes a pull. Treat it like a corner. Set a braced post at each end of the guard, and build a proper H-brace where the wing meets the line. Our post on [fence corner brace types](/blog/fence-corner-brace-types/) walks through which brace fits which load.

Set the wing top at fence height, because a low wing is a step over for a determined cow, and armor the wing end with rock if water runs along the fence.

Extension fence guides back up the basic point. The University of Georgia says plainly that gates and cattle guards should be sturdily built and adequately supported in its [guide to fences for the farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/), and the University of Tennessee notes that stock gaps or cattle guards suit high-traffic spots in its [guide to planning and building fences on the farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf).

## Build order for a light-duty guard

Stake the footprint and wing lines, dig 12 to 18 inches of open depth plus the footings, pour the footings level end to end and side to side, and let them cure. Weld the beams and rails on the ground with a scrap spacer holding the gaps even, and cap the pipe ends. Lift the guard in, check level both ways, backfill and compact both approaches, then set the wing braces before you hang the panels. Hang a bypass gate last. On blackland, give the footing extra attention. On limestone, expect the pit to take longer than the welding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I build my own cattle guard?

Yes, for light traffic on your own land. A welded pipe guard on a level gravel bed or concrete footings will turn cattle on a pasture crossing. Where it goes wrong is a heavy vehicle crossing a guard nobody calculated, or a county road where a rated design may be expected. Build for the heaviest vehicle that will ever cross it.

### What size pipe should I use for a homemade cattle guard?

Round steel pipe or tube, 2 7/8 inch or larger, with the heaviest wall you can get. Thin pipe bends under wheel loads, especially on wide spacing. The beams under the rails matter more than the rails, so size them for the heaviest wheel, not for the pipe.

### How deep should the pit be under a cattle guard?

Plan for 12 to 18 inches of open space under the rails once the guard is set, with a drain at the low corner. Shallower pits fill with silt and lose their effect. Very deep pits are harder to clean and are a hazard if the rail gap is wide.

### How far apart should the rails be?

Builders disagree, so match the gap to your stock. For mature cattle, a clear opening of roughly 3 to 5 inches is a workable middle. Go tighter if you run calves, and avoid wide gaps over a deep pit because a foot can drop in. Build one section first and test it.

### Will a homemade cattle guard hold a feed truck?

Only if the beams, rails and footings were designed for that axle load, and a guess is not a design. A loaded feed or fuel truck is well above what a pickup puts on a guard. For heavy trucks, buy a rated guard or build a bypass gate and keep the trucks off the guard entirely.
