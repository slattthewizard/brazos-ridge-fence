# Cross Fencing a Pasture: Build the Inside Light, Build the Outside Right

Cross fencing is the interior fence that splits one big pasture into smaller ones so you can rest grass, separate stock and move animals on a plan. It does not have to be built like your perimeter fence. The boundary fence is the one that keeps cattle off the road and out of the neighbor's oats, so it gets the heavy wire, the tight post spacing and the good corners. Interior fence only has to hold animals standing on grass with water, and a two-wire high-tensile electric line often does that.

That split is where most of the money in a pasture plan is saved or wasted. Spend it on the outside line and on corners and gates. Save it on the inside, where a failure means cows in the wrong paddock instead of cows on a highway.

## Perimeter fence vs interior fence: two different jobs

The NRCS Texas fence specification (Conservation Practice Code 382) draws the same line most builders do. It describes permanent fences as used for "exterior (boundary) fencing" and for "interior division fencing (cross fencing)", and it says permanent electric fence is mostly used for interior cross fencing. Temporary electric fence, in that spec, is only for interior use where livestock pressure is light. The full [NRCS Texas Fence Code 382 specification](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf) is worth a read.

The reason is pressure. A perimeter fence takes the worst of everything: a bull who smells a cow on the other side, a herd spooked by a loose dog at night, hogs rooting along the bottom, cedar falling on it in an ice storm, and the grass that always looks better across the line. An interior fence divides two pastures you own. The stock on both sides are yours and they were just moved onto fresh grass.

Here is how the two usually compare on a Central Texas cattle place:

| | Perimeter (boundary) fence | Interior (cross) fence |
|---|---|---|
| Typical build | 5-strand barbed wire or net wire with barbed on top | 1 to 3 strands of high-tensile smooth wire, electrified |
| Line posts | Steel T-posts and wood, often 12 to 16 ft apart | Far wider spacing is allowed on high-tensile |
| Corners and braces | Full H-braces at every corner, gate and change of direction | Braces at ends and gates, lighter elsewhere |
| Which side the wire goes on | The side facing the animals | Either side |
| What a failure costs | Livestock on a road or a neighbor's place | Cattle in the wrong paddock |

That wire-side detail comes straight from the NRCS spec: boundary fences are built with the wire on the side of the post facing the animals you are controlling, and cross fence wire may go on either side. When a cow leans on a boundary fence, she pushes wire into posts, not staples out of wood.

### What the NRCS spec allows for each

For cattle, the Texas 382 table calls for barbed wire fences with at least 4 wires and a top wire at 44 inches or higher. For high-tensile smooth electric wire, it lists 1-wire and 2-wire fences, then footnotes both as "for use as cross-fence in a grazing system" and "not recommended for exterior fences." The 4-wire version (top wire 42 to 60 inches) is footnoted as an interior or boundary fence, and the 5-wire version with a top wire at 50 to 60 inches is described as a boundary fence.

It also allows high-tensile electric line posts up to 100 feet apart without stays, 150 feet with them. On rolling ground west of the Brazos you set a post at every high and low point anyway, so real spacing ends up well inside that.

## Why cheap interior fence works

Cattle respect a hot wire once they have been trained to it. The NRCS spec recommends training animals in a well-fenced area with an electric wire across it, with at least 12 hours of exposure, and says most animals are trained within 48 hours. Skip that step and a new set of calves will walk through a single hot wire the first time something startles them.

High-tensile wire also holds up. The University of Tennessee Extension guide [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm (PB1541)](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) describes 12 1/2 gauge high-tensile wire with a breaking strength of about 1,800 pounds, and says high-tensile fence should be used with electricity. It notes that 8 to 10 wires are recommended for a non-electric high-tensile cattle fence, but 3 to 5 are enough if the fence is electrified. For a cross fence between two pastures of the same herd, the NRCS table goes as low as 1 or 2 wires.

That means fewer wires, far fewer posts and faster building. High-tensile electric has no line on our published price table, but the UT Extension guide lists lower building cost than most conventional fences among its advantages. The [ranch fence cost page](/ranch-fence-cost/) has the published ranges for barbed wire, net wire, pipe and the rest.

### Where electric cross fence gives trouble in Central Texas

- **Dry clay grounding.** Blackland clay cracks open in August and a single ground rod can stop doing its job. The NRCS spec says that where soil dries out enough to weaken the shock, you should use a mix of positive and negative wires instead of an all-hot fence.
- **Brush and weeds.** Cedar sprouts, mesquite and johnsongrass against a hot wire drain the charge. A cleared strip under the fence is part of the job, not an extra. Our [fence line clearing](/fence-line-clearing-waco/) page covers what that work involves.
- **Other stock.** A 2-wire hot fence is a cattle fence. Goats walk through it, and feral hogs go under it.

## Laying out cross fences for rotation

### Start with water

Every paddock needs water, and it has to be close enough that cattle will graze the far side. Virginia Cooperative Extension's guide [Using Temporary Watering and Fencing in a Rotational Grazing System (SPES-710P)](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/SPES/spes-710/spes-710.html) says pasture is poorly used beyond 800 to 1,000 feet from the water source, and less than that on steep ground.

Run the numbers on a hypothetical. Say you have 80 acres outside McGregor, roughly square. That is about 1,867 feet on a side. Split it into four 20-acre paddocks and each one is about 933 feet square. Put the water at the outside corner and the far corner of that paddock is about 1,320 feet away. Move the water to the middle of one side and the farthest point drops to about 1,040 feet. Put the four paddocks around one water point where their corners meet, and all four drink from the same trough, though the far corners are still about 1,320 feet out. On 80 acres that argues for a second water point or more, smaller paddocks.

PB1541 says the same thing from the other direction: place fences so one water source can serve two, three or more pastures.

### Gates in corners, not the middle

PB1541 advises against putting gates in the middle of a straight fence and says corners are best. Cattle moving along a fence line will follow it into a corner, and a gate there lets them flow through instead of balking. It also recommends putting gates in the corner of each field closest to the buildings, and says a lane should connect working facilities and water to every field that may be grazed.

Plan the lane and gates before any wire goes up. Moving a gate later means cutting wire and setting a new brace.

### Follow the land

The NRCS spec says to lay out interior fences on the shortest and straightest lines you can, to avoid irregular terrain and water crossings where possible, and to consider fencing along the contour to cut down on cattle trailing and erosion. In Bosque County, that often means letting a cross fence follow a limestone ledge instead of climbing over it. On blackland, it means keeping gates and lanes on the higher ground so they are not a mud hole every February.

If a cross fence has to cross a creek, it needs a water gap that can give way in a flood without taking the whole fence with it. The post on [water gap fences](/blog/water-gap-fence/) walks through the options.

## What never gets skimped: road frontage, corners and gates

### Road frontage

Texas law is plain about highways. The AgriLife Extension publication [A Landowner's Guide to Fence Law in Texas (EAG-029)](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Texas-Fencing-Law-Information.pdf) explains that land along U.S. and state highways is treated as closed range, and that a person responsible for livestock may not "knowingly permit" them to roam at large on a highway right-of-way. The guide notes that farm-to-market roads are open range unless a local stock law changes that. In one court case the guide describes, a landowner was found to have acted knowingly when he knew his fences could not stand up to heavy rain, his cattle had escaped through them before, and he had not inspected them before the accident.

So any fence that faces a highway or county road is a perimeter fence of the heaviest kind you build. Five strands of barbed wire or net wire with barbed on top, braced properly, checked after every storm. If you want an electric wire on it, add it as an offset to a sound fence, not as the fence itself. Our post on [cattle on road liability in Texas](/blog/cattle-on-road-liability-texas/) goes further into who pays when a herd gets out.

### Corners and braces

A high-tensile cross fence is only as tight as its end braces. The wire is stretched hard, and PB1541 notes that high-tensile fence needs treated posts set properly with bracing that can take that pull. On blackland clay, a brace that is too light or too shallow will lean inside two summers. The [H-brace basics](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) post shows what a corner assembly should look like.

### Gates

Interior gates get used more than perimeter gates, sometimes every few days in a rotation. Hang them on posts that are set deep and braced, and make them wide enough for whatever you will drive through them. A cheap gate on a cheap post is the first thing that fails in a paddock system.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is cross fencing?
Cross fencing is interior fence that divides one pasture into two or more smaller ones. Ranchers use it to rotate grazing, rest grass, separate cattle by class or keep stock off a field for a season. It sits entirely inside your own property, so it does not have to carry the load a boundary fence does, and it is often built lighter, commonly as high-tensile electric wire on cattle places.

### Can I use electric fence for my perimeter fence?
You can, but it is not what belongs along a road. The NRCS Texas fence spec says 1-wire and 2-wire high-tensile electric fences are for cross fencing in a grazing system and are not recommended for exterior fences. It lists 4-wire and 5-wire electric fences, with the top wire up to 60 inches, as boundary options. Along a highway, the safer build is barbed wire or net wire, with a hot wire added as an offset if needed.

### How far apart should cross fence posts be?
It depends on the fence. Barbed wire and net wire need close spacing; UT Extension lists 12 to 14 feet for barbed wire and 14 to 16 for woven wire. The NRCS Texas spec allows high-tensile electric line posts up to 100 feet apart without stays and 150 feet with them, but posts also have to be set at every high and low point. On rolling or rocky ground west of the Brazos, spacing ends up tighter than the maximum.

### How many paddocks do I need for rotational grazing?
There is no single number. It depends on your acreage, your herd, how often you want to move stock and where water is. Start from water: Virginia Cooperative Extension says pasture is poorly used beyond 800 to 1,000 feet from water, so paddock size and shape should keep every corner within reach of a trough. A few well-watered paddocks beat many badly watered ones.

### How much does cross fencing cost?
It depends on what you build. Our published range for 5-strand barbed wire is $2.50 to $5.50 per foot and net wire runs $3.50 to $8 per foot, both on the [ranch fence cost page](/ranch-fence-cost/). High-tensile electric is generally cheaper to build than conventional fence, but brush, rock and post spacing move every number, so get the cross fence priced after someone walks the line.
