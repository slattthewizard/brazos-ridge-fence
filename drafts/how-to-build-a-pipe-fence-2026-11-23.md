# How to Build a Pipe Fence: Layout, Posts, Rails and Welds, in Order

A pipe fence goes up in six steps: stake and string the line, set the corner and gate posts, set the line posts, cut the tops, weld the rails, then cap and paint. Do them in that order and the fence comes out straight. Skip ahead, say by welding a rail to posts that haven't been lined up, and you'll see the mistake from the road for as long as the fence stands.

## Before you cut anything: layout

Most pipe fence problems start on paper, or rather on the ground.

**Know where the line is.** If the fence runs along a neighbor's boundary, get the pins located first. A welded pipe fence is not something you move over a foot next spring.

**Decide the style before you order.** The number of rails (3, 4 or 5), or a pipe top rail over mesh, drives the amount of pipe and the welding hours. The [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page lists welded pipe at $12 to $28 per foot installed, which is a wide band because rail count, pipe, ground and gates all move it. At that range a quarter mile (1,320 feet) lands somewhere between about $15,840 and $36,960. For a feel of which build fits which animal, see the [pipe fence page](/pipe-fence-waco/).

**Set the post spacing.** Rail fences are normally built on 8 to 10 foot post spacing, which the University of Georgia's horse fence bulletin gives for rail fences ([UGA "Fences for Horses," B 1192](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/)). Eight feet is stiffer and suits pens and horse lots. Ten feet saves posts on a long perimeter. Math it out so the spacing is even. On a 1,320 foot run, 10 foot spacing takes 133 posts and 8 foot spacing takes 166, counting the end posts. Spread a few extra feet over each bay rather than finishing with one short bay at the end.

## Corners and gate posts first

Set the corners, the ends and both sides of every gate before any line post. Everything else is strung off them.

**Why corners come first.** A corner or gate post takes more load than any other post in the fence. A gate hangs a few hundred pounds on a lever arm, and a corner takes the pull of rails or wire running two directions. Those posts also decide where the line lies. Get them plumb and in the right place, then run a string between them.

**Depth and concrete.** The NRCS Texas fence specification (Conservation Practice Code 382, linked in the [2015 PDF hosted by AgriLife](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf)) calls for brace posts buried at least 3 feet unless they're driven. UGA's horse fence bulletin puts end posts and brace posts at about 4 feet. The NRCS document also says concrete-set posts should sit in a hole at least 12 inches across, filled completely, and crowned at the top so water runs off the post instead of pooling at it.

That crowning matters. On a pipe post, the weak point is the ground line, where water sits against steel.

**Blackland clay.** East of the Brazos the clay swells wet and shrinks dry, and it will grab a smooth pipe and push on it. The same NRCS document notes that on Blackland soils a post hole may be filled with concrete to within 12 inches of the surface, with soil backfilled on top. Our guide to [setting fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers how that soil moves. West of the Brazos and in Bosque County, limestone and caliche mean a rock auger, not a post-hole digger: see [setting fence posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/).

## Setting the line posts

Stretch a string from corner to corner, pulled tight, at the height you want the top of the posts or at a fixed offset from it. Mark every post position with a stake or paint.

**Plumb in two directions.** Check each post from the side and from the end with a level. A post that leans along the fence line throws the rail off at the weld. A post that leans across it ruins the line. Brace it with two stakes or scrap pipe until the concrete takes hold.

**Let the concrete set.** Wait for the concrete to harden before you weld rails or hang weight on the posts. How long depends on the mix and the weather, so check the bag. Welding rails to a post that is still moving in wet concrete gives you crooked rails with a lot of stress in the welds.

## Cutting the post tops

Once the posts are set and the concrete has hardened, cut the tops to a consistent height. There are two common finishes.

- **Flush tops with the rail welded across the top.** A clean, continuous look along frontage. Cut each post at the same height off the string or a laser line and weld the top rail across the tops.
- **Posts standing proud of the top rail.** The posts rise a few inches above the rail and take caps. It reads more like a board fence, and the caps can echo the entrance.

Cap every post either way. An open pipe collects rainwater, and the NRCS Texas specification requires pipe posts to be permanently capped to keep rain out and either painted or galvanized. A welded flat plate or a pressed cap does it. Cap posts before you paint, and seal the weld all the way around.

## Welding the rails

This is where the build takes the most time, and where a careful beginner can do fine work if they take it slowly.

### Fit-up before you strike an arc

Hold the rail against the post, level it, and clamp it. Check the fit. A gap you fill with weld is weaker than a joint that was cut to fit, and it looks worse.

Where a rail meets a round post, two common joints work:

- **Rail butted on the post face.** Quick, and common on pipe rail fence. The end of the rail is cut square and meets the post, so a fillet weld runs around a curved contact line. Gaps show on the sides.
- **Saddled or fish-mouthed rail.** The end of the rail is cut with a curve (a notch, or a hole saw cut at the right size) so it wraps the post. This fits tighter, welds cleaner and looks better. It takes more time to cut. A grinder or a hole saw sized to the post will do it.

### Tack, check, then weld

Tack every rail in place first. Step back and look down the line. Check that the rails are level or follow the grade you meant them to. Then weld the joints out. Weld all the way around where you can reach, or at least on both sides. A rail tacked on one side is the first thing a cow rubbing on the fence pops loose.

### Welding safety on the fence line

Treat a fence weld like any other hot work.

- **Fire.** A Texas pasture in August is a fuel bed. Mow or clear a wide area around the weld, keep water and a shovel handy, and watch the grass downwind. Sparks from grinding can start a fire as well.
- **Galvanized and zinc-coated metal.** OSHA's page on welding hazards names zinc oxide from galvanized steel and zinc-enriched paint as the usual cause of metal fume fever, with fever, nausea and coughing that last about a day ([OSHA, welding and respiratory hazards](https://www.osha.gov/ship-building-repair/welding)). Grind the coating back from the joint, work in moving air and stay out of the plume.

## Pipe fence corners

On a full pipe fence, corners are welded in the rail line. At a corner the rails of the two runs meet the corner post, so weld each rail to the post on both runs. Use a larger pipe for the corner post and the gate post, as covered in the [pipe size guide](/blog/what-size-pipe-for-pipe-fence/).

On a pipe fence with wire or cable under a top rail, the corner has to resist wire pull, not just animal contact. That takes a brace. The NRCS specification calls for an H-brace or a welded brace where the fence changes direction by 20 degrees or more, with welded steel assemblies painted for rust protection. Its minimum for steel upright and brace pipe is 2 3/8 inch outside diameter.

## Building a pipe fence on uneven ground

Hills and dips are where pipe fence looks the best or the worst.

**Follow the grade or step it.** You can run rails parallel to the slope, or step them level between posts. Pick one for each stretch. Following the grade usually looks more natural on rolling ground. Stepped rails look right on short, steep drops, such as a pen floor. A random mix looks wrong.

**Keep post height consistent above the ground.** On a slope, measure every post's top from the ground, not from a level line. A fence measured off a level line ends up with the rails at different heights above the ground along the slope.

**Dips and creek crossings.** A long dip under a pipe rail leaves a gap that animals will use. Close it with extra rails, or with a post set at the low point so the gap stays small. Flood-prone creek crossings are a different problem: a solid welded fence across a flood path catches debris and takes damage. Think hard about whether a solid rail belongs across a creek at all.

## Gates

Gate posts are the most heavily loaded in the fence. Set them first, make the opening a bit wider than the gate to leave room for the hinges and latch, and let the concrete fully harden before you hang the gate. ## Pipe fence without welding

You can build a pipe fence without welding, with limits.

- **Bolted or clamped rail fittings.** Manufactured brackets and clamps hold rail pipe to the post. They suit smaller builds and tend to loosen as animals rub on them. Check the maker's load rating and look at what the fitting is made of.
- **Pipe posts with cable or wire.** Posts can carry cable or wire fastened with eyelets or clips rather than welded rails. It's a different fence.

For a corral, an entrance or any fence where a failed joint puts an animal on a road, welded joints are what most builders use. A structural weld on a corner, a gate post or an overhead header is a job for a certified welder. If you can't weld well, build the posts and rails you can handle and hire out the structural pieces.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What order do you build a pipe fence in?

Mark and string the line, set corner and gate posts, set line posts to the string, let the concrete harden, cut the tops, weld the rails, then cap, clean and paint. Gates are hung last. Doing the rails before the posts have hardened or been lined up is the usual way to end up with a crooked fence.

### How deep should pipe fence posts be set?

The NRCS Texas fence specification puts brace posts at a minimum of 3 feet unless they're driven, and UGA's horse fence bulletin gives about 4 feet for end and brace posts. Gate and corner posts go deepest. In blackland clay and in rock the practical depth changes, so [get the ground looked at](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) first.

### How far apart should pipe fence posts be?

Rail fences are normally built on 8 to 10 foot spacing, per the UGA horse fence bulletin. Eight feet is stiffer and suits pens and horse lots, and 10 feet saves posts on long runs. Divide the run evenly, so the spacing is the same all the way down the line and no bay ends up short at the end.

### Can you build a pipe fence without welding?

Yes, for lighter jobs, using bolted or clamped rail fittings or by running cable between posts. Expect fittings to loosen as animals rub on them, and check the maker's load rating before you buy. Corners, gate posts and entrances that carry weight, or that keep stock off a road, are better welded.

### Should pipe fence rails follow the slope or stay level?

Either works if you stay consistent along each stretch. Following the grade looks more natural on rolling ground, and stepped rails suit short, steep drops. Pick one for each stretch rather than mixing them, and measure each post top from the ground rather than from a level line, so the rails sit at a steady height above the dirt.
