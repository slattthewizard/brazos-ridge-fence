# How to Splice Barbed Wire: Western Union, Loop Splices, Crimp Sleeves and Gripples

There are four ways to splice barbed wire that hold up on a working cattle fence: a Western Union wrap, a loop splice (the figure 8 is a cousin of it), a crimp sleeve, or a tool-free joiner like a Gripple. The wrap and the loop cost nothing but fencing pliers and some wrist. Sleeves and joiners cost a little per splice and are faster and easier to get right the first time. Pick by what is in your truck, what gauge the wire is, and how many splices you have to make.

The splice itself is the easy part. The hard part is getting two ends back together under tension without the wire taking a piece of you, and knowing when a strand has too many splices in it to be worth another.

## What you need before you start

Have all of this on the tailgate before you cut anything.

- **Leather gloves and eye protection.** A barbed strand that lets go under tension coils back fast, and the barbs lead.
- **Fencing pliers.** The multi-tool kind with a cutter, hammer face, staple puller and wire grip.
- **A wire stretcher.** A come-along with wire grips, a chain-type fence stretcher, or a bar strainer. You cannot pull two ends of 12.5 gauge barbed wire together by hand on a line that was tight.
- **A short length of new wire.** Broken ends curl back and lose length, so most breaks need a patch piece, which means two splices. Match the gauge and type of the existing strand. Our post on [barbed wire gauge](/blog/barbed-wire-gauge/) covers 12.5 gauge mild steel versus 15.5 gauge high-tensile, and it matters here.
- **Sleeves and a crimp tool, or joiners**, if you are going that route.

## Safety with a loaded wire

The real danger in a repair is cutting or releasing a strand that is still tight. When you cut a stretched line, both ends travel.

1. **Take the load off first.** Hook your stretcher across the spot you plan to cut, grip the wire on both sides, and take up a little tension so the stretcher is holding the load, not the wire. Now the cut releases almost nothing.
2. **Stand to the side, never in line with the wire.** If a grip slips, the wire runs along its own line.
3. **Pull staples or clips a few posts each side** if you need slack. A strand pinned tight in its staples will not slide toward your splice.
4. **Watch the whole strand.** Old wire can break somewhere else in the span when you put a stretcher on it. If it does, stop and look at the rest of the run.

A strand that has already snapped has dumped its tension. The risk there is in pulling it back up, so ease tension on rather than jerking it. And keep cattle off that pasture while a strand is down. One slack wire is all it takes to teach a cow the fence gives.

## Splice 1: the Western Union wrap

The Western Union splice needs no parts. The Texas NRCS fence specification calls it the preferred splice for standard (non high-tensile) wire and asks for a minimum of four wraps on each side of center, "tightly wound and closely spaced" ([NRCS Texas Practice Specification, Fence Code 382, 2021](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/29884/382_TX_PS_Fence_2021)). The 2015 edition of the same spec asked for eight, so four is a floor, not a target.

How to tie it on barbed wire:

1. Pull the ends together with the stretcher until they overlap 10 to 12 inches, enough tail for four or more tight wraps a side.
2. Clear the overlap. Barbs keep the turns from seating, so knock them off the tails with the pliers or set the center of the splice between two barbs.
3. Cross the wires at the center and hold that point with the pliers.
4. Wrap the first tail around the standing wire, each turn touching the last, pulling every turn snug with the pliers. Finger-tight wraps slide under load.
5. Wrap the second tail around the other standing wire, going the opposite way.
6. Snip the tails close and bend the ends in so they do not catch hide or hands.
7. Release the stretcher slowly and watch the splice. If the wraps creep, redo it.

If you only learn one splice, learn this one. Its weak point is the person tying it. Barbed wire is two twisted strands, so it takes more wrist than smooth wire to wrap tight, and loose, spaced-out wraps will pull apart the first time a steer leans on the strand.

## Splice 2: the loop splice and the figure 8

A loop splice joins two wires with interlocking loops. Form a small loop in one end and lock it by wrapping the tail back around its own standing part. Pass the other wire through that loop, pull it up to tension, then loop it back and wrap its tail around itself. The Texas NRCS spec asks for at least four wraps on each side here too.

Its edge on a repair: you can pull tension through the first loop before you finish the last wraps, which makes it forgiving when you are working alone.

The figure 8 is two interlocking loops that draw tight as the fence is stretched, and Texas NRCS lists it for high-tensile wire. University of Maine Cooperative Extension calls it the most efficient knot for splicing high-tensile wire and puts its hold at up to 76% of the wire's strength ([UMaine Extension, working with high tensile fence wire](https://extension.umaine.edu/livestock/pasture-course/lesson-3/working-with-high-tensile-fence-wire/)). On 15.5 gauge high-tensile barbed wire, which is springy and hard to wrap tight, a figure 8 or a sleeve usually beats a wrap.

Barbed wire tells the same story. The British Columbia agriculture ministry's [fence wire factsheet](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/agriculture-and-seafood/farm-management/structures-and-mechanization/300-series/307100-2_fence_wire_-_dispensing_stapling.pdf) says the common knots used to join and tie off barbed wire hold about 80 percent of the wire's breaking strength, and that no knot is as strong as the wire it joins.

So a hand-tied knot is a good splice, not a full-strength one. That is usually fine. On a strand that gets hit hard, like a water gap or a corner where cattle crowd, use a sleeve.

## Splice 3: crimp sleeves

A crimp sleeve is a short metal tube. The two wire ends go through it from opposite sides and a crimp tool squeezes it down onto the wire. UMaine Extension puts a crimped sleeve splice at 100% of wire strength on high-tensile smooth wire.

On barbed wire, three things matter.

**Buy sleeves made for barbed wire.** Two twisted strands will not fit a sleeve sized for single 12.5 gauge smooth wire. Stay-Tuff, for one, sells separate barbed wire crimp sleeves for 12.5 to 15.5 gauge barbed wire and says they exceed the wire's breaking strength ([Stay-Tuff splicing sleeves](https://staytuff.com/product/splicing-sleeves/)). Read the pack.

**Use enough of them.** The Texas NRCS spec allows sleeves on standard and high-tensile wire if the splice holds at least 80% of the wire's strength, installed to the maker's instructions, with two 3/8-inch sleeves or one 3/4-inch sleeve at each splice.

**Crimp between the barbs.** A barb inside the sleeve keeps it from closing evenly, so clear the barbs off that length first.

The steps:

1. Slide the sleeves (two short or one long) onto the first wire end.
2. Pull the ends together with the stretcher until they overlap through the sleeve length plus a few inches.
3. Feed the second end through the sleeve from the opposite direction.
4. Crimp each sleeve fully with the matching notch on the tool, in the order the maker shows. A half-crimped sleeve looks finished and slips later.
5. Release the stretcher and check that nothing moved.

The catch with sleeves: a crimp is permanent. If the strand was too tight or too loose when you crimped, cut it out and start over.

## Splice 4: Gripple-type joiners

A Gripple is a small housing with a ceramic wedge inside. Wire pushes in one way and locks against pulling back out. Gripple makes two barbed wire versions, and they are not interchangeable ([Gripple barbed wire joiners](https://www.gripple.com/us/products/wire-joining-and-tensioning/barbed/)):

| Joiner | Wire it fits | Maximum working load (maker's figure) |
|---|---|---|
| Gripple Barbed Low Carbon Steel | 12.5 gauge mild steel barbed wire | 330 lbs |
| Gripple Barbed High Tensile | 15 to 14.5 gauge high-tensile barbed wire | 880 lbs |

Those are working loads, not break strengths, so read them as how hard Gripple says you should load the joint in service. For scale, the BC factsheet above puts nonelectric wire fences, barbed included, at roughly 250 pounds of running tension. The mild steel joiner clears that, but not by a wide margin, which is one more reason not to over-stretch a mild steel strand. (The same factsheet credits the Gripple splice with 100 percent of wire strength at break, which is a different measure from the working load.)

Check the gauge range on the pack against your wire before you buy a bucket of them. Gripple's instructions: cut the wire at a barb, leaving at least 3 inches, with the end tightly wound; push the first wire in until it sits tight against the barb; push the second in from the other side; then tension from the end post. That last step matters. The barbed joiner joins. It does not tension, so you still need a stretcher or you re-tension from the brace.

Joiners are the easiest splice to get right, and the pick when an ice storm drops cedar limbs across a quarter mile of fence.

## Which splice to use

| Situation | Best splice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One break, only pliers in the truck | Western Union | No parts, holds well if the wraps are tight |
| Working alone | Loop splice | Pull tension through the loop before you finish |
| 15.5 gauge high-tensile barbed | Figure 8, or a sleeve or joiner rated for that gauge | Stiff wire does not wrap tight by hand |
| Water gap, corner, high-pressure spot | Crimp sleeves | Closest to full wire strength |
| Dozens of splices after a storm | Gripple-type joiners | Fast, consistent, no tool |

When you join new wire to old, match the type. Splicing high-tensile barbed into a mild steel strand puts two wires with different stretch in one line, and the splice is where the difference shows. New [Class 3 galvanized wire](/blog/class-3-galvanized-wire/) spliced into old Class 1 will outlast everything around it; just do not expect the old wire to follow suit.

## Splice it or replace the run

A splice fixes one spot. Before you make one, look at the wire on either side of the break.

Splice when the break has a clear cause (a limb, a hog, a truck, a tangled cow), the wire on either side bends without cracking and takes tension without snapping elsewhere, the span has one or two splices in it, and the posts and braces are solid.

Think about replacing the strand or the run when the wire breaks again while you stretch it, when old splices every few spans are pulling apart, when barbs are loose or rusting off, or when sag comes back within weeks. That last one is almost always a brace or post problem, and no splice fixes it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you splice barbed wire without special tools?

Yes. A Western Union wrap or a loop splice needs only fencing pliers and gloves, plus a stretcher to pull the ends together if the line was tight. Clear the barbs from the overlap, cross the wires, and wrap each tail in tight, closely spaced turns around the other wire. The current Texas NRCS fence spec uses four wraps on each side as its minimum, and its 2015 edition asked for eight, so more is better.

### Are Gripples as strong as barbed wire?

Gripple publishes a maximum working load rather than a break strength: 330 lbs for its low carbon steel joiner (12.5 gauge mild steel barbed wire) and 880 lbs for its high-tensile barbed joiner. The BC agriculture ministry's fence wire factsheet credits the Gripple splice with full wire strength at break. The mild steel working load clears normal fence tension, but not by much, so do not over-stretch a strand that has joiners in it.

### Do crimp sleeves work on barbed wire?

They do if you buy sleeves sized for barbed wire. Two twisted strands will not fit a sleeve made for single smooth wire. Clear the barbs where the sleeve sits, use two short sleeves or one long one per splice as the Texas NRCS spec describes, and crimp each one fully with the matching notch on the tool.

### How many splices can one strand have before it should be replaced?

There is no set number, but a strand with splices every few spans is telling you something. If the wire breaks again while you stretch it, if old splices are pulling apart, or if barbs are rusting off, another splice buys weeks, not years. At that point replacing the strand or the run costs less than the repeat trips.

### Can I splice new barbed wire onto old barbed wire?

Yes, as long as the old wire still bends without cracking and holds tension. Match gauge and type: mild steel to mild steel, high-tensile to high-tensile, since mixing them puts two wires with different stretch in one strand. If the old wire snaps when you pull it up, the splice was never the problem, and the run is due for replacement.
