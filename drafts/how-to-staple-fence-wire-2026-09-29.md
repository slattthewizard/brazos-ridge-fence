# How to Staple Fence Wire: Staple Size, Angle and the Loose-Staple Rule

For barbed wire or net wire on wood posts, use 9-gauge, hot-dip galvanized fence staples 1.75 inches long, or 2 inches in treated pine. Barbed-shank staples hold best. Drive each one diagonally across the wood grain, give it a slight turn so the legs spread, and stop before the crown pins the wire. On line posts the wire should be able to slide. The only places it gets fastened tight are the tie-off posts at the ends of a run.

The rest is detail, and the detail decides whether your staples are still in the post in ten years.

## Fence staple size: 1.5, 1.75 or 2 inch

Staples are sold by leg length and gauge. For ranch fence the gauge is almost always 9. Leg length is where people go wrong.

A staple has to straddle the wire, so part of each leg is spent going around a 12.5-gauge strand before any of it reaches wood. A short staple ends up with very little leg in the post. The British Columbia agriculture ministry's [fence wire stapling factsheet](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/farming-natural-resources-and-industry/agriculture-and-seafood/farm-management/structures-and-mechanization/300-series/307100-2_fence_wire_-_dispensing_stapling.pdf) sets 9 gauge by 1-3/4 inch as the minimum for exactly that reason, and prefers 2 inch. Staples are a tiny part of the cost of a mile of fence, so going up a size is cheap insurance.

| Staple (9 gauge) | Approx. count per pound | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| 1.5 inch | 72 | Dense, dry hardwood; small repairs where a longer leg would split a narrow post |
| 1.75 inch | 61 | The everyday size for barbed and net wire on cedar and most wood posts |
| 2 inch | 58 | Treated pine, big corner and brace posts, anywhere pull-out has been a problem |

Counts per pound are from the BC factsheet above and vary a little by maker.

Here is what that means on the ground. Say you are building a quarter mile (1,320 feet) of 5-strand barbed wire with wood line posts every 12 feet. That is about 110 posts, and 110 posts times 5 strands is 550 staples. At 61 per pound, you need roughly 9 pounds of 1.75 inch staples, plus extra for the braces, tie-offs and the ones that fly off into the grass.

## Slash-cut points and barbed-shank staples

Look at the tip of a fence staple and you will see the points are not centered like a nail. Each leg is cut on a slant, and that is called a slash-cut point. As a slash-cut leg enters wood, it curves away from the flat face of the cut. Set a staple the right way and the two legs spread apart inside the post. Set it the wrong way and they cross over each other.

That difference is not small. Both the BC factsheet and the University of Georgia's [Fences for the Farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/) report that staples turned the right way have about 40 percent more resistance to pulling out than staples turned the wrong way.

Barbed-shank staples take that further. Each leg has a small cut that raises a barb. It slides into the post easily and grips like a fishhook if anything tries to pull it back out.

### Galvanizing matters as much as shape

Buy hot-dip galvanized staples. The rough zinc surface lasts longer and grips the wood better than bright or smooth-coated staples. A staple rusts at the same spot the wire rubs, and once the staple goes, the wire starts working against the post. If you paid for Class 3 wire, match it with staples that will last as long. ## Stapling cedar vs treated pine

The two posts you see most on Central Texas fence lines take staples very differently.

**Treated pine** is soft, and the preservative makes it slick. The University of Tennessee's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) names staple pull-out as a common problem on pressure-treated softwood posts, blaming the soft wood and the lubricating effect of the treatment. Their answer, and UGA's, is 1.75 or 2 inch, 8 or 9 gauge, hot-dip galvanized staples with cut points and barbs. Pine takes a staple easily going in. Keeping it there is the job.

**Cedar** here usually means Ashe juniper. Sound heartwood is dense and holds a staple well. Both extension guides allow shorter staples in untreated hardwood posts because staples do not pull out of them as easily, but cedar is not oak, and not every cedar post is sound heartwood. The catch with cedar is what the post looks like. Many are crooked, carry bark and soft sapwood on the outside, and check (crack) as they dry through a Texas summer. A staple driven into bark or a check holds nothing, and a staple driven along a check can split the post open. Pick a spot on solid wood, stay off the cracks, and use 1.75 inch as the default rather than going shorter. ## How to staple barbed wire to a wood post, step by step

This works the same for barbed wire and smooth wire. Net wire has one extra rule, covered further down.

1. **Put the wire on the pressure side.** UGA says to hang the wire on the side of the post nearest the livestock, unless appearance matters more, and Tennessee says to string wire on the outside of curves. The idea is the same: when an animal leans on the fence or the wire pulls around a bend, it pushes the wire into the post, not away from it and against the staples.
2. **Mark your strand heights** with a notched stick so every strand sits at the same height post to post.
3. **Hold the wire to the post with your hand.** Do not use the staple to drag the wire over to the post. That bends the staple and loads it before the fence is even finished.
4. **Set the staple across the grain.** Never line the staple legs up with the grain, which is how posts split. Tip it off vertical so the legs straddle the grain.
5. **Give it a slight twist off square.** Tennessee's guide says to rotate the staple about 25 degrees from the flat surface of the point, which is what makes the legs spread. The BC factsheet notes most staples come with the same slash, so they usually get turned slightly clockwise. Check a few on scrap wood and you will know your box.
6. **Drive it most of the way, not all of the way.** Stop when the crown is just clear of the wire. The strand should slide through the staple when you pull on it.
7. **Do not hit the wire.** A missed hammer blow knocks the zinc off the wire, and that spot will rust first. Slow down on the last blow.

## Staple angle on rises, dips and corners

Where the fence line changes direction, up, down or sideways, the wire pushes on the post and the staple takes that load. The BC factsheet lays out the standard rules:

- **Rise posts** (the post at the top of a hump, where the wire presses down): angle the staple downward into the post. Double staple on steep rises.
- **Dip posts** (the post in a low spot or creek swale, where the wire pulls up): angle the staple upward. Double staple on steep dips.
- **Shallow corners**: one staple plus a slider staple, so the wire can still move around the post.
- **Sharp corners**: two staples plus two slider staples.

Dip posts are the ones to watch. When the wire lifts a staple out, the fence rises off the ground and leaves a gap for calves and hogs. If a dip post keeps pulling out, a bigger staple will not fix it. The post itself needs anchoring. For true direction changes, the post should be a braced corner, and our post on [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) explains why.

## Loose on line posts, tight at the ends

A line post staple is a guide, not a clamp. The BC factsheet says to staple fence wire loose everywhere except the tie-off points. Loose stapling lets the wire move along the fence, so when a tree drops on it or a cow leans on it, the load spreads across many spans instead of one. UGA says the same in plain terms: hold the wire close to the post but not tight.

Driving staples tight causes three problems:

- The staple crushes and nicks the wire, and the zinc goes first right there.
- The wire cannot slide to even out tension, so one span takes all the load and breaks or sags.
- Summer heat and winter cold make the wire expand and shrink, and a pinned wire pulls against its staples every day. That is one way staples work out of the post.

The BC guide also notes loose stapling helps less on barbed wire than on smooth, because the barbs catch on staples at some posts anyway. It is still the right way to do it. It just will not make barbed wire act like high-tensile smooth wire, which can slide freely from one end brace to the next.

**Tie-off posts are the exception.** At the end or corner post, the wire wraps around the post and gets stapled tight, because that post is holding the whole run. That is where tension belongs.

### Net wire: never staple the stays

With woven or field fence, staple over the horizontal line wires only. UGA is direct about it: do not staple the vertical stay wires. A staple over a stay pins the fabric so it cannot move, and it does nothing to hold the wire in line. More on the fence itself on our [net wire fence](/net-wire-fence-waco/) page.

## Mistakes that pop staples out

Most failed staples in a Central Texas fence come from a short list:

- **Parallel to the grain.** Splits the post, especially dry cedar in August.
- **Too short for the post.** Short staples in treated pine are the classic pull-out problem both extension guides warn about.
- **Into bark, sapwood or a check.** Looks fine on the day, holds nothing.
- **Stapled to a live tree.** The tree grows around the wire and staple, and you lose that section of fence to the tree.
- **Posts that have moved.** On blackland clay, posts heave and lean with the shrink-swell cycle. When a post leans, its staples twist and loosen. Restapling that post without straightening it just buys a few months.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What size fence staples should I use for barbed wire?
Use 9-gauge, hot-dip galvanized staples 1.75 inches long for most wood posts, and 2 inch for treated pine or large brace posts. Part of each leg goes around the wire before it reaches wood, so short staples leave very little holding the post. The 1.5 inch size is for dense hardwood and small repairs. Barbed-shank staples grip better than smooth ones in any post.

### What angle should fence staples be driven at?
Set the staple diagonally across the wood grain, never lined up with it, and turn it about 25 degrees off square so the slash-cut legs spread as they go in. On posts at the top of a rise, angle the staple downward into the post. On posts in a dip, angle it upward. Steep rises and dips get two staples.

### Should fence staples be driven all the way in?
No, not on line posts. Stop when the crown is just clear of the wire so the strand can slide through the staple. That lets the wire spread loads along the fence and move with heat and cold. The exception is the tie-off post at each end of a run, where the wire is wrapped and stapled tight because that post holds the tension.

### Which side of the post does fence wire go on?
Put the wire on the side of the post facing the livestock, and on the outside of any curve. When animals push on the fence, or the wire pulls around a bend, the wire is pressed into the post instead of pulling against the staples.

### Are barbed staples worth it over smooth staples?
In treated pine, yes. Pine is soft and the preservative lets staples back out over time, and the small barb on each leg grips like a fishhook when anything tries to pull the staple out. In sound cedar heartwood, a plain slash-cut staple set at the right angle often holds fine, but barbed staples do no harm.
