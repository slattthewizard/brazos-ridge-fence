# Fence on Sloped Ground: Bracing Hills, Anchoring Dips and Closing Draw Gaps

Wire wants to run in a straight line between two braces. Hills and dips make it do something else, and that is where fence on sloped ground goes wrong. In a dip, tight wire pulls up and lifts the post. Over a crest, it presses down and the bottom wire digs into the dirt. The fix is the same in both cases: brace at the changes in grade, anchor the low posts down, and don't ask one long stretch to cover ground that goes up and down.

This guide covers ranch wire fence on rolling and broken ground: where to brace, how to hold a post in a swale, net wire on a slope, and the gap that opens under the fence at a draw. ## Why slope changes how a fence loads

On flat ground, tension pulls along the line and the braces at each end take it. On rolling ground, the wire also pushes and pulls across the posts, up or down depending on the shape of the land. Picture a fence running down one hillside and up the next. Stretched wire wants to take the shortest path from one high point to the next, so at the bottom it hangs above the dirt, and every post in the dip is being pulled upward.

Over a crest it flips. The wire presses down on the hill, and the bottom strand drags into the ground or the mesh bunches. Over time you get leaning posts, pulled staples, and a bottom wire that is either buried or floating off the ground.

Central Texas adds its own twist. Blackland clay swells and shrinks, so a post holding by friction in a dip works loose first. West of the Brazos and in Bosque County, thin soil over limestone can keep you from getting depth where you most need it (see [setting posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/)).

## Follow the contour, or go straight over?

You have two choices on a hillside: run straight over, or follow the contour around it.

**Straight over.** Shorter, cheaper, and easier to lay out. It also makes the steepest wire pulls and puts the most load on the braces at the foot and top of the hill. It suits moderate slopes with decent soil.

**Following the contour.** Longer, and every bend is a corner that needs bracing. It keeps wire heights consistent and can keep a fence out of the path of runoff. The University of Georgia's fence guide says contour fences more than 350 feet long should have a braced line post assembly, and that a brace assembly shouldn't go on a curve because it won't hold well. Put the brace in a straight section instead ([UGA, Fences for the Farm](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/)).

More corners means more cost. A rebuilt corner or H brace runs $600 to $1,400 in our repair pricing, so a new brace is a real line item. It still costs less than rebuilding a run that pulled out.

## Brace at the foot and the top of each hill

The most useful single rule for hilly fence: put a braced line assembly where the ground changes grade. The UGA guide says that on rolling land, stretching is easier if braced line post assemblies sit at the foot and top of each hill. The NRCS Texas fence specification puts it a different way: in-line pull assemblies belong in straight sections of the line or where there are sudden changes in elevation, such as the bottom and top of steep slopes ([NRCS Texas fence specification, Code 382, 2015 edition](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf)).

What that buys you is a fence you can stretch one face of a hill at a time. Without a brace at the crest, you are dragging wire over the hump and every post on the way. With braces, each slope is its own pull, and a failure on one slope doesn't unzip the whole line.

For the brace design itself, see our post on [H brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/). One more point from that same NRCS spec: a brace assembly fails when the soil is too weak to support the load, and the brace moves through the soil. A brace at the bottom of a clay swale that stays wet is a brace in weak soil. Set the posts deep, and let the ground settle before you pull wire.

## Posts in dips and low spots

This is the post that fails. In a swale, the wire above it is trying to straighten out, and it will take the post with it if nothing holds it down.

The NRCS Texas spec is blunt about this. It says posts set in low areas or gullies need to be weighted down to prevent lifting out, and it lists weights (rocks or blocks) or setting the post in concrete as ways to meet that. It also says line posts must be set at significant high and low points along the fence to keep spacing and wire height right.

Options for a dip, from lightest to heaviest:

- **A deeper, heavier post.** A wood post set deeper than its neighbors, with tamped backfill, resists pulling better than a t-post. A t-post at the bottom of a swale is the first thing to pull out.
- **A weighted post.** Rock or blocks around the post, as the spec describes.
- **Concrete.** The poured mass resists lifting. It has its own downsides on clay and at the ground line, so weigh it against the options above.

Post spacing should tighten on hills generally. The University of Tennessee's fence guide says line posts move closer together as terrain goes from flat to hilly, with battens or spacers at every dip ([UT Extension PB1541](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf)). The UGA guide agrees that narrow spacings are better over irregular ground. For standard spacings on level ground, see [fence post spacing](/blog/fence-post-spacing/), and plan to go tighter than those numbers on a hillside.

Stapling matters too. The Tennessee guide says to drive staples at an upward angle into posts in dips and at downward angles into posts on rises, and not to drive them in too deep. That matches how the wire is pulling at each spot. (That guide is written around high-tensile wire.)

## Net wire on a slope

Net wire is stiffer than barbed strands because the verticals tie every horizontal together. That is good for holding stock, and a problem when the ground drops away under it.

On a steady slope with no sharp break, net wire follows the grade fine. It is stretched in one pull, fastened to the posts, and the mesh tilts with the hillside. The trouble comes where the slope breaks. At a dip, the stretched wire lifts off the ground and leaves a gap along the bottom. At a crest, the mesh bunches and the bottom digs in.

How to handle it:

- **Brace at the break.** Treat each change of grade as its own stretch, tied off to an in-line brace. Our walk-through of [how to stretch woven wire](/blog/how-to-install-woven-wire-fence/) covers the build order and how dips and rises get handled.
- **Add posts at the low point.** More line posts in the dip hold the wire down to the ground instead of letting it bridge across.
- **Don't expect rigid panels to bend.** Cattle panels, welded wire panels and pipe rail don't flex the way net wire does. On a slope they get set in steps, each section stepped down to follow the grade, which leaves a triangle of open ground under the high end of each step. If livestock can get through that triangle, a barbed strand or a short skirt of net wire below the panel usually closes it.

Net wire runs $3.50 to $8 per foot on the [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page, and rough terrain can push a job toward the top of that range because of the extra posts, braces and hand work. Our [net wire fence](/net-wire-fence-waco/) page covers what goes into one.

## Barbed wire on uneven ground

Barbed wire is more forgiving than mesh because each strand moves on its own and follows a slope more easily. The usual problems are the same dips and crests, plus one more: strand height.

Fence height is measured from the ground at each post, not from a level line. Set the top wire at the same height above the ground at every post, and let the wire follow the ground in smooth runs. Where the ground drops away suddenly between two posts, the wire becomes a long chord above the ground, and animals will go under it. Add a post where the ground falls instead of depending on tension to hold the wire down.

For the base price of a barbed wire build, see the [barbed wire fence](/barbed-wire-fence-waco/) page. The published range is $2.50 to $5.50 per foot for 5-strand barbed wire ($13,200 to $29,000 per mile), and broken ground can push a job toward the upper end.

## The gap under the fence in a draw

A draw, gully or creek bed is where a fence line meets the worst combination: a low spot (so the wire lifts), loose soil (so posts pull), and water (so everything gets pushed). The ground drops away below the bottom wire, and now there is a gap big enough for a calf, a hog or a goat.

Three questions sort it out.

**Does the draw run water?** A draw that only flows after hard rain can still move a lot of water and brush. If it runs, a stretched fence across it acts like a dam and gets taken out. Build it as a water gap that is meant to give way, with solid braces on each bank. Our post on [water gap fences](/blog/water-gap-fence/) covers the designs. The NRCS Texas spec makes a related point: water gaps need end brace assemblies of the same kind as the fence on both sides, and draws need special measures to prevent washout or posts being lifted out of the ground. A rebuilt washed-out water gap runs $700 to $1,800 in our repair pricing.

**Is it a dry low spot that rarely flows?** Then you can hold the wire down. Set a heavy post at the lowest point, weigh it down as described above, and close the opening under the bottom strand with stays or short wires tied between the strands, spaced tight enough for the smallest animal in the pasture.

**What is the ground like under the fence?** Soft fill invites hogs to root under a fence. Rock may mean drilling the hole. Resetting or replacing posts runs $45 to $120 each in our repair range.

Check draws after every big rain.

## Layout checklist before you build

Walk the line before any posts go in. Say you have 40 acres outside Valley Mills with a ridge down the middle and a draw at the back corner. A walk like that should answer the following.

- Mark every change of grade, top and bottom, with a stake or flag.
- Put braced line assemblies at the foot and top of the steeper hills.
- Flag every low spot where wire will lift, and plan a heavy, weighted post there.
- Sort the draws into those that need a water gap and those you can hold down.
- Check for rock before you plan posts.
- Call 811 before you dig.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you build a fence on a steep hill?

Brace at the foot and top of the slope so each face is its own pull, set posts closer together as the ground gets steeper, and anchor posts in any low spots. The fence follows the ground in straight segments, and each change of grade or direction gets a brace.

### How do you keep posts from pulling out of a dip?

Use a heavier, deeper post than the line posts around it, and weigh it down. The NRCS Texas spec lists rocks, blocks or concrete for posts set in low areas and gullies. A t-post at the bottom of a swale pulls out first, so use wood there or add an anchor.

### Does fence on a slope cost more?

Often, yes, because of the extra posts, braces and hand work. The published ranges for barbed wire ($2.50 to $5.50 per foot) and net wire ($3.50 to $8 per foot) apply, and rough ground can push a job toward the top of the range. The [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) page has the full table, and a free on-site estimate prices a broken line properly.

### How do you close the gap under a fence in a draw?

First decide whether the draw runs water. If it does, build a water gap that is meant to give way, with strong braces on each bank. If it is only a low spot, anchor a heavy post at the bottom and close the opening under the bottom strand with stays or short wires.

### Should a fence follow the contour of the land?

Sometimes. A contour fence keeps wire heights consistent and can stay out of runoff paths, but it adds corners, and each one needs a brace. Going straight over is cheaper but makes steeper pulls. UGA says a contour fence more than 350 feet long should have a braced line post assembly.
