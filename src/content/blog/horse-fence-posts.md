For a horse fence, the best posts are pressure-treated wood (8 inch tops on corners and gates, 4 to 6 inch line posts) or steel pipe. Space board and rail fence posts about 8 feet apart, set wood line posts at least 2 feet deep (3 feet or more in blackland clay), and keep bare t-posts out of the horse's reach unless they're capped and sitting behind a hot wire. Everything else below is the reasoning and the exceptions.

Horse fence is harder on posts than cattle fence in one specific way. A cow leans on a fence. A horse leans, rubs, paws, chews, gets a foot hung up and then panics. The post has to hold when the animal is at its worst, and it also can't hurt the animal when it's at its worst. That second part is what changes the post choice.

## Treated wood posts: the default for a reason

Pressure-treated pine is the most common horse fence post in the country. UGA's extension bulletin "Fences for Horses" ([UGA B1192](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/)) lists CCA-treated pine as the usual choice and notes that these posts are harder and longer-lived than the older creosote and penta posts, which also helps nails and staples hold.

### Round or square

Round posts (the kind sold as fence posts) are treated deeper into the wood than a squared timber. Square 4x4 lumber from a building-supply yard is a different product. The UGA bulletin points out that common builder's lumber is treated to a level meant for weather exposure, not for soil contact, and says sawn lumber that touches the ground needs a higher ground-contact treatment. Look at the tag. If it doesn't say ground contact, it's a deck post, and it will rot at the ground line in a few seasons.

For a deeper comparison of wood species, see [cedar vs treated pine posts](/blog/cedar-vs-treated-pine-posts/).

### Sizes that work

UGA's numbers for wood posts, which match what most builders use:

- Corner and gate posts: at least 8 inches across the top.
- Brace posts: 5 inches or more.
- Line posts: as small as 2.5 inches for wire, though larger is stronger and lasts longer.

Those are wire-fence numbers, where the corner and brace assemblies carry the load. The bulletin makes the point that a rail fence is different: every section has to be equally strong, so you use the same post size all the way down the line. For board or rail horse fence, plan on 4 to 6 inch line posts, not the 2.5 inch minimum. A horse that leans on a rail is loading the post sideways at one spot, not spreading the pull down a wire.

### Length

UGA gives the formula: post length equals the height of the top wire or rail above ground, plus the depth you'll set it, plus 6 inches. For a 5-foot horse fence set 2.5 feet deep, that's an 8-foot post. A post that's too short either sits too shallow or comes up short on top.

## Steel pipe posts: the long-life option

Welded pipe is the other common post for horses, usually 2 3/8 inch pipe on line posts, with heavier pipe or more depth at corners and gates. The pipe is the post, so there's nothing to rot. On horse fence it's usually paired with pipe rails or used as the post for a no-climb mesh with a pipe top rail.

Pipe posts have two horse-specific rules:

- Cap every open end. An open pipe top fills with water, rusts from the inside, and gives a flying hoof or a shoulder a hard edge to catch. A welded flat cap or a plug takes a minute.
- Grind or round the welds and any burr at the rail ends. Horses find the one sharp spot on a fence.

## T-posts: where they belong near horses

A steel t-post is cheap, light and drives fast, and UGA notes that steel posts are fireproof and help ground the fence against lightning. For a cattle fence they're a fair line post. For horses they come with two problems.

First, the tops. A bare t-post top is a narrow steel edge, and a horse that goes over a fence or falls against it can be hurt on it. If you use t-posts anywhere a horse can reach, put a cap or a safety top on every one. Caps are a cheap general-retail item and they need to be checked each year, because heat and UV make plastic brittle and they crack off.

Second, they bend. UGA, and the University of Tennessee's [PB1541 fence bulletin](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf), both describe the same workaround: set a wooden line post every 50 to 75 feet to keep the steel posts in line. UGA also says to drive t-posts until the anchor plate is just below the surface.

The honest position on t-posts for horses:

- Fine as the inside posts behind a hot wire or an offset tape, where the horse doesn't touch them.
- Fine for temporary cross-fencing with step-in posts and polytape.
- Not what we'd choose as the visible, touchable post on a perimeter horse fence, even capped.

## Spacing by fence type

Closer posts mean a stiffer fence and fewer places a horse can get a leg through a sagging span. The table below is the range a builder would typically set for each type. The 8-foot board number matches the University of Tennessee bulletin's table of recommended spacings.

| Fence type | Typical post spacing |
|---|---|
| Board or rail (wood) | 8 ft |
| Pipe fence, 3 or 4 rail | 8 to 10 ft |
| No-climb 2x4 mesh with top rail | 8 to 10 ft |
| High-tensile wire (UGA) | about 25 ft |

Treat those as starting points. Tighten them on a curve, on a slope, and anywhere a gate or a feeder draws horses to a spot. [Fence post spacing for barbed wire, net wire and pipe](/blog/fence-post-spacing/) goes through the wire-fence numbers if you're building cattle fence on the same place.

## How deep to set horse fence posts

UGA's number for wooden line posts is at least 2 feet deep, with corner and brace assemblies deeper than that. It also says to go deeper in sand or wet soil. That's a minimum, not a target.

Around Waco, here's how the ground changes it:

- **Blackland clay.** The soil swells wet and shrinks dry, and posts heave and lean. Setting a line post 3 feet deep gets below the worst of the movement. Corners and gates should go deeper than line posts. [Setting fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/) covers this.
- **Shallow limestone and caliche** (west of the Brazos and through Bosque County). You may hit rock at 12 to 18 inches. That means drilling a hole, not driving a post, and then setting the post in the drilled hole. See [setting fence posts in rocky ground](/blog/fence-posts-in-rocky-ground/).
- **Sand along the creek bottoms.** Go deeper, as UGA says, and use a longer post.

### Concrete or tamped soil

UGA says concrete around a wood post isn't recommended because it shortens the post's life, and says to tamp soil back in layers instead, paying special attention to the bottom 6 inches. The Tennessee bulletin adds that driven posts are 1.7 times as strong as tamped posts. For pipe, a concrete collar is normal and often the right call.

### Corners and gates take the load

On a wire or mesh horse fence, the corner and gate posts do the real work. UGA says the pull on a corner can reach about 3,000 pounds when the wire is first stretched, and about 4,500 pounds in cold weather. Each line running into a corner needs its own brace assembly. That's why corners get the 8-inch post and an H-brace. A heavy gate on a light post sags within a year.

## Which side of the post do the rails or mesh go on?

For board and rail horse fence, the usual practice is to put the rails on the horse side, with the posts outside the paddock. When a horse leans on or runs into the fence, the load pushes the rail against the post instead of pulling the nails or screws out of it. It also keeps the posts, the part that can't flex, out of reach of a horse that's rubbing or running the fence.

The trade-off is that a rail on the inside face is where a pawing or chewing horse gets hold of it, so the rail needs to be smooth, flush and fastened with the right hardware, with no nail or bolt ends showing. On no-climb mesh, the same logic applies: the mesh goes on the horse side and is clipped or stapled to the posts.

That lines up with UGA's guidance for wire, which puts staples and clips on the side nearest the livestock.

On the top end, UGA puts the minimum height for a horse perimeter fence at 5 feet, which is what sets the post length in the formula above.

## Quick post checklist for a horse fence

- Ground-contact treated wood, round, with a tag you can read.
- 8-inch tops at corners and gates, 5-inch or larger braces, 4 to 6 inch line posts for rails.
- Posts 8 feet on center for board, pipe and rail.
- 3 feet deep for line posts in clay, deeper at corners and gates, drilled where there's rock.
- Rails or mesh on the horse side.
- Every pipe top capped and every t-post top capped or kept behind a hot wire.
- No bolt ends, nails or wire tails sticking out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best post for a horse fence?
Pressure-treated round wood posts rated for ground contact are the standard choice, with 8 inch tops at corners and gates and 4 to 6 inch line posts. Steel pipe is the long-life alternative and is common with pipe rails. T-posts work only behind a hot wire or with every top capped.

### How far apart should horse fence posts be?
For board, rail and pipe fence, about 8 feet apart is typical, and the University of Tennessee bulletin lists 8 feet for board fence. Wire-type horse fence can run wider, but tighten the spacing on curves, slopes and near gates. High-tensile lines can run much farther between posts, with stays.

### How deep should horse fence posts be set?
UGA says at least 2 feet for wooden line posts, deeper for corners and braces and deeper still in sand or wet soil. In Central Texas blackland clay, 3 feet for line posts is a more useful target because of the shrink and swell. Where there's rock, the post goes in a drilled hole.

### Can I use t-posts for a horse fence?
You can, with limits. Bare tops are a hazard to a horse that falls or goes over the fence, so cap every one. Steel posts also bend under a leaning horse, so set a wood post every 50 to 75 feet, as UGA suggests. They suit electric offset lines and temporary cross-fences better than a perimeter fence.

### Should the posts be inside or outside the rails?
On a board or rail horse fence, the rails usually go on the horse side with the posts outside, so a push from the horse presses the rail into the post instead of pulling the fasteners out. Keep the rail face smooth and flush, and don't leave any nail or bolt ends exposed.

### Is concrete needed around horse fence posts?
Not for wood. UGA advises against concrete around wood posts because it shortens their life, and recommends tamping soil in layers instead. For steel pipe posts, a concrete collar is normal and works well, particularly at corners and gates. In clay, depth matters more than the width of the hole.
