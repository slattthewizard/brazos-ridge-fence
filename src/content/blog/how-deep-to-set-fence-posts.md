Wood line posts go at least 2.5 feet deep. Corner, end and gate posts go deeper, at least 3 feet and more like 3.5 to 4 feet for a wire fence under real tension. T-posts are driven until the anchor plate is a couple of inches under the ground. Those are the baseline numbers. Then the dirt gets a vote, and around Waco it votes hard.

The old "one-third of the post in the ground" rule is a fair starting point for a line post. It's a bad rule for a corner, and it ignores that blackland clay moves and limestone doesn't. This guide gives depth by post role, shows where the rule of thumb breaks, and says what to do when the hole stops at rock. ## Why depth matters more than post size

A fence post fails one of three ways. It pulls out, it leans, or it snaps at the ground line. Burial depth is what fights the first two. A tight wire fence pulls on its corners with a lot of force, and the only thing holding a corner against that pull is the soil on the buried part of the post and the brace.

A post that's too short works fine until you stretch wire. Then it tips toward the pull, the brace goes slack, and the run loses tension.

Depth is cheap while the hole is open. Fixing it later means pulling the post, re-digging and re-tamping, and a lot of ranch fence repair is exactly that. Resetting or replacing posts runs $45 to $120 per post, and rebuilding a corner or H-brace runs $600 to $1,400. See [ranch fence cost](/ranch-fence-cost/) for the full table.

## Quick depth table by post role

These numbers come from the 2021 NRCS Texas fence standard (Code 382) where it gives one, and from the UGA Extension fence bulletins, ["Fences for Horses"](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/B1192/) and ["Fences for the Farm"](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/C774/fences-for-the-farm/). Pipe and brace figures come from the NRCS Texas fence spec, and the [2015 edition](https://leon.agrilife.org/files/2022/05/382_Fence_Specification-Revised_2015.pdf) is the one that can still be linked. Where a number is our field advice for Central Texas soil, the notes say so.

| Post | Minimum depth | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wood line post (cedar, treated pine) | 2.5 ft | 2021 NRCS Texas standard. Our advice in clay: 3 ft. |
| Steel T-post line post | Anchor plate 2 in below ground | 2021 NRCS Texas standard. Rock: see below. |
| Steel pipe line post (2 3/8 in.) | 2 ft set, 5 ft if driven | From the NRCS Texas spec text. |
| Corner, end and pull posts (wood) | 3 ft minimum, 3.5 to 4 ft better | UGA shows 3.5 ft for an H-brace and 4 ft for a dead-man brace. |
| Gate posts | Same as corner or deeper | Gate posts carry a swinging load. Treat them as corners. |
| Pipe corner or gate post (2 3/8 in.) | 3 ft set, 5 ft if driven | Bigger pipe can be driven shallower. |

The sections below explain each row.

## Line posts: 2.5 feet is the floor

A line post's job is to hold the wire at height and spacing. It doesn't carry the pull of the fence. The corners and braces do that. So line posts can be lighter and shallower, which is why this is where people save money.

The 2021 NRCS Texas fence standard sets wood line posts 2.5 feet deep. The earlier edition said 24 inches, so the newer number is a half foot deeper. UGA's "Fences for the Farm" says wood line posts should be set at least 2 feet deep, "preferably deeper."

Here's the length math. UGA's horse fence bulletin gives a simple formula: height of the top wire, plus depth in the ground, plus about 6 inches extra. For a 4-foot barbed wire fence with posts set 2.5 feet deep, that's 4 + 2.5 + 0.5, or 7 feet. A 6-foot post, which is common, is short for that job. Buy 7-footers, or accept a shallower hole and a weaker post.

### The one-third rule, and where it breaks

Burying one-third of a post is the rule most people quote. On a 7-foot post that works out to about 2.3 feet, which is a hair under the NRCS number. On a taller post, it asks for more depth, which is right. On a 6-foot post, it gives 2 feet, which is too shallow for a fence that's 4 feet tall above the ground.

The rule breaks in three places:

- **Corners and gates.** They need depth set by the pull, not by the post length.
- **Soft or wet ground.** UGA notes that settings need to go deeper in sand or wet soil. Blackland clay in a wet spring behaves the same way.
- **Rock.** You can't bury one-third of a post in a hole that stops at 14 inches. The fix isn't a shorter hole. It's a different method (more on that below).

## Steel T-posts: drive to the plate

The 2021 NRCS Texas standard says T-posts are driven until the anchor plate is 2 inches below ground. Plain and simple, the post is in far enough when the plate is buried. UGA says the same thing in plainer words: drive until the anchor plate is beneath the surface.

For a standard 6.5-foot T-post that usually leaves roughly 18 to 24 inches in the ground and a little under 5 feet above it, which suits a 4-wire cattle fence. Weight matters too, and the NRCS spec prefers 1.33 pounds per foot over the 1.25 minimum. We cover sizes, clips and caps in [T-post sizes explained](/blog/t-post-sizes-and-clips/).

One caution: the anchor plate only helps when the dirt around it is firm. In loose sand or wet bottomland, T-posts pull out, so drive them a few inches deeper or add a wood post every few hundred feet.

## Corner, end and pull posts: go deeper than you think

This is where the real depth goes. The NRCS Texas spec (2015 edition) says brace assembly posts are buried a minimum of 3 feet, unless they're driven. UGA's "Fences for Horses" shows H-brace posts at 3 feet 6 inches, and says a dead-man brace end post should go at least 4 feet down. The same bulletin says to go deeper still in sand or wet soil.

A corner doesn't just hold itself. It holds the fence on both sides, and the brace turns that pull into compression on the horizontal rail. If the post pulls out of the ground or rotates, the brace is only decoration. For brace types and how to pick, see [H brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/).

Diameter matters as much as depth. UGA calls for corner and gate posts at least 8 inches across at the top. For pipe, the NRCS Texas spec's minimum for corner and gate assemblies is 2 3/8 inch outside diameter.

### Driven pipe corners

The NRCS Texas spec gives driven depths by pipe size. A 2 3/8 inch pipe is driven 5 feet. Pipe from 2 7/8 up to 4 1/2 inches is driven 4 feet. Pipe 5 inches and larger is driven 3 feet. Bigger pipe has more surface in the soil, so it needs less depth. Driving also packs the soil instead of loosening it, which is why a driven post is stronger than a tamped one. UT Extension's [Planning and Building Fences on the Farm](https://utbeef.tennessee.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/127/2020/11/PB1541.pdf) puts a driven wood post at about 1.7 times as strong as a tamped one.

## Gate posts: treat them like corners

A gate post is a corner that moves. It carries the gate's weight, it gets slammed, and cattle lean on it. The NRCS spec requires an end or gate brace assembly on both sides of a gate opening. So the answer to "how deep should a gate post be" is the same as a corner post: 3 feet at the very least, and 3.5 to 4 feet for a wood post that's holding a 12 or 16 foot gate.

The mistake to avoid is setting gate posts like line posts because they're "just a post." Sagging gates are almost always a leaning gate post. If you're building from scratch, make the hinge post the biggest post on the property. ## What blackland clay changes

East of the Brazos and through much of McLennan and Falls counties, the surface is blackland clay. It swells when wet and shrinks and cracks when dry. Posts set shallow in it move with the seasons. They heave up a little each wet cycle and lean when the dry crack opens beside them.

Practical changes in clay:

- Our field advice: add about 6 inches to the line post depth, so 3 feet instead of 2.5.
- Set corners and gates at 4 feet or more.
- Don't flare the top of a concrete footing at the surface. The NRCS Texas spec allows concrete up to 12 inches below the surface on blackland, with soil on top. That keeps the clay from gripping a collar and lifting it.
- Tamp in lifts of 4 to 6 inches. The NRCS spec calls for a hole at least 6 inches wider than the post, backfilled and tamped in 4 to 6 inch lifts.

The full story is in [setting fence posts in clay soil](/blog/fence-posts-in-clay-soil/), and the concrete question is in [setting fence posts in concrete](/blog/setting-fence-posts-in-concrete/).

## What shallow limestone changes

West of the Brazos, in China Spring, Bosque County and the hills toward Clifton and Meridian, a lot of the ground is a few inches of soil over limestone or caliche. You can't set to full depth with a post hole digger, and you shouldn't pretend a 14-inch hole is a post.

The NRCS Texas spec has a shorter-post allowance for rocky soil, but that's for line posts, not corners. Say you have 20 acres outside Valley Mills with a limestone shelf along the back line. The line posts can be shorter T-posts driven as far as they'll go. The corner can't. It needs a drilled hole, either with a rock auger or a hydraulic drill, set in concrete or grout so the rock does the holding. Rock is the one case where concrete is the right call at the full depth.

## Adjusting for the job

Depth isn't only about the post. These things push it up or down:

- **Fence height and load.** An 8-foot game fence puts far more leverage on every post than a 4-foot cattle fence. Plan on setting both line and corner posts deeper than the cattle numbers above.
- **Soil moisture.** Creek bottoms and low spots stay soft. The NRCS Texas spec says posts in low areas and gullies need to be weighted down, with rocks, blocks or concrete, so they don't lift.
- **Wire tension.** A loose fence puts less on the corners than a tight woven wire or high-tensile fence. The tighter the wire, the deeper the corner.

## Getting it right the first time

Mark your digging tool or driver at the target depth before you start. UT Extension suggests exactly that, marking the digging tool or a steel post for uniform depth. Check the first few holes with a tape. A hole you guessed at 30 inches is often 22.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep should a fence post be in the ground?

A wood line post should be at least 2.5 feet deep under the 2021 NRCS Texas fence standard, and 3 feet in blackland clay is better. Corner, end and gate posts go at least 3 feet, with 3.5 to 4 feet for a tight wire fence. T-posts are driven until the anchor plate is about 2 inches underground.

### How deep should a gate post be?

Treat a gate post like a corner post. Set it at least 3 feet deep, and 3.5 to 4 feet if it's wood and holding a 12 to 16 foot gate. UGA recommends a top diameter of at least 8 inches for corner and gate posts. The NRCS spec calls for a brace assembly on both sides of any gate opening.

### Is the one-third rule right for fence posts?

It's a decent rule for a line post, and it breaks elsewhere. A corner needs depth set by the pull of the wire, not by the post's length. Rock stops you from reaching it at all, and clay needs more than one-third. A better formula from UGA is: top wire height, plus depth in the ground, plus about 6 inches.

### How deep do you drive a T-post?

Drive it until the anchor plate is about 2 inches below the ground, per the 2021 NRCS Texas standard. On a 6.5-foot post that leaves roughly 18 to 24 inches in the soil. In rocky ground you can use a shorter post set shallower, but expect it to be weaker, and use wood or pipe for any corner. See [T-post sizes explained](/blog/t-post-sizes-and-clips/).

### Do I need concrete to set fence posts?

Not for line posts in most ground. Corners and gates in soft soil can use concrete, but on blackland the NRCS Texas spec stops the concrete 12 inches below the surface with soil on top.
