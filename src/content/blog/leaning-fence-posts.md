## Why fence posts start leaning in the first place

Every leaning post comes down to one of five causes. Sometimes two of them are working together.

**Wire tension.** A fence is a constant tug of war. Four or five strands of barbed wire pulling at 150 to 250 pounds each puts real load on every post, and corner posts carry the whole line. If a corner wasn't braced right on day one, it starts tipping toward the pull within a few years. Our post on H-brace corners covers why a single fat post, no matter how deep, can't hold a quarter mile of tight wire by itself.

**Rot at the ground line.** Wood posts don't rot at the bottom. They rot in the top 6 inches of soil, where there's enough moisture and enough oxygen for fungus to work. The post looks fine above ground and solid below, but that ring at ground level turns to punk. Wind or a cow leaning on the wire does the rest. If your post wiggles like a loose tooth, read our piece on fence post rot before you spend a dime straightening it.

**Shallow setting.** The old rule is a third of the post in the ground. A 6.5 foot line post needs about 2 feet of burial, and a corner post needs 3 to 3.5 feet. Plenty of fences around here were built with 18 inch holes because the digging got hard, and those posts never had a chance.

**Livestock and wildlife.** A 1,300 pound cow scratching on a post is a battering ram on a schedule. Feral hogs rooting along the fence line undermine posts from below. Horses crib on top rails and push posts sideways over time.

**Soil movement.** This one deserves its own section, because in Central Texas it's the big one.

## Central Texas clay is the main reason for leaning fence posts

Most of McLennan and Falls counties sit on Blackland Prairie clay. That soil shrinks when it dries and swells when it soaks, and the movement is strong enough to crack house slabs, so a fence post doesn't stand a chance. In a dry August the clay pulls away from the post and leaves a gap. The first big rain fills that gap with water, the clay swells, and the post gets squeezed and shoved, usually not in the direction you'd like.

Drought years are the worst. The 2022 and 2023 dry spells opened cracks in pastures around Lorena wide enough to drop a fence pliers into. Posts that had stood straight for 15 years leaned that winter, because the soil that held them simply wasn't in the same place anymore. [Texas A&M AgriLife Extension](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/) has good background on how these Vertisol clays behave if you want the science.

Kenny Braddock, who runs about 60 pairs outside Crawford, called us in March after a wet winter followed that kind of [drought](/blog/drought-fence-problems/). Both corner assemblies on his south line had heaved and tipped, and 14 line posts between them were leaning past 15 degrees. The wire was still attached, which meant the whole quarter mile was slowly going slack. We rebuilt the two corners as proper H-braces set 3.5 feet deep, reset the 14 line posts, and re-tensioned five strands. The bill was $1,850. His neighbor had quoted him "just pushing them back up" for $400, and that fix would've lasted one season.

## How to straighten a leaning fence post yourself

Some leaning posts are a fair DIY job. Here's how we'd tell you to do it, in order.

**Step 1: Find the cause first.** Grab the post at chest height and push it back and forth. If it pivots at ground level and feels spongy, it's rotted and straightening is wasted effort. If the whole post moves like it's in loose sand, the soil failed and you can re-tamp. If it barely moves but leans toward the wire pull, your brace is the problem, not the post.

**Step 2: Take the tension off.** Never fight a post against tight wire. Un-staple the strands from the leaning post and the two posts on either side, or loosen your inline strainers a few clicks. This alone lets some posts come back near plumb.

**Step 3: Excavate the high side.** Dig a wedge of soil out of the side the post is leaning away from, down 12 to 18 inches. You're making room for the post to swing back without compressing soil against it.

**Step 4: Pull it plumb.** A fence post level that straps onto the post is a $12 tool worth owning. For stubborn posts, a ratchet strap to the truck's receiver hitch works, but go slow. Pull a rotted post too hard and it snaps at ground level, and now you've got a stub to dig out.

**Step 5: Tamp in lifts.** This is where most DIY fixes fail. Backfill 3 or 4 inches of soil at a time and tamp each lift hard with a digging bar before adding more. Dumping the whole pile in and stepping on it leaves air pockets that turn to mush in the first rain. Done right, tamped native soil holds a line post as well as concrete in most cases. In heavy clay, mixing in crushed limestone or road base gives water somewhere to go and cuts down on future heaving.

**Step 6: Re-tension the wire.** Bring the strands back up to tension evenly, working from the middle strand out. If the wire itself has gone slack and stretchy, it may need splicing and re-stretching too.

For steel t-posts, the fix is usually simpler. Straighten the post with a t-post jack or pull it entirely, then re-drive it a foot over in undisturbed soil. A bent t-post is a $6 part. Don't spend an hour saving one.

**Know when to stop.** If the post is a corner or gate post, if it's set in a failed concrete footing, or if more than a handful of posts in a row are leaning, put the shovel down. Those jobs involve serious wire tension and heavy pulling, and a snapped brace wire or a post that lets go under load can hurt you. That's a call, not a project. ## When a leaning fence post can't be saved

About a third of the leaning posts we look at aren't worth straightening. Here's how to tell.

**Rot you can see or feel.** Poke the post at ground level with a screwdriver. If it sinks in more than half an inch, the post is done. Replace it.

**Concrete footing moving as one piece.** If the post and its concrete plug rock together, the footing has broken loose from the soil. You'll need to pull the whole plug, which usually means a tractor, and reset from scratch.

**Corner and gate posts.** These carry too much load to gamble on. A failed corner takes the whole line slack, and slack wire is how cattle end up on the county road. If that's already happening on your place, check every corner before you blame the wire.

Rosa Delgado keeps 11 acres with goats and two horses off Highway 317 near Valley Mills. Her gate post had leaned far enough that the gate dragged and wouldn't latch, and she'd been lifting it into place for a month. The post looked fine, but it was a cedar post set 20 inches deep in sand with no concrete and no brace. We replaced it with a 4 inch drill stem post set 4 feet deep in concrete and hung the gate fresh. Total was $685, and that gate will still swing true when her grandkids own the place. Pipe posts cost more up front, but for gates and corners they end the leaning problem permanently. ## How to keep posts from leaning again

Straightening a post is maintenance. Keeping it straight is design. A few habits make the difference.

Set posts deep enough the first time. A third of total length in the ground, minimum, and 3.5 to 4 feet for corners and gates. The USDA [Natural Resources Conservation Service](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/) fencing standards that ag programs use call for braced corner assemblies on any stretched wire fence, and there's a reason cost-share money won't pay for a fence without them.

Build real H-braces at every corner, gate, and direction change, and add brace assemblies every quarter mile on long straight runs. Tension belongs on braces, not on line posts.

Walk your fence twice a year, spring and fall, and after every big storm. Catching a 5 degree lean costs you ten minutes with a tamping bar. Catching it at 30 degrees costs a Saturday or a service call. Keep the fence line clear of brush too, since roots and falling limbs start more leans than most people think.

And match the post to the soil. In our clay, that means road base or crushed rock in the backfill, or pipe posts at the pressure points. In sand, it means concrete and extra depth.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I set a leaning fence post back in concrete?

Usually not for line posts. Well-tamped native soil, or soil mixed with crushed rock, holds a line post fine and drains better than concrete in our clay. Concrete earns its keep on gate posts, corner posts, and posts in loose sandy soil. If you do use concrete, crown it above grade so water sheds away instead of pooling around the post.

### How much lean is too much to ignore?

Anything past about 10 degrees, or any lean on a corner or gate post, needs attention now. A leaning corner is already letting your whole fence line go slack, and slack wire leads to escaped livestock. A slight lean on one line post in the middle of a run can wait for your next fence-walking day, but put it on the list.

### Why do my fence posts lean after every heavy rain?

That's Central Texas clay doing what it does. The soil shrinks and cracks in dry weather, then swells hard when it soaks, and each cycle shoves posts a little further off plumb. Backfilling with road base or crushed limestone, setting posts deeper, and using pipe at high-load points all help. Shallow-set posts in straight clay will keep leaning no matter how many times you push them back.

### Can I just push a leaning post upright and pack the dirt?

You can, and it'll look fixed for a few weeks. Without digging out the high side, tamping in shallow lifts, and dealing with whatever caused the lean, the post goes right back over with the next rain or the next cow that scratches on it. Ten extra minutes of doing it right saves redoing the job every season.
