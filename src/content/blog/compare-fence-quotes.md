Three quotes land on your kitchen table for the same mile of pasture fence: $9,800, $14,500, and $21,000. Same land, same cattle, same request. So why is one bid more than double another, and which contractor is telling you the truth?

If you own land around Waco, Crawford, or Valley Mills, you'll face this exact situation sooner or later. Knowing how to compare fence quotes is the difference between a fence that holds cattle for 30 years and a fence you're rebuilding in three. The good news is that fence quotes aren't complicated once you know what to look for. The bad news is that the worst contractors count on you not knowing.

This guide walks you through comparing fence bids line by line, the numbers that actually matter, and the red flags that should send you looking for somebody else.

## Why Quotes for the Same Fence Can Be Thousands Apart

A fence quote isn't one number. It's a stack of decisions about posts, wire, spacing, bracing, and labor, and every one of those decisions moves the price.

Take a plain 5-strand barbed wire fence. One contractor bids it with treated wood posts every 12 feet, class 3 galvanized wire, and proper H-braces at every corner. Another bids t-posts every 20 feet, class 1 wire, and a single wood post where the braces should be. Both quotes say "barbed wire fence, one mile." The first fence will outlive your truck. The second one will sag inside five years, and you'll be out fixing it every time a cow leans on it.

Terrain matters too. Rocky ground off Highway 6, heavy clay bottoms near the Brazos, creek crossings that need water gaps, brush that needs clearing before a single post goes in. A contractor who's actually walked your line prices that work. A contractor bidding off a satellite photo doesn't, and the difference shows up later as a change order.

So a big spread between bids doesn't automatically mean somebody's cheating you. It usually means the bids aren't describing the same fence. Your job is to make them describe the same fence, and that starts with the paperwork. ## How to Compare Fence Quotes Line by Line

Never compare bottom-line totals. Compare line items. If a quote doesn't have line items, that's your first red flag, and we'll get to those shortly.

Here's what every serious ranch fence quote should spell out:

**Total footage.** Measured on the ground or with a wheel, not eyeballed. If one bid says 5,100 feet and another says 5,400 feet for the same line, somebody didn't measure. On a $3.50 per foot fence, that 300-foot gap is more than a thousand dollars of confusion.

**Price per foot.** This is your true comparison number. Divide the total by the footage if the contractor didn't do it for you. Around Central Texas, barbed wire typically runs about $2 to $4.50 per foot installed, net wire more, and [pipe fence](/pipe-fence-waco/) considerably more. We break down full numbers in our [barbed wire fence cost guide](/blog/barbed-wire-fence-cost/).

**Post type, size, and spacing.** "Wood posts" means nothing. You want species and treatment (treated pine or cedar), diameter, set depth, and spacing between posts. Tighter spacing costs more and holds better. So does concrete on corners and gates.

**Wire specification.** Gauge, class of galvanizing, number of strands, and brand if possible. Class 3 galvanized wire costs more up front and lasts roughly twice as long as class 1 in our weather.

**Corners and braces.** Corner and end bracing is where cheap fences fail first. The quote should count H-brace assemblies and say how they're built. If braces aren't mentioned at all, assume they'll be done wrong. Our post on [H-brace fence corners](/blog/h-brace-fence-corners/) shows what right looks like.

**Gates, clearing, tear-out, and haul-off.** Are gates included or extra? Who clears the brush on the fence line? Who removes the old fence, and where does it go? Every one of these can be a surprise bill if the quote is silent on it.

**Timeline, payment terms, and warranty.** When they start, when they finish, what you pay and when, and what happens if a post heaves or wire sags in the first year.

Once every bid answers those questions, you can finally compare apples to apples. You'll often find the "expensive" bid and the "cheap" bid are pricing two very different fences.

## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some warning signs mean "ask more questions." These mean "walk away."

**A big cash deposit up front.** A third down is normal on larger jobs so the contractor can buy materials. Half or more up front, especially in cash, is how landowners lose money. Levi Brandt, who runs cattle west of West, learned that in 2024. A door-knocker quoted him $7,200 for a quarter mile of net wire, took a $3,500 cash deposit "for materials," and never came back. No contract, no address, no recourse. The Federal Trade Commission lists this exact pattern in its guidance on [hiring a contractor](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/hiring-contractor).

**No written contract.** If it's not on paper, it doesn't exist. Footage, materials, price, timeline, payment schedule, warranty. All of it, signed.

**No proof of insurance.** Ask for a certificate of general liability insurance, and verify it's current. If a worker gets hurt on your place or a skid steer takes out your water line, an uninsured contractor's problem becomes your problem.

**A bid that's dramatically below the others.** If two bids are around $14,000 and one is $8,500, the cheap one isn't a bargain. It's missing something: post depth, wire class, braces, insurance, or the intention to finish the job.

**Pressure to sign today.** "This price is only good until I leave your driveway" is a sales tactic, not a business practice. Fence prices don't expire overnight.

**They won't walk the line.** Nobody can price rocky ground, creek crossings, or brush from the road. A contractor who bids without walking your fence line is planning to make up the difference later.

**Vague answers about who does the work.** Some outfits sell the job and sub it out to whoever's available. That's not automatically bad, but you should know who's showing up and who stands behind the work.

## Make the Bids Match, Then Pick the Contractor

Once you've got itemized quotes, force them onto the same spec so the comparison is fair.

Call each contractor and say: "Bid me 5-strand, 12.5 gauge class 3 barbed wire, treated posts on 12-foot centers set 3 feet deep, H-braces at all corners and gates, old fence torn out and hauled off." Now every number describes the same fence. County extension programs, like Texas A&M AgriLife Extension's fencing workshops, teach this same spec-first approach, and the University of Georgia Extension publishes a solid free reference on fence construction basics in [Fences for the Farm](https://extension.uga.edu/publications/detail.html?number=B1192) if you want to study up on materials before those calls.

With matching specs, pick on these four things, in order:

1. **Completeness of the paperwork.** Contract, insurance, warranty, payment schedule.
2. **Quality of the answers.** A pro explains why they space posts the way they do. A pretender gets annoyed that you asked.
3. **Local track record.** Ask for two or three fences they built nearby that you can drive past. Around here, work from three years ago is visible from the road.
4. **Price.** Last, not first. Between two qualified contractors, sure, take the better number.

And know when the decision isn't about quotes at all. If a fence is down and cattle are on the road tonight, you don't need three bids, you need a crew. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many fence quotes should I get?

Three is the sweet spot for most ranch fencing jobs. One quote gives you no context, and five starts wasting everybody's time, including yours. Make sure all three are bidding the exact same spec, footage, and scope, or the comparison is worthless.

### Is the cheapest fence quote always a bad sign?

Not always, but a quote far below the others usually means something is missing: lighter materials, wider post spacing, no braces, no insurance, or no plan to finish. If the low bidder can show you an itemized spec that matches the others and local fences you can go look at, the price may be honest. If they can't explain the gap, believe the other two bids.

### How much deposit is normal for a fence job in Texas?

Expect somewhere between 10% and about a third down, mostly to cover materials, with the balance due when the work is done and you've walked the line. Be very cautious of anyone wanting half or more up front, and never pay a large deposit in cash without a signed contract and a physical business address.

### What should a written fence quote include?

At minimum: measured footage, price per foot, post type and spacing, wire gauge and galvanizing class, brace and gate counts, who handles clearing and old fence removal, a start window, a payment schedule, and a workmanship warranty. It should also come with proof of liability insurance. If a contractor can't put those things on paper, keep looking.
