Here's something that surprises almost every new landowner we meet: in most of Texas, the law doesn't require you to fence your cattle in. It expects your neighbor to fence them out.

That's the heart of Texas open range law, a rule that goes back to the trail-drive days and still shapes who pays when a bull walks through a hayfield or a heifer ends up on a farm-to-market road at midnight. But the rule has big exceptions. County stock laws, highway statutes, and plain old neighbor disputes can flip the whole thing on its head.

We build fence for a living in McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties, and we've seen what happens when folks guess wrong about these rules. It usually costs real money. So let's walk through what open range actually means, where stock laws change it, and what kind of fence keeps you out of trouble either way.

Quick note before we start: we're fence builders, not lawyers. This article explains the general rules so you can ask better questions. For a legal opinion on your land, talk to an attorney.

## Texas Open Range Law Starts With "Fence Out," Not "Fence In"

Texas inherited its livestock rules from a time when cattle grazed free across unfenced prairie. The courts settled it more than a century ago: under the common law of Texas, livestock owners have no general duty to prevent their animals from roaming.

That's called being an open range or "fence out" state. If you don't want your neighbor's cows eating your coastal bermuda, the default rule says it's on you to build a fence that keeps them out. If a cow wanders onto unfenced land and grazes it down, the cow's owner usually owes nothing, because the animal wasn't trespassing in the legal sense.

Compare that to "fence in" states, where the animal's owner must contain their stock and is on the hook for damage when they don't. Most of the country works that way now. Texas kept the old rule as its starting point, then let counties opt out of it piece by piece.

That opt-out system is where stock laws come in, and it's why the honest answer to "is Texas open range?" is always the same: it depends on where your land sits and sometimes on which animal we're talking about.

## How County Stock Laws Close the Range

Starting in the late 1800s, the Legislature let local voters hold elections to adopt "stock laws." A stock law makes it unlawful to let certain animals run at large in that county or precinct. Where a stock law passed, the range is "closed" and the rule flips to fence in.

The details live in Chapter 143 of the Texas Agriculture Code, which you can read yourself on the [Texas Legislature's statutes site](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/AG/htm/AG.143.htm). A few things worth knowing about how these laws work:

- Stock laws are adopted by local election, sometimes countywide, sometimes for a single precinct or an area described by old survey lines.
- They're animal specific. An election might cover cattle and horses but not sheep or goats, or hogs but nothing else.
- Many of these elections happened between the 1890s and 1930s. The records sit in county clerk archives, and some are handwritten.

That last point trips people up constantly. There's no clean statewide map of which counties are closed range. Most of the settled parts of Central Texas voted in stock laws for cattle generations ago, but the only way to know for sure what applies to your pasture is to ask the county clerk or sheriff's office, or have a title company or attorney pull the records.

Here's the practical takeaway. If a valid stock law covers your land and your animals, you have a legal duty to keep them off other people's property and off the roads. Your fence stops being a courtesy and becomes your liability shield.

## The Highway Rule Catches Everybody, Open Range or Not

Even in a true open range area, there's one exception with teeth. Texas Agriculture Code Section 143.102 makes it illegal to knowingly permit cattle, horses, mules, donkeys, sheep, goats, or hogs to roam at large on the right-of-way of a U.S. or state highway.

That means the fence along a highway frontage matters everywhere in Texas, stock law or not. If your bull has been getting out onto Highway 6 for weeks and you knew about it, "we're open range out here" is not going to save you. The word "knowingly" does a lot of work in these cases, which is exactly why courts look at the condition of your fence, how often the animal escaped before, and what you did about it.

County roads are a different story, and farm-to-market roads have their own wrinkles that lawyers argue about. The Texas State Law Library keeps a plain-language guide to these rules in its [animal law research guide](https://guides.sll.texas.gov/animal-law), and it's worth twenty minutes of your time.

The pattern we see in real wrecks is simple: a loose cow on pavement at night is a disaster for everyone, including the cow. Which brings us to what this looks like when it goes wrong.

## Open Range vs. Closed Range: What Changes for You

Here's the side-by-side, keeping in mind that highway frontage rules apply in both columns.

| Question | Open range area | Closed range (stock law) area |
|---|---|---|
| Must I fence my livestock in? | No general duty, except along U.S. and state highways | Yes, letting them run at large is unlawful |
| Neighbor's cattle eat my crops | Usually my problem if my land wasn't fenced | Usually the cattle owner's problem |

## How to Find Out What Applies to Your Land

Don't guess. Here's the checklist we give buyers and new landowners:

1. Call the county clerk and ask whether stock law elections cover your precinct, and for which animals. In McLennan, Bosque, and Falls counties, expect to do some digging in old records.
2. Ask the sheriff's office how they handle loose livestock calls in your area. Their answer tells you a lot about how the county treats the question in practice.
3. Note every foot of U.S. or state highway frontage on the property. That fence has to hold no matter what the county records say.
4. Walk the fences before you close on land, and budget honestly for what you find. Old wire hides a lot of sins.
5. When real money or a real dispute is on the table, spend a few hundred dollars on an hour with an ag law attorney. It's the cheapest thing on this list.

Then look at your fences the way an insurance adjuster would. Would they hold a motivated bull tonight? If the answer is "probably," that's a no.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is all of Texas open range?

No. Texas is open range by default, but most populated counties and precincts adopted local stock laws long ago that close the range for some or all animals. The only reliable way to know your land's status is to check stock law records with your county clerk or an attorney, because there's no official statewide map.

### If my county is open range, can my cattle legally roam anywhere?

Not quite. Even in open range areas, state law makes it illegal to knowingly let livestock roam on the right-of-way of a U.S. or state highway. Repeated escapes and a known bad fence are exactly the kind of evidence used to prove "knowingly," so a sound fence protects you even where no stock law applies.

### Who pays if my neighbor's cattle damage my property?

In a closed range area, the cattle owner is generally responsible for damage their loose animals cause. In a true open range area, you usually can't recover for grazing damage unless your land was fenced and the animals broke in. Either way, documenting the damage with photos and dates helps, and a solid perimeter fence prevents the fight entirely.

### What kind of fence do I need to meet the law?

There's no single statewide spec for every situation, but the practical standard for cattle in Central Texas is a tight five-strand barbed wire fence on braced corners, or net wire if you also run goats or sheep. Plan on $2.50 to $4.50 per foot for barbed wire and more for net wire or pipe. Put your best fence on highway frontage, where the legal duty is strictest.
